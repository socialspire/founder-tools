# Precedent Bank: The One Message Sheet

Twenty cards, every one traced to a named file in
`socialspire/skool/insights/`. They hold the two rulings this skill makes: how
many things a founder is saying and which single one survives, and who the
surviving message is actually written for.

**Why a bank and not a deck.** A deck of principles about clarity and focus is
knowledge the model already holds, so it adds nothing a strong model would not
reach alone. Twenty specific verified cases, most of them ones no model
volunteers, are information. Read the index, route to the sections the member's
situation needs, and read only those. Do not read the whole file on every run.

**Attribution rule.** Every card is paraphrase. Not one phrase is quoted here
and none may be quoted downstream. Cards drawn from a book or an article name
the work and its author. Cards marked PODCAST name the episode — the show, the
date, and either the founder who spoke or the biography the episode works from.
Neither kind ever goes inside quotation marks, because the card text is this
bank's rendering, not anyone's words. If a member asks for a quotable line, the
bank holds none.

# INDEX

Route from the situation the member arrives in. Read every section whose row
matches, plus section G always.

| Section | Read it when | Cards |
|---|---|---|
| **A. You are saying several different things** | The member has a stack of published assets and needs to know how many claims they actually add up to | A1 to A4 |
| **B. You have candidates and have to rule one** | Two or more lines are live, or the member wants to know whether the current line was ever chosen at all | B1 to B4 |
| **C. The targeting is a demographic** | The who-this-is-for line names an age, a job title, a company-size band or a country | C1 to C4 |
| **D. You cannot say what your buyers believe** | The member cannot name a belief shared by the people who have already paid | D1 to D3 |
| **E. You cannot rule it from where you sit** | Two clusters both carry evidence, or the member is arguing about what cutting will cost instead of measuring it | E1 to E2 |
| **F. The message is ruled and now has to hold** | An adjacent claim is being added, or the member wants to talk about channels | F1 to F3 |

Then read **G. What is not on record** before writing the output block. It is a
required line.

# A. You are saying several different things

**A1. Steve Jobs and Lee Clow's paper balls.** Situation: an agency chief
trying to make a founder feel, rather than merely accept, why an ad carries one
claim. Did: crumpled a single sheet of paper and threw it across the room,
which Jobs caught, then threw five sheets at once. Happened: Jobs caught none of
the five, and one message per ad became the standing Apple rule. Then vs now: a
landing page has far more room than a magazine page ever did, which is why the
demonstration matters more now, not less — publishing capacity went up and the
reader's catching hand did not. Move: read your hero section aloud to one
person, once, and count how many distinct claims they can hand back to you.
That count is your real message count, not the one on the page.
Source: *Insanely Simple: The Obsession That Drives Apple's Success*, Ken
Segall.
`founders/2024-07-02-how-steve-jobs-kept-things-simple.md`

**A2. Todd Graves and the two seconds.** PODCAST. Situation: a one-item
restaurant chain under permanent pressure to widen the menu. Did: priced every
proposed addition in time rather than cash — two seconds of order time is worth
roughly a point of sales, and at about $6B in sales a point is about $60M.
Happened: new items and limited-time offers are refused outright, each one
carrying training modules, new service mechanics and fresh sales expectations on
a sixty-day cycle. Then vs now: a homepage has no drive-thru timer, so the
dollar figure does not transfer, but the accounting does. An added claim is
never neutral. It is paid for out of the reader's seconds. Move: before adding a
claim, write down what the reader has to skip past in order to reach the
surviving one, and decide whether that trade is worth making.
Source: Todd Graves, on the David Senra podcast, 2025-11-09.
`david-senra/2025-11-09-how-todd-graves-built-raising-canes.md`

**A3. James Dyson and the Sea Truck buyers.** Situation: a boat sold as
adaptable, aimed at divers, oil crews and the military at the same time. Did:
pitched the one all-purpose craft to all three groups. Happened: it converted
nobody. Selling a purpose-built diving boat to a diver worked. Then vs now: this
is the identical failure to a homepage claiming to serve three buyer types at
once, except his correction cost a product cycle and yours costs an afternoon of
rewriting. Move: take your broadest asset and rewrite it as though only one of
your three audiences will ever read it, then see which of the other two actually
complains.
Source: *Against the Odds: An Autobiography*, James Dyson.
`founders/2024-03-17-james-dyson-against-the-odds.md`

**A4. James Dyson and the capability he buried.** Situation: a bagless vacuum
entering a market that had never seen one, which also happened to dry-clean
clothes. Did: never mentioned the second capability anywhere in the selling, on
the reasoning that a buyer can barely absorb one genuinely new idea. Happened:
the single claim survived intact instead of being undercut by a true but
competing one. Then vs now: nothing has made buyers more absorbent since, and
the number of claims a reader meets in a day has only gone up. Move: find the
true benefit you are proudest of that is not your surviving message, and delete
it from the hero this week. The test is whether it is true, not whether it fits.
Source: *Against the Odds* and *Invention: A Life of Learning Through Failure*,
James Dyson.
`founders/2025-09-11-the-stubborn-genius-of-james-dyson.md`

# B. You have candidates and have to rule one

**B1. Dietrich Mateschitz and the fifty rejected campaigns.** PODCAST.
Situation: a broke founder with an unlaunched drink and an agency generating
positioning lines for it. Did: rejected roughly fifty proposals over eighteen
months, and covered the bill by going to work inside the agency as a freelancer
because he had no money to pay it. Happened: the line about the drink giving you
wings arrived by phone in the middle of the night, and both men recognised it
instantly. Then vs now: he then had five years in one small market to settle the
message before opening a second country. A founder today gets feedback in weeks,
which removes the excuse for keeping a line he does not believe. Move: ask
whether your current hero line was chosen or merely arrived first. If it arrived
first, file it as a draft rather than as the survivor.
Source: Founders (David Senra), 2024-04-20, on Wolfgang Fürweger's
German-language Mateschitz biography.
`founders/2024-04-20-red-bulls-billionaire-maniac-founder-dietrich-mateschitz.md`

**B2. DHH and the word he refused to assert.** PODCAST. Situation: a
project-management tool whose founders knew every competitor also claimed to be
easy to use, which made the claim carry no information from their own mouth.
Did: stopped asserting it and instead surveyed customers on the single reason
they stayed. Happened: simplicity relative to competitors came back as the
number one reason, which converted a boast into a message with evidence
attached. Then vs now: the mechanism is cheap to rerun — five buyers and one
question — and the claim has only got more crowded since, so the test is more
necessary. Move: ask your last ten customers the one question of why they have
not left, and rule your surviving message on the words that come back rather
than the words you like.
Source: David Heinemeier Hansson, on the David Senra podcast, 2026-07-26.
`david-senra/2026-07-26-why-constraints-lead-to-better-companies-products-dhh-37signals.md`

**B3. Lulu Cheng Meservey and the founder with seven minutes.** PODCAST.
Situation: a founder who took six of his seven minutes to describe a marketplace
connecting home cooks with people tired of delivery apps. Did: she handed him a
single compression — that the product turns a cook into a chef — and he used it
from then on. Happened: the same move turned another founder's
data-ingestion-and-inefficiency pitch into a weight-loss-drug analogy for
companies. Her claim is that the listener supplies the rest, so you get
complexity and simplicity at once. Then vs now: she refuses to hand anyone a
template and says so explicitly, so there is no formula here, only the practice
of naming the one existing thing you are the equivalent of. Move: say your pitch
to a sharp outsider, ask them to give it back in one sentence, and use their
sentence rather than yours.
Source: Lulu Cheng Meservey, on the David Senra podcast, 2026-08-12.
`david-senra/2026-08-12-the-comms-strategist-behind-anduril-shopify-cognition-lulu-cheng-meservey.md`

**B4. Larry Ellison and the product that would not sell.** Situation: an Oracle
product going nowhere, called Network Computing Architecture. Did: renamed it
Internet Computing Architecture and changed nothing else about it. Happened:
sales took off. The difference was the name. Then vs now: the lesson is that the
claim, not the product, was the binding constraint. Still true, though a rename
today is checked against search demand rather than a salesforce's reaction.
Move: before rebuilding anything that is not selling, check whether it has ever
once been described in the words the buyer already uses for the problem.
Source: *Softwar: An Intimate Portrait of Larry Ellison and Oracle*, Matthew
Symonds.
`founders/2025-11-04-how-larry-ellison-thinks.md`

# C. The targeting is a demographic

**C1. Steve Stoute and the industry's three boxes.** PODCAST. Situation: an ad
industry that divided the audience into black, white and Hispanic and bought
media against those boxes. Did: went to the purchase data instead of the
personas — DMX selling in Iowa where radio would not play him, Eminem being
bought in Harlem. Happened: the data contradicted every box the industry used.
He built his agency on shared culture instead, on the reading that an
eighteen-year-old in Compton and one in Greenwich who both skate are the same
reader. Then vs now: the boxes have been renamed but ad platforms still sell
them, which is exactly why the purchase record has to be read against the
targeting rather than alongside it. Move: pull your actual buyer list and find
the single place where it contradicts the audience you are currently paying to
reach.
Source: Steve Stoute, on the David Senra podcast, 2026-06-21.
`david-senra/2026-06-21-the-marketing-genius-of-steve-stoute.md`

**C2. Steve Stoute and the customers nobody addressed.** PODCAST. Situation: a
category whose customers were buying products whose entire presentation excluded
them, which the industry took for granted rather than competing for. Did: named
it as a mispriced market rather than a curiosity, and built the practice of
marketing deliberately to the group that was already buying. Happened: brands
that connected authentically to that culture took share the incumbents had
assumed was permanently theirs. Then vs now: the specific mispricing he found
has long been arbitraged away. The diagnostic survives — anyone buying despite
your marketing is a segment you have not chosen. Move: list the buyers who
bought even though nothing in your marketing was aimed at them, and build the
reader line from those names rather than from the ones you meant to reach.
Source: Steve Stoute, on the David Senra podcast, 2026-06-21.
`david-senra/2026-06-21-the-marketing-genius-of-steve-stoute.md`

**C3. Joseph Pulitzer and the two words he deleted.** Situation: a newly bought
newspaper whose own name carried a city inside it. Did: removed New York from
the New York World the moment he took ownership. Happened: the name stopped
capping how large the readership was allowed to become. Then vs now: this is the
same move as striking a geography from targeting that no buyer belief supports,
except a masthead change was permanent and public while a targeting setting
takes a minute to reverse. Move: read your who-this-is-for line and strike every
place name, company-size band and job title that does not follow from something
your buyers actually believe.
Source: *Pulitzer: A Life in Politics, Print, and Power*, James McGrath Morris.
`founders/2026-06-20-joseph-pulitzer-the-inventor-of-mass-media.md`

**C4. Ingvar Kamprad and the half of the market he refused.** Situation: a
furniture industry that pointed its best design at a wealthy minority. Did:
wrote the purpose as well-designed furniture priced so the majority could afford
it, and treated the choice as one never to be revisited. Happened: that single
sentence became the instrument that killed every later proposal drifting
upmarket, without a debate each time. Then vs now: he wrote his operating
principles once and refused ever to amend them, a harder commitment than most
founders will make. The usable half is having one sentence with the authority to
kill a proposal. Move: write the sentence naming the buyer you are permanently
choosing, then use it this week to kill one piece of targeting that contradicts
it.
Source: *Leading By Design: The IKEA Story*, Ingvar Kamprad and Bertil Torekull.
`founders/2024-11-25-the-founder-of-ikea-ingvar-kamprad.md`

# D. You cannot say what your buyers believe

**D1. Herb Kelleher and the buyers who would otherwise drive.** Situation: an
airline being priced against other airlines on a route where rivals charged $62.
Did: worked out that the customer's real alternative was a car or a bus rather
than another carrier, and priced at $15. Happened: the fare opened a market that
was invisible at the incumbent's price. Then vs now: the substitute today is
usually a spreadsheet, a junior hire or doing nothing at all, and it is still
absent from every competitor set founders draw. Move: for each of your five
buyers write what they would have done if you did not exist, and check whether
your message answers that rather than answering a rival.
Source: *Nuts!: Southwest Airlines' Crazy Recipe for Business and Personal
Success*, Kevin and Jackie Freiberg.
`founders/2024-04-09-herb-kelleher-founder-of-southwest-airlines.md`

**D2. Gaston Glock and the room that wrote his spec.** Situation: a manufacturer
with no experience of the product he intended to build and no view at all on
what the buyer wanted. Did: convened the experts and buyers, including the man
who ran procurement, asked them outright what a product of the future should do,
and had every guest sign and date the sheet recording their answers. Happened:
he left with the requirements he then had to beat rivals on, and later sold more
than twice his entire first-year break-even volume on the opening day of a
single trade show. Then vs now: he sold to institutions who will attend a
meeting. A founder selling to individuals cannot convene his buyers and has to
collect the same information one call at a time. Move: get five real buyers to
define what good looks like in their own words, and date the sheet so you can
tell later whether the belief moved or you did.
Source: *Glock: The Rise of America's Gun*, Paul Barrett.
`founders/2024-03-26-the-biography-of-the-founder-of-glock.md`

**D3. Lulu Cheng Meservey and the hundred listeners.** PODCAST. Situation:
founders who need a specific and very small set of people to believe them, and
default to going wide instead. Did: works backwards from the named people whose
opinion matters. Her example of the error is an AI lab that needs five
particular machine-learning engineers, knows exactly who they are, and goes on
national morning television, set against Brian Armstrong appearing on a podcast
with roughly a hundred listeners because that is where his audience actually
was. Happened: the small precise room did the work the large one could not, and
she argues those people are usually one message away. Then vs now: she is
describing recruiting and belief-building rather than a purchase, so applying it
to a buying audience is an inference rather than a proven transfer. Move: write
down the names of the people who must believe you, find where they already get
their information, and delete every targeting line that does not point at them.
Source: Lulu Cheng Meservey, on the David Senra podcast, 2026-08-12.
`david-senra/2026-08-12-the-comms-strategist-behind-anduril-shopify-cognition-lulu-cheng-meservey.md`

# E. You cannot rule it from where you sit

**E1. Todd Graves and the veto vote.** PODCAST. Situation: an entire industry
predicting that a single-item restaurant would lose the whole car whenever one
passenger wanted something else. Did: shipped the narrow offer anyway and
watched repeat-visit rate rather than arguing about variety. Happened: his visit
frequency now matches any quick-service competitor, because people return for
the same thing rather than for choice. Then vs now: this is the cleanest
counter-evidence in the library to the fear that narrowing to one message loses
the undecided reader. Food has an obvious repeat cycle that a service business
may not, so treat it as a reason to measure rather than proof you are safe.
Move: when you cut four claims to one, name in advance the number you expect to
fall, then go and look at it in thirty days instead of relitigating the
decision.
Source: Todd Graves, on the David Senra podcast, 2025-11-09.
`david-senra/2025-11-09-how-todd-graves-built-raising-canes.md`

**E2. Herb Kelleher and the empty repositioning flight.** Situation: a plane
being flown empty to reposition, and no evidence at all about who else might
fly. Did: sold those seats at a third of the normal fare rather than reasoning
about who the passenger might be. Happened: it filled instantly, revealing a
price-sensitive leisure market invisible at business-traveller prices. Two-tier
peak and off-peak pricing followed and the whole industry copied it. Then vs
now: he had spare capacity that cost nothing to test with. A founder's
equivalent is one asset rewritten to the second belief and run for a fixed
window, which is cheap but not free. Move: when two clusters both carry
evidence, refuse to rule between them and instead run the cheapest live test
that would separate them, then rule after.
Source: *Nuts!: Southwest Airlines' Crazy Recipe for Business and Personal
Success*, Kevin and Jackie Freiberg.
`founders/2024-04-09-herb-kelleher-founder-of-southwest-airlines.md`

# F. The message is ruled and now has to hold

**F1. Larry Gagosian and Bernard Arnault's chair.** Situation: an art dealer
operating in a market that trades on discretion, needing buyers to know
instantly what he was. Did: reduced his entire posture to one legible message,
that everything in his world is for sale, and applied it without exception —
when Arnault admired a chair in his own townhouse he was told he could buy the
chair. Happened: it made him absurdly easy to transact with and he built the
largest dealership in the business. Then vs now: he sells scarce objects to a
handful of buyers, which is not most founders' situation. What carries is that
one message applied everywhere removes the reader's question about what you
actually do. Move: write the single sentence a stranger could act on
immediately, then go find the place on your own site where you contradict it.
Source: *How Larry Gagosian Reshaped the Art World*, Patrick Radden Keefe, New
Yorker.
`founders/2024-04-12-billionaire-art-dealer-larry-gagosian.md`

**F2. Dietrich Mateschitz and the licensing offers.** PODCAST. Situation: a
single-product brand, flooded with offers to put its name on gummy bears,
underwear and perfume, after an early lemonade line had already failed. Did:
refused every one of them on a stated rule that all marketing exists to sell
more of the one product. Happened: the brand, its athletes and its events stayed
one campaign with many manifestations instead of several programmes competing
for the same attention. Then vs now: he was defending a shelf position with
physical inventory behind it. The modern equivalent is defending one sentence,
which is cheaper to hold and much easier to lose quietly. Move: list the
adjacent claims you have been invited to make, put them under Stop, and for each
one name the surviving message it was competing against.
Source: Founders (David Senra), 2025-11-25, on Wolfgang Fürweger's Mateschitz
biography.
`founders/2025-11-25-red-bulls-billionaire-maniac-founder.md`

**F3. Joseph Pulitzer and the 1,028 competing newspapers.** Situation: a market
of 1,028 newspapers in which every publisher had identical access to the medium.
Did: treated what the paper said as the whole competition and never the format,
and when he wanted an issue to stick he ran it as many as 112 consecutive times.
Happened: he built the most-read paper in the world, with a standing newsroom
bar of publishing one thing a day worth repeating at dinner. Then vs now: the
number of available channels has multiplied since, which makes the channel
argument more tempting rather than less. The message question still has to be
settled first. Move: settle what you are saying before you spend anything on
where you say it, and refuse to let the channel question be raised in the same
meeting.
Source: *Pulitzer: A Life in Politics, Print, and Power*, James McGrath Morris.
`founders/2026-06-20-joseph-pulitzer-the-inventor-of-mass-media.md`

# G. What is not on record

Every one of these was searched for and not found. State the relevant one in
every run. It is a required output line, because a corpus that has been searched
can say what it does not contain, and nothing reasoning from memory can.

**How to state it.** As an absence, never as a verdict. Holding no case of a
services business ruling a single message does not mean a services business
cannot do it. It means nothing is on record either way, so the member should
price the attempt as untested.

**The words the member reads.** Open with the plain statement that there is no
documented case of their own situation, name that situation in their words, then
name the closest case and how it differs. The member never reads the words library, bank, card or corpus of
this file, and never a flourish about being the first. An absence is information, not a warning against the ruling.

1. **No founder in the library ever counted his own published claims.** Every
   case here is a founder who already knew his one message, or who rejected
   drafts before publishing. Nobody audits ninety days of live assets and
   tallies what they add up to, which is the actual job of this procedure. There
   is no precedent for the count itself, only for the ruling that follows it.
2. **No case in the library attaches a number to an individual claim.** The
   evidence in the corpus is total sales, survival, or press coverage attached
   to a whole campaign or a whole product. Ogilvy's finding that one ad can
   outsell another by as much as nineteen to one is the closest, and it compares
   finished ads, not the claims inside them. Nobody reports signups or replies
   per message.
   `founders/2024-03-24-david-ogilvy-confessions-of-an-advertising-man.md`
3. **No services, agency or consulting business in the library rules a single
   message.** Every one-message case in the library is a physical product, a
   media property, a restaurant, or software. A founder selling a done-for-you
   service is at the edge of what this bank supports.
4. **No case of a founder choosing between two messages that both carried
   evidence.** Kelleher's discounted repositioning flight is the nearest thing
   to a deliberate test, and it was a capacity experiment that revealed a
   segment, not a contest between two claims. The tie-break refusal rests on no
   precedent at all.
5. **No reader line was ever built from a founder's own last five buyers.**
   Stoute reasons from category-wide purchase data, Glock from a convened room
   of institutional buyers, Lulu Cheng Meservey from named people she wants to
   reach. Nobody in the library works from the five people who most recently
   paid them.
6. **No B2B buyer's belief is on record.** Every belief-based segmentation case
   in the corpus is a consumer one — music, skateboarding, energy drinks,
   cosmetics. Nothing covers a belief held by someone spending a company's
   money.
7. **No threshold for how many claims is too many across a whole body of
   marketing.** Within a single piece the library is firm, and says one message
   per ad, always. Across ninety days of assets it gives no number. Jobs
   advertising four or five of a dozen innovations in a year is the nearest, and
   it counts what he chose to advertise, not claims made per asset. Any count
   the skill treats as a limit across a body of work is the skill's own invention.
   `founders/2024-03-14-steve-jobs-make-something-wonderful.md`
8. **No record of a founder narrowing to one message and being wrong about which
   one.** Every narrowing case in the library is a survivor telling the story
   afterwards. The failure mode is entirely undocumented.
9. **No timing on how long a message takes to show a number.** Ogilvy ran the
   same campaign for twenty-one and thirty-one years, Mateschitz spent five
   years settling his in one market. Nothing says how long after rewriting a
   hero line a founder should expect to be able to rule on it.
   `founders/2024-03-24-david-ogilvy-confessions-of-an-advertising-man.md`
   `founders/2024-04-20-red-bulls-billionaire-maniac-founder-dietrich-mateschitz.md`
10. **Nobody records what a founder gave up by refusing the second message.**
    Mateschitz turned down licensing offers and Graves refuses menu items;
    neither the forgone revenue nor the customers lost is on record anywhere.
    The cost side of the one-message rule is missing from every case.
11. **No case where the founder's own preference was tested against the evidence
    and lost — or won.** Jobs choosing a colour over a forty-one-shade test is
    the nearest, and that is design, not messaging. The library holds no contest
    between what the founder liked and what the numbers said.
    `founders/2024-01-16-working-with-steve-jobs.md`
12. **No method for finding a belief-based segment inside an advertising
    platform's targeting menu.** Stoute names the principle and every case
    predates or sidesteps the platforms. The skill can rule the targeting out;
    it has nothing on record about what to put in its place operationally.
13. **No case of a pre-launch founder with fewer than eight published assets.**
    Every founder here already had something in market. There is no precedent
    for what to do when the count cannot be run at all.
