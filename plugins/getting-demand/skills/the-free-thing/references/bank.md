# Precedent Bank: The Free Thing

Twenty-one cards on giving something away and getting paid for it, every one
traced to a named file in `socialspire/skool/insights/`. The bank covers the
three things this skill has to do: swap paid spend for product in people's
hands, design a free asset that only pays off through more usage, and close the
exit points where a prospect leaves with nothing.

**Why a bank and not a deck.** A deck of principles about free offers is
knowledge the model already holds, so it adds nothing a strong model would not
reach alone. A bank of twenty-one specific, verified cases — most of them ones
no model volunteers — is information. Read the index, route to the sections the
member's situation needs, and read only those. Do not read the whole file on
every run.

**Attribution rule.** Every card is paraphrase, including the ones marked
PODCAST. Not one phrase is quoted and none may be quoted downstream. Book cards
name the book and author. PODCAST cards name the speaker, the interviewer and
the date. Neither kind ever goes inside quotation marks, because the card text
is this bank's rendering, not the founder's words. If a member asks for a
quotable line, the bank holds none.

# A. The ad budget could buy units instead

**A1. Gaston Glock and the American police agencies.** Situation: an unknown
Austrian plastics manufacturer with a new pistol, no reputation in the American
market, and buyers who would not risk a purchase order on him. Did: shipped free
samples to every agency that asked — by the thousand within a single year — each
one carrying an invitation to simply mail a check at a steep discount if they
wanted to keep it rather than send it back. He had already built a cost
structure where a modest first-year unit volume broke even, so he knew what each
unit cost him before a single one left the building. Happened: nine agencies in
ten sent the check. He also sold more than twice his entire first-year target on
the opening day of one trade show. The library's separate note on the same play
is that free units belong in the hands of the buyers your real market watches;
professional buyers set the taste of the far larger civilian market. Then vs
now: the mechanism is a giveaway with a recoverable unit and a known marginal
cost, aimed at the buyer your market imitates. Both halves still transfer. What
does not is the era's tolerance for shipping unsolicited hardware; you now need
the agency to ask first. Move: instead of discounting, ship the real product
with an invoice that only becomes due if they keep it, and set that discount at
what you would otherwise have spent acquiring them.
Source: *Glock: The Rise of America's Gun*, Paul Barrett.
`founders/2024-03-26-the-biography-of-the-founder-of-glock.md`

**A2. Les Schwab and the woman with the flat tire.** Situation: an independent
tire dealer against national manufacturers who gave their own stores better
pricing, with an advertising budget he could have spent conventionally. Did:
advertised that his crew would drive out to a stranger's house and fix her flat
for nothing, no purchase required. His stated reasoning was that if you fix
every flat in town you get all the tire business in town. Happened: it became
his cheapest acquisition channel and the company reached roughly $1.8 billion in
eventual sales. He never hired a manager from outside in 34 years, so the free
service was delivered by people who had done the job themselves. Then vs now:
this works only because the free service puts you physically in front of the
buyer at the exact moment the paid purchase becomes obvious. Strip out that
adjacency and the same free service is charity. Note the tension with the loop
test: a free flat repair does pay off for someone who never buys a tire, and it
worked anyway because the timing was right. Move: name the free service that
puts you in the room at the moment the paid need appears, cost one instance of
delivering it, and advertise that service instead of advertising the product.
Source: *Les Schwab: Pride In Performance — Keep It Going!*, Les Schwab.
`founders/2024-04-17-les-schwab-charlie-munger-recommended-this-book.md`

**A3. David Baszucki and the first 1,500 Roblox users.** PODCAST. Situation: a
four-person, self-funded company with a live product, no organic discovery, and
no budget to speak of. Did: on another founder's advice, bought roughly 50 users
a day from Google at about a dollar each, for about a month, and then stopped
rather than scaling the channel. Happened: Baszucki believes the entire Roblox
social graph traces back to something like 1,500 users seeded by that one month
of ads. Then vs now: a dollar a user is long gone and the file offers no modern
equivalent, so do not carry the price across. What transfers exactly is the
sizing decision — a trivial fixed budget bought a first cohort, not a growth
channel, and the spend was capped before it was proven. Move: name the smallest
cohort your loop needs to start turning, price it at your real cost per unit,
buy exactly that many, then stop and watch whether it propagates before spending
again.
Source: David Baszucki with David Senra, 2026-04-26.
`david-senra/2026-04-26-robloxs-david-baszucki-built-the-biggest-playground-on-earth.md`

**A4. Jimmy Iovine and the people the customer copies.** PODCAST. Situation:
launching Beats headphones into a category where incumbents already owned retail
shelf space and advertising, and where the products looked like medical
equipment. Did: put the product into Interscope's music videos from the start
rather than buying advertising, and funded the placement himself by putting more
of his own money into the videos. Happened: by the time Apple bought the
company, Beats was number one in fifty countries, ahead of Sennheiser in Germany
and Sony in Japan. Then vs now: he owned the videos, which is the half most
founders do not have. The transferable half is the choice to place free units
where the culture is made rather than where ad inventory is sold — the same play
Glock ran with police agencies in A1. Move: list the ten people your buyer
imitates, work out what one free unit costs you to deliver, and spend the next
increment of ad budget putting units on those ten instead.
Source: Jimmy Iovine with David Senra, 2026-02-01.
`david-senra/2026-02-01-jimmy-iovine-building-interscope-records-beats-by-dre.md`

# B. Nobody has priced the free thing

**B1. Soichiro Honda and the dealers who never paid.** Situation: a product
obsessive pushing machines out to a dealer network to build volume, with nobody
costing what was going out the door. Did: Honda handed dealers machines on
credit and then never collected on them. The fix was not a collections effort —
Fujisawa rebuilt the distribution network and required dealers to pay up front.
Happened: the library's flat verdict is that there is no Honda without Fujisawa.
The structural change to payment terms is what solved it, not chasing invoices.
Then vs now: the failure mode is timeless. A giveaway nobody priced, handed to a
channel under no obligation, gets reinvented by every generation of founders.
The remedy on record is structural, not behavioural. Move: before you give
anything away, write down what one unit costs you and who is on the hook when it
is not paid for. If the answer to either is nobody, you have a leak, not a
campaign.
Source: *Honda: The Man and His Machines*, Sol Sanders.
`founders/2026-06-28-the-practical-genius-of-honda.md`

**B2. Jerry Jones and the press box on the fifty-yard line.** Situation: bought
an NFL franchise for $140 million that was losing $9 million a year with over 90
percent of its luxury suites empty. Did: audited what the business was already
giving away. The press box sat on the fifty-yard line and was handed to
newspaper writers at no charge; he moved the writers to the five-yard line and
sold the best seats in the building. He also inventoried the stadium's unsold
surfaces and created advertising where there had been none. Happened: the prior
owner had sold six of 188 suites. Jones found that suite revenue, unlike ticket
revenue, was not shared with other owners, and poured his selling effort there.
Then vs now: the arithmetic holds in any business with finite inventory. The
test is whether the free recipient generates demand for you or merely consumes
the asset — the writers were consuming the best seats and generating coverage
that would have arrived from worse seats. Move: list everything you currently
hand over free, price each line at what a buyer would pay for it, and move every
recipient who generates no demand to a cheaper seat.
Source: *King of the Cowboys: The Life and Times of Jerry Jones*, Jim Dent.
`founders/2025-03-03-the-life-story-of-jerry-jones-owner-of-the-dallas-cowboys.md`

**B3. Jensen Huang and the university computer science departments.** Situation:
a graphics chip company that had invented something the market did not yet know
how to use. Did: seeded universities with hardware in exchange for them teaching
courses on it, gave hundreds of talks, hosted developer summits, and wrote the
textbook itself. He then attended the summits personally to hear how researchers
were actually using it. Happened: making the GPUs CUDA-compatible took four
years and dropped gross margin from 45 percent to 35 percent while the stock
fell over 80 percent. The loop it built is that more CUDA users create more GPU
demand, which creates more CUDA users; the defence is the loop, not a wall. Then
vs now: this is the library's best-priced free asset. The cost was named, it was
a margin hit rather than a cash line, and it was held through a bad year on
purpose. The magnitude is a public-company decision; the discipline of writing
the price down first is not. Move: write down what your free asset costs to keep
alive for a year, who does that work, and what you will hold through — before
you announce it.
Source: *The Nvidia Way: Jensen Huang and the Making of a Tech Giant*, Tae Kim.
`founders/2025-01-14-how-jensen-thinks.md`

**B4. Sol Price and the membership fee.** Situation: a retailer deciding where
in the transaction the profit should sit. Did: bought as cheaply as possible,
marked goods up only enough to cover costs — around 12 percent against
competitors' 36 to 50 — and took the profit on membership fees instead. He
lifted the membership device from Korvette, which in 1948 had used memberships
purely to dodge fair-trade price floors. Happened: forty years on, membership
fees are the profit engine of a company worth hundreds of billions. Price framed
the near-zero markup not as generosity but as an alignment machine that put his
margin structure on the customer's side of the table. Then vs now: the structure
transfers wherever you can move margin off the thing being consumed and onto the
right to consume it. It needs volume, and it needs a customer who buys often
enough for the access to be worth paying for. Move: before you give something
away, ask whether it could instead be sold at cost with the profit moved onto
the access — and check whether your buyer returns often enough for that to work.
Source: *Sol Price: Retail Revolutionary and Social Innovator*, Robert Price.
`founders/2024-03-22-sol-price-the-founder-who-taught-jim-sinegal-sam-walton-jeff-bezos-and-bernie-ma.md`

# C. Prospects leave with nothing

**C1. Estée Lauder and the hat and dress counters.** Situation: a cosmetics
counter inside a department store, with salespeople on other counters who
touched the same customer before she ever did. Did: befriended the hat and dress
salespeople in every store and gave them free product, so they would recommend a
matching lipstick or a free makeover at her counter. Separately she gave samples
to every beauty editor in every town she visited, and refused to leave a town
without seeing all of them. Happened: the file's own thesis is that persistence
and free samples were the entire growth engine of what became a $16 billion
company built out of one counter in a beauty salon. Then vs now: the
adjacent-staff handover needs a physically shared customer. In a business
without one, the analogue is whoever your prospect speaks to immediately before
you — an accountant, a supplier, a platform support rep — and that person still
has to be handed both the product and the sentence. Move: name the person your
prospect talks to right before you, hand them a free unit and the exact line to
say, and make it something they can pass on without asking anyone's permission.
Source: *Estée: A Success Story*, Estée Lauder.
`founders/2024-09-04-estee-lauder.md`

**C2. Estée Lauder and the barefoot woman in San Antonio.** Situation: a store
salesperson telling her not to waste time on a customer who looked like she
could not pay — an exit point the staff had already written off before it
happened. Did: did the full makeover anyway, free, on the prospect everyone else
had filtered out. Happened: the woman bought two of everything, and her
relatives came back the next day. Then vs now: the mechanism is that the
filtering happens before the giveaway and quietly deletes the exit points you
would have converted. That is unchanged. What has changed is that the filter is
now often a form field or a qualification score rather than a person's
judgement, which makes it harder to notice you are doing it. Move: find the exit
point your team writes off on appearance or fit, hand those prospects the same
free thing everyone else gets, and count what comes back.
Source: *Estée: A Success Story*, Estée Lauder.
`founders/2024-09-04-estee-lauder.md`

**C3. Michael Ovitz and the deal nobody asked him for.** Situation: an agent
with no claim on a target client and no standing to ask for one. Did: called it
poaching by assumption — behaved as though he already represented the person,
delivered a completed deal, and let the signature become a formality. Earlier,
he made himself a senior executive's unpaid after-hours assistant, sorting memos
by priority, stocking his fridge, gathering stock tips, having first worked out
who actually held decision rights rather than who was famous. Happened: he built
the most powerful agency in Hollywood on it, and the firm's founding rules kept
every client shared across the whole partnership. Then vs now: the unrequested
finished deliverable still lands, and it needs no approval from anyone to hand
over. Ovitz's own caveat in the same file is that CAA's rise coincided with a
commercial peak, before fragmented attention, so expect the finished piece to
have to be better now than it was then. Move: take your single highest-value
stalled prospect and hand them one finished piece of the actual work,
unrequested, with no ask attached.
Source: *Who Is Michael Ovitz?*, Michael Ovitz.
`founders/2025-03-21-the-rise-and-fall-and-rise-of-the-most-powerful-man-in-hollywood-michael-ovitz.md`

**C4. Gaston Glock and the trainer who came to the customer.** Situation: rival
gun makers required buyers to travel to an academy at company headquarters
before they could be trained on the product. Did: bundled a trainer into every
procurement deal and sent trainers out to the customer instead. The library's
stated precedent is Ogilvy selling stoves door to door, where offering free
cooking lessons with the purchase transformed conversion. Happened: the visits
pulled in curious neighbouring agencies and turned into regional open-house
seminars, so the free training became a sales channel in its own right. Then vs
now: the test this case supplies is that the free attachment travelled to the
customer and generated new prospects while it was being delivered. Apply that to
any onboarding, training or setup you give away: does the act of delivering it
put new people in the room? Move: find the one step where your industry makes
the customer come to you, invert it, and count how many new prospects the
delivery itself produces.
Source: *Glock: The Rise of America's Gun*, Paul Barrett.
`founders/2024-03-26-the-biography-of-the-founder-of-glock.md`

**C5. Sol Price and the community charities.** Situation: a broke young lawyer
with no book of business and no way into the city's commercial life. Did: did
free legal work for community charities — real work, in person, for named
organisations, before there was any prospect of payment. Happened: those
relationships became his clients, and later his investors, his partners, and his
first landlord. Then vs now: unpaid work as a way into a community still works.
What has changed is that founders now default to doing it publicly and at a
distance, which strips out the relationship that did the actual work in this
case. The library records no version of this that worked without the personal
contact. Move: pick one community you want to be known in and do a real piece of
unpaid work inside it, for a named organisation, with a person who will remember
your face.
Source: *Sol Price: Retail Revolutionary and Social Innovator*, Robert Price.
`founders/2024-03-22-sol-price-the-founder-who-taught-jim-sinegal-sam-walton-jeff-bezos-and-bernie-ma.md`

# D. The free asset that has to pay off through usage

**D1. André Michelin and the road signs of France.** Situation: a tire company
that had pointed everything at one loop — more driving causes more wear causes
more tire sales — and needed the driving to be possible at all. Did: paid to
build thousands of road signs across France at its own expense, doing a
government's job, and printed on each sign that it was a gift from Michelin.
Trip planning at the Paris office was free too, and the guide was free for its
first twenty years. Happened: each free thing produced longer journeys, and
longer journeys wore out tires. A reporter noted that people assumed Michelin
tires were good because the maps and guides were so good, rather than the
reverse. Then vs now: the mechanism holds wherever a missing piece of
infrastructure caps how much your customer can use you. What has changed is that
the missing piece is now usually informational rather than physical, which makes
it cheaper to build and far easier for someone else to copy. Move: name the
missing thing that limits how much your customers can use you, build the crudest
version of it this week, and put your name on it.
Source: *The Michelin Men: Driving an Empire*, Herbert Lottman.
`founders/2025-07-09-the-greatest-marketer-of-all-time-and-the-cofounder-of-the-michelin-family-dynas.md`

**D2. André Michelin and the third star.** Situation: a free guide already
sitting in drivers' hands, and a company whose revenue depended on those drivers
travelling further than they had planned. Did: built the star ratings as a
distance multiplier rather than a quality score — one star is good locally, two
are worth a detour, three are worth the entire trip. Happened: the tier that can
only be reached by driving more became the most famous rating system in the
world, and the guide's fame produced another layer of coverage Michelin never
paid for. Then vs now: the design is available to anyone. Engineer the free
asset so its top tier is only reachable by consuming more of the paid product.
The century of accumulated authority behind the stars is not available to
anyone. Move: add one tier to your free asset that a customer can only reach by
using more of the paid thing, and name what that extra usage is worth to you.
Source: *Michelin: A Century of Secrets*, Alain Germain.
`founders/2025-07-09-the-greatest-marketer-of-all-time-and-the-cofounder-of-the-michelin-family-dynas.md`

**D3. André Michelin and the twelve thousand correction letters.** Situation: a
free guide that had to be kept accurate every year or it would stop producing
the longer journeys it existed to produce. Did: shipped a rudimentary first
edition in 1900 with a printed admission that it was imperfect and a promise it
would improve annually, and put a questionnaire inside every copy asking readers
what was wrong. Happened: eventually 150 employees worked on the guide and
12,000 readers mailed corrections back every year. He also ran newspaper ads
asking people to help improve the guide, which was itself an ad. Then vs now:
this is the clearest case in the library of a founder naming what it costs to
keep a free asset alive and then routing part of that cost to the users. It
transfers directly. The scale of 150 staff is what a working loop eventually
justifies, not what you start with. Move: ship version one of the free asset
this month with an explicit invitation to correct it, and put one specific
question inside it asking what you got wrong.
Source: *Michelin: A Century of Secrets*, Alain Germain.
`founders/2025-07-09-the-greatest-marketer-of-all-time-and-the-cofounder-of-the-michelin-family-dynas.md`

# E. Giving the thing away to build the audience

**E1. Dietrich Mateschitz and the seventy broadcasters.** Situation: a drinks
company that had turned itself into a producer of extreme sports events,
spending roughly half its marketing budget on them, with few people attending in
person. Did: funded and produced its own events, never released a single media
right, then handed hundreds of hours of finished footage to broadcasters at no
charge. The Air Race World Series went to over 70 television stations worldwide
free. Happened: the outlets took it eagerly because they skipped the production
cost entirely. The footage is worthless to anyone who is not selling cans, which
is exactly the test the loop demands. Then vs now: the mechanism of producing
content you fully own and giving it to channels that need programming still
works. What has changed is that broadcasters are no longer the scarce
distribution, so the counterparty who needs your by-product is now someone else
and you have to name them. Move: identify who needs the thing you already
produce as a by-product, give it to them free, and keep every right.
Source: the Mateschitz biography, Wolfgang Fürweger.
`founders/2025-11-25-red-bulls-billionaire-maniac-founder.md`

**E2. Bernie Ecclestone and the pan-European broadcast deal.** Situation:
Formula One teams negotiating individually, staying broke, with no assembled
television audience for sponsors to pay against. Did: priced the pan-European
broadcast deal cheap on purpose, and refused cherry-picking — broadcasters had
to air every Grand Prix in full or none at all. Every race was also moved to the
same hour so fans always knew when to show up. Happened: the fee was never the
point. Hundreds of hours of guaranteed exposure made sponsorships worth a
fortune. One bulk deal built the audience for a decade, after which he sold
country by country for far more. Then vs now: deliberately underpricing
distribution to manufacture the audience your real revenue depends on still
works. The all-or-nothing clause required leverage a founder starting out does
not have, so do not copy that half. Move: name the revenue line the audience
actually feeds, price the distribution that builds it at cost, and put a date in
the calendar for when you re-price it.
Source: *The Formula: How Rogues, Geniuses, and Speed Freaks Reengineered F1
into the World's Fastest-Growing Sport*, Joshua Robinson and Jonathan Clegg.
`founders/2025-07-22-the-outsiders-who-changed-f1-forever.md`

**E3. David Heinemeier Hansson and the insight budget.** PODCAST. Situation:
37signals with no money and a tiny team, competing against Microsoft's marketing
budget. Did: made publishing their best insights freely the operating strategy
for two decades — out-teach rather than outspend, on the reasoning that the
insight budget is the one you can actually win. He credits Kathy Sierra for it.
Happened: Senra's own case as the customer is that he had no employees for a
decade and no use for project management software, so he bought their books
instead, and bought Basecamp the moment he had a reason to. DHH endorses the
mechanism. Then vs now: DHH himself flags that algorithmic social feeds are
breaking this loop, because followers no longer see what you post unless it goes
semi-viral. Treat the publishing as still free and the reach as no longer free,
and budget for the difference. Move: publish the insight you would normally keep
as a competitive secret, and put the path to paying you in the same place,
obvious and frictionless.
Source: David Heinemeier Hansson with David Senra, 2026-07-26.
`david-senra/2026-07-26-why-constraints-lead-to-better-companies-products-dhh-37signals.md`

# F. The cases that tell you when not to give it away

**F1. Elon Musk and Tesla's patents.** PODCAST. Situation: an electric car
company operating in a category that barely existed, holding patents on the
thing it made. Did: open-sourced the patents, on the bet that by the time anyone
shipped a copy he would already be years ahead. The file files this under speed
as both offence and defence. Happened: the file records the bet and the
reasoning and gives no adoption numbers, so the return is undocumented here.
Then vs now: the mechanism only works if your genuine edge is iteration speed
rather than the thing you are handing over. Most founders' edge is the thing.
This is the case that tells you when not to give the asset away. Move: before
you publish your method, name the edge you keep after it is public. If the
method is the edge, keep it.
Source: Eric Jorgenson with David Senra, 2026-03-24.
`david-senra/2026-03-24-how-elon-thinks.md`

**F2. Gustav Söderström and the free tier aimed at Apple.** PODCAST. Situation:
Spotify with one product, no fallback, and the most respected product company on
earth publicly intending to kill it. Did: chose freemium as one of three
explicit counter-positions, each a bet on something the incumbent structurally
could not do. The freemium bet was that Apple would never execute advertising
well, given how much control advertising forces you to give up. The other two
were personalisation and ubiquity. Happened: he assesses all three bets as
having held. Internal Apple messaging at the time gave Spotify roughly six
months. Then vs now: the transferable idea is that a free tier is not a growth
tactic but a bet anchored to something a rival will not change about itself. A
free tier with no such anchor is just discounting, and a larger competitor can
match it whenever it decides to. Move: write down the one thing your largest
competitor would have to stop believing in order to copy your free tier. If you
cannot name it, do not run the free tier.
Source: Gustav Söderström with David Senra, 2026-06-07.
`david-senra/2026-06-07-the-company-apple-couldnt-kill-spotify-co-ceo-gustav-soderstrom.md`

# G. What is not on record

Every one of these was searched for and not found. State the relevant one in
every run. It is a required output line, because a corpus that has been searched
can say what it does not contain, and nothing reasoning from memory can.

**How to state it.** As an absence, never as a verdict. Holding no case of a
free software tool does not mean free software tools fail. It means nothing is
on record either way, so the member should price the attempt as untested and say
so in the artifact.

**The words the member reads.** Open by saying you have no documented case of
it, name their own situation in plain words, then name the closest case and how
it differs. The member never reads the words library, bank, card or corpus of
this file, and never a flourish about being the first. An absence is
information, not a warning against the ruling.

1. **No conversion rate from a giveaway to a paying customer, anywhere.** Not
   one founder on record reports one. Glock's nine in ten is a payment rate on
   units the agencies had already asked for, not a cold trial conversion, and
   nothing else comes close. Any conversion rate a member is given has to be
   labelled as their own guess.
   `founders/2024-03-26-the-biography-of-the-founder-of-glock.md`
2. **No founder states the marginal cost of delivering one free unit as a
   number.** Roblox's roughly a dollar per user is an advertising cost, not a
   cost to deliver. Every other founder on record names the giveaway without
   ever pricing it, which means the cost discipline this skill enforces is not
   itself demonstrated by a precedent.
   `david-senra/2026-04-26-robloxs-david-baszucki-built-the-biggest-playground-on-earth.md`
3. **No giveaway run as a measured test against a control, or stopped because
   it missed a number set in advance.** The tests on record either ran until
   they obviously worked or were never framed as tests at all.
4. **No founder describes deciding when to stop giving something away.** The
   Michelin Guide was free for its first twenty years and then was not; no file
   says why the price appeared or what happened to demand when it did.
   `founders/2025-07-09-the-greatest-marketer-of-all-time-and-the-cofounder-of-the-michelin-family-dynas.md`
5. **No free digital asset — a tool, template, calculator, dashboard or app —
   built by a founder to drive paid usage.** Every free asset on record is
   physical (samples, tires, road signs, printed guides, footage, hardware) or
   human (training, unpaid professional work). A member proposing a free
   software tool is reasoning past the evidence.
6. **Nothing on gating, email capture, lead magnets, nurture sequences or
   retargeting.** Not one founder on record describes a signup wall or a form
   in front of the free thing.
7. **No case of a free tier cannibalising paid revenue, and no founder
   measuring that cannibalisation.** Spotify's freemium is described only as a
   counter-position bet that held; the cost side of it is never discussed.
8. **No services, agency or software founder gave away billable hours as a
   trial and reported what it cost or what it returned.** Ovitz and Sol Price
   both gave unpaid work, and neither states the hours, the cost, or the
   conversion.
9. **No case of a giveaway tried and then abandoned as a failure.** Honda's
   uncosted credit is the nearest thing to a documented loss, and it was fixed
   by changing payment terms rather than by stopping the practice — so there is
   no example on record of a founder killing a free programme and saying what it
   had cost.
10. **No benchmark of any kind for how many free units are enough to draw a
    conclusion from.** Any sample size in the artifact is a judgement call and
    has to be stated as one.
