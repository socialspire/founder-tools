# Precedent Bank: The Repetition Ledger

Twenty cards, every one traced to a named file in
`socialspire/skool/insights/`. They exist to answer one question the member
almost always arrives with backwards: whether the thing that is running should
be changed, or run harder.

**Why a bank and not a deck.** Say the same thing until you are sick of it is a
principle any strong model already holds, so stating it adds nothing. Twenty
specific, verified cases — a stamp that ran roughly thirty years, a slot held
for forty-three, a run of 112 consecutive stories, a staff where not one person
asked had seen the previous week's video — are information. Read the index,
route to the sections the member's situation needs, and read only those. Do not
read the whole file on every run.

**Attribution rule.** Every card is paraphrase, including the ones marked
PODCAST. Not one phrase is quoted and none may be quoted downstream. Book cards
name the book and author. PODCAST cards name the founder, the show and the
date. A card drawn from a written profile read on the show names the profile,
its author and the show. No card ever goes inside quotation marks, because the
card text is this bank's rendering, not the founder's words. If a member asks
for a quotable line, the bank holds none.

# A. It still works and they want to change it

**A1. Jimmy Donaldson and his own staff.** Situation: the most studied content
operation on its platform, and the people employed inside it. Did: asked around
whether anyone had watched the previous week's gaming video, on the assumption
that the team producing the work would know its own catalogue. Happened: not one
person he asked had seen it. He built the onboarding memo around forced
consumption of the company's own back catalogue, and demands ten-hour-a-week
immersion in the medium. Then vs now: this is one anecdote inside an internal
memo, and it measures employees rather than customers, which is a real weakness.
It earns its place because it is the only point in the library where someone
actually checks whether a recent piece of work was seen, and the answer is no —
from the people paid to make it. Move: before refreshing anything because it
feels overexposed, ask five people in your market to name the last thing you
published. Approve the refresh only if they can.
Source: *How to Succeed in MrBeast Production*, Jimmy Donaldson, internal
onboarding memo.
`founders/2024-10-15-mr-beasts-leaked-memo.md`

**A2. Steve Stoute and the songs he never chose.** PODCAST. Situation: a music
executive living inside a system where radio and MTV decided what reached
anyone. Did: nothing deliberate — he was simply exposed, repeatedly, to records
he had no interest in. Happened: they lodged in his memory anyway. He turns it
into a commercial rule: awareness on its own changes the odds, because a coin
flip on something already heard beats a shot at something never heard. His
practice follows it — he refuses one-off placements and structures partnerships
as multi-quarter repetition schedules. Then vs now: the media monopoly that
could force that frequency is gone, so nobody can buy guaranteed repetition the
way a label could. The finding about the listener is untouched: disliking a
message and remembering it are separate systems, so your team's fatigue is not
evidence of the audience's. Move: strike the phrase people are sick of hearing
it from your reasoning entirely. Your evidence for an asset being tired is the
month-by-month numbers or it is nothing.
Source: Steve Stoute, on the David Senra podcast, 2026-06-21.
`david-senra/2026-06-21-the-marketing-genius-of-steve-stoute.md`

**A3. Michele Ferrero and the most stubborn employee.** Situation: a founder
with a handful of operating maxims and a workforce that had already heard all of
them. Did: repeated them up to sixty times in a single week, deliberately
calibrated to the least receptive person in the building rather than the
quickest. Happened: the maxims entered the company and stayed. He ran the
business for decades with no press conferences, no interviews and no factory
tours across 65 years, so the internal repetition was carrying weight no
external message was carrying. Then vs now: an employer has a captive audience
and a customer does not, so the frequency he could impose is not available to
you. The calibration is the transferable part — repetition is set by the slowest
person you need to reach, never by the speaker's own tolerance for hearing it.
Move: count the number of times your best customer has actually encountered your
one claim this quarter. If it is under five, the message has not been tested yet
and cannot be declared tired.
Source: *Michele Ferrero*, Salvatore Giannella.
`founders/2025-06-23-michele-ferreros-40-billion-privately-owned-chocolate-empire.md`

**A4. Thomas Peterffy and his father's envelope.** Situation: a boy in Hungary
receiving letters from America, and a stamp design nobody was trying to sell him
anything with. Did: nothing on his side — the green Statue of Liberty stamp on
the envelopes ran roughly 30 years without being redesigned. Happened: Peterffy
names it extremely effective advertising and says it helped pull him to America.
An asset aimed at nobody in particular changed one person's country decades
after it was designed, and he still cites it as the reason to commit a brand
asset for years rather than refresh it annually. Then vs now: a postage stamp is
not a channel a founder can buy, and the payoff arrived far too late to have
been measurable while it ran, so this proves nothing about knowing an asset is
working. What survives is the argument for leaving one thing unchanged long
enough to be recognised. Move: take the single brand asset you were going to
redesign this quarter and commit to running it unchanged for three years
instead, then spend the redesign budget on frequency.
Source: Colossus profile of Thomas Peterffy, Dom Cook (Founders Podcast, David
Senra, 2025-10-05).
`founders/2025-10-05-the-billionaire-who-automates-everything-thomas-peterffy.md`

**A5. Dietrich Mateschitz and the fifty rejected slogans.** Situation: a new
drink with no money and no traction, searching for the line it would run on.
Did: rejected roughly 50 slogans and campaigns over a year and a half before
accepting one, then never replaced it. Happened: the accepted line became the
permanent claim, recognised instantly by both men when it arrived in the middle
of the night. Everything afterwards pointed at it rather than replacing it —
roughly half the marketing budget went to sports so remote almost nobody
attends, and a $200M-a-year Formula One programme was justified on editorial
media value rather than profit. Then vs now: eighteen months of rejection is
affordable to someone already selling in one market and impossible for a founder
who needs revenue this quarter. The asymmetry is what transfers: the search cost
so much that the finished message was never treated as disposable afterwards.
Move: write down what finding your current message actually cost you in weeks.
Put that number at the top of any document proposing to replace it, and require
the replacement to be justified against it.
Source: *Dietrich Mateschitz biography*, Wolfgang Fürweger.
`founders/2025-11-25-red-bulls-billionaire-maniac-founder.md`

# B. Ruling on numbers, and running a challenger

**B1. Jerry Lambert and the monthly cycle.** Situation: a marketer running a
brand in an era when the annual advertising plan was the unit everyone worked
in. Did: broke the marketing into monthly cycles instead, reviewing advertising
and profit every month rather than once a year. Happened: roughly $25M in eight
years. Ogilvy carried the practice into his own agency and states the rule
flatly — run the business by the month, not by the year. Then vs now: monthly
review now reads as slow rather than fast, and the file records nothing about
what he did when a month came in low, which is the interesting half. The cadence
itself is the point: a monthly series is the only thing that makes a kill number
computable, and a founder reviewing quarterly cannot produce one at all. Move:
open a sheet with one row per month for your main asset and backfill every month
it has run. If you cannot fill the rows, stop the refresh conversation — that is
your finding.
Source: *Confessions of an Advertising Man*, David Ogilvy.
`founders/2024-03-24-david-ogilvy-confessions-of-an-advertising-man.md`

**B2. David Ogilvy and the nineteen-to-one gap.** Situation: an agency measuring
creative work against sales rather than against internal taste. Did: measured
execution quality in sales, wrote no fewer than 16 headlines per advertisement
before choosing one, and tested headline length against results. Happened: one
advertisement outperformed another by as much as 19 to 1. Five times as many
people read the headline as the body, so writing it spends most of the dollar,
and a headline change alone could swing sales tenfold. Headlines of ten words or
more consistently outsold short ones. Then vs now: those spreads came from
print, where one variable could be isolated cleanly and the sale attributed. The
number that matters is the spread rather than the medium — if two versions of
the same asset can differ that much, a challenger that merely ties the incumbent
has lost. Move: when you run a challenger against a proven asset, write the
incumbent's current monthly number down first and treat anything short of
beating it as the challenger failing. The challenger does not replace anything
on launch day.
Source: *Confessions of an Advertising Man*, David Ogilvy.
`founders/2024-03-24-david-ogilvy-confessions-of-an-advertising-man.md`

# C. Something new is proposed while the existing thing is under-run

**C1. Ned Green and the foreclosed railroad.** Situation: an heir handed a
railroad that had already been foreclosed on, with every reason to look for
something new instead. Did: spent ten years adding miles, fixing bridges, and
riding beside the engineer — one metric improved repeatedly rather than a fresh
initiative launched. Happened: the foreclosed line became a valuable railroad.
The file states the principle without softening it: value improves through
relentless small increments, not one dramatic move. Then vs now: ten years is
longer than most founders' runway, and the asset was owned outright with no
quarterly result to show anyone, which removed the pressure that usually causes
the switch. The direction still holds against the instinct to launch something
new the moment the existing thing looks merely unimpressive. Move: name the one
number in your business you will improve every week for the next quarter, and
cancel whichever new initiative was going to consume those hours.
Source: *Hetty: The Genius and Madness of America's First Female Tycoon*,
Charles Slack.
`founders/2025-01-13-the-richest-woman-in-america-hetty-green.md`

**C2. Russ and the thirteenth song.** PODCAST. Situation: an independent artist
with no label distribution and no gatekeeper willing to push him. Did: released
at high frequency rather than concentrating everything into one launch, and
owned the entire production stack — writing, recording, engineering, producing,
mixing and mastering himself. Happened: the thirteenth release reached someone
the first twelve had not, and that listener then went back and consumed the
whole catalogue. Stoute names a billion-stream album made alone on that method.
Then vs now: streaming pays per play and a back catalogue stays live
permanently, which most founder assets do not. The mechanism still transfers
cleanly: the new instalment finds the person and the archive is what converts
them, so instalment count is the asset, not the individual instalment. Move:
before adding a channel, count how many instalments a newcomer to your best
channel can actually find today. Under ten and the fix is more instalments in
that slot, not a new slot.
Source: Steve Stoute, on the David Senra podcast, 2026-06-21.
`david-senra/2026-06-21-the-marketing-genius-of-steve-stoute.md`

**C3. André Michelin and the advertisement for the advertisement.** Situation: a
company whose strongest marketing asset was a free guide it already published
and had already paid for. Did: bought newspaper advertising pointing at the
guide itself — asking readers to help improve it, announcing a competition and
repeating the brand name in one placement — and put a questionnaire inside every
copy asking what was wrong. Happened: eventually 150 employees worked on the
guide and 12,000 readers mailed corrections back each year. The asset became
famous enough that American publications wrote about it, producing a further
layer of coverage he never paid for. Then vs now: newspapers were the only mass
channel, so buying attention for your own asset was cheap and uncontested. The
move survives the change: the highest-return use of a campaign slot is often
pointing it at the asset you already own rather than building another one. Move:
instead of building a new asset this month, run your existing best one as the
subject of its own campaign, and put one specific question inside it asking
customers what you got wrong.
Source: *The Michelin Men: Driving an Empire*, Herbert Lottman.
`founders/2025-07-09-the-greatest-marketer-of-all-time-and-the-cofounder-of-the-michelin-family-dynas.md`

# D. Before you multiply: what to run, and what has to change

**D1. Les Schwab and the shovel of walnut shells.** Situation: a tire retailer
standing in a plant watching retread rubber get blended. Did: noticed the
blenders added either walnut shells or sawdust, asked for one shovel of each,
and built a claim on it — walnut for ice, sawdust for snow. He wrote all his own
radio spots rather than buying campaign work. Happened: customers brought old
tires in specifically to get it, for years. One observation on a factory floor
funded an advertising campaign that kept running, and he had lifted the shape of
the slogan from an unrelated industry rather than inventing a format. Then vs
now: retreading has largely disappeared and the specific claim is dead. The
mechanism costs nothing and is intact: the thing worth repeating for years was
found by standing where the product is physically made, not generated in a
planning session. Move: spend one hour inside the place your service is actually
delivered and write down one variable nobody in your category mentions. That is
your candidate for the action you run a hundred times, not ten.
Source: *Les Schwab: Pride In Performance — Keep It Going!*, Les Schwab.
`founders/2024-04-17-les-schwab-charlie-munger-recommended-this-book.md`

**D2. Charles Schulz and the four-panel frame.** Situation: a cartoonist working
inside one unchanging format for a working lifetime. Did: hand-drew nearly
18,000 strips over almost 50 years without altering the frame. The file sets him
beside Picasso, who averaged roughly a work a day for 71 years, and Dior's
house, which moved over 100,000 dresses out of 16,000 sketches in a decade.
Happened: the market selected the memorable output from the volume. The file's
stated mechanism is that people do not remember the weak work and that this is
the design rather than a defect — a designer never presents one line and lets
the market pick from the variety. Then vs now: three of these are creative
houses with a public that consumes new work continuously, which a service
business does not have. What transfers is the direction of causation — the hit
is selected out of volume produced inside one fixed frame, and cannot be chosen
in advance. Move: fix the frame before you raise the count. Do not let the
format change from instalment to instalment while you are still finding out
which instalment works, because then you have measured nothing.
Source: *Creators: From Chaucer and Durer to Picasso and Disney*, Paul Johnson.
`founders/2024-04-02-the-biography-of-balenciaga.md`

**D3. Michael Ovitz and Coca-Cola's commercial count.** PODCAST. Situation: a
client producing six commercials a year through a conventional agency
arrangement. Did: took the account and restructured how the work was produced
rather than how well it was produced. Happened: CAA delivered 35 commercials on
the same budget, and over 350 across the whole relationship. Ovitz's stated
frame is that volume is a strategy and not a byproduct, and he gave Senra the
identical instruction about the podcast — volume, and owned intellectual
property. Then vs now: he had an agency's roster of directors and writers to
draw on, which is what let the same budget stretch nearly six times. A founder's
ten-times comes out of hours and templates, not roster. His question transfers
exactly: what would have to change structurally to multiply output, not to
improve it. Move: take your single best-performing marketing action and name the
structural change — a template, a fixed slot, a delegated step — that would let
you run six times as many. Name the structure before you name the number.
Source: Michael Ovitz, on the David Senra podcast, 2025-11-23.
`david-senra/2025-11-23-caa-co-founder-michael-ovitz-failure-is-not-an-option.md`

# E. Setting a slot you are going to hold

**E1. Bernie Ecclestone and the two o'clock start.** Situation: a racing series
scattered across time zones, with an audience that had to hunt for it each week.
Did: moved every race to 2:00 p.m. Europe time so fans always knew when to show
up, and separately priced the pan-European broadcast deal cheap on purpose to
buy hundreds of guaranteed hours of exposure rather than a fee. Happened: the
file names consistency of schedule as a growth lever in its own right, not as
housekeeping. The underpriced bulk deal built the audience for a decade, after
which he unbundled and sold country by country for far more. Then vs now: a
global sport can dictate its own start time and you cannot dictate when anyone
opens anything. The half that transfers is the memorisable slot — same day, same
hour, so the audience never has to look for you and never has to decide. Move:
fix your publishing day and hour and refuse to move it, before you spend another
hour deciding what goes in the slot. The schedule is the commitment; the content
is the easy part.
Source: *The Formula: How Rogues, Geniuses, and Speed Freaks Reengineered F1
into the World's Fastest-Growing Sport*, Joshua Robinson and Jonathan Clegg.
`founders/2025-07-22-the-outsiders-who-changed-f1-forever.md`

**E2. Ingvar Kamprad and the same sermon for 43 years.** Situation: a founder
with a written set of operating principles and a company that never stopped
hiring. Did: delivered essentially the same sermon on those principles for 43
consecutive years to executives and new hires, and explicitly forbade amending
the core ones, separating what could be revised from what never could. Happened:
the written testament was distributed to over 100,000 employees, and the
principles governed the company rather than drifting. The audience refreshed
continuously because the company kept growing, so the same sermon met new ears
every year. Then vs now: this is the cleanest instance of the moving parade
anywhere in the library — the audience turned over by hiring, which made the
repetition obviously necessary rather than obviously boring. Nothing here
touches distribution, so it says nothing about getting a message heard by
strangers who did not join your company. Move: split your claims into the ones
you may revise and the ones you never will, tell your audience which is which in
writing, and then say the permanent ones again this month.
Source: *Leading By Design: The IKEA Story*, Ingvar Kamprad and Bertil Torekull.
`founders/2024-11-25-the-founder-of-ikea-ingvar-kamprad.md`

**E3. Ettore Bugatti and the first page of the annual catalogue.** Situation: a
car maker publishing one catalogue a year to substantially the same list of
buyers. Did: opened every annual catalogue with a list of races won, and made
every racing model a catalogue model any customer could actually buy. Happened:
the proof and the product were the same object, so each year's instalment
restated one unchanging claim with that year's fresh evidence rather than
reaching for a new claim. Customers could buy the exact thing that produced the
result. Then vs now: an annual catalogue to a small luxury list is far slower
than any cadence a founder needs now, and the racing record was ruinously
expensive to generate. The frame transfers directly and cheaply: a fixed opening
section that always carries the newest evidence for the same claim. Move: build
your standing slot's fixed frame so its first section is always the newest proof
of the one message, never the message restated. If you have no new proof this
week, that is the week's real problem.
Source: *The Bugatti Story*, L'Ebe Bugatti.
`founders/2024-04-03-the-biography-of-bugatti.md`

**E4. Joseph Pulitzer and the 112 consecutive stories.** Situation: an editor
with one issue he wanted to stick, in a market where 1,028 newspapers were
competing. Did: ran it day after day rather than once — as many as 112
consecutive stories on a single subject. He also reprinted every press comment
about the paper inside the paper, and set the newsroom a standing daily bar of
one thing worth talking about at dinner. Happened: repetition rather than a
single hit is what he credited for attention. He demanded the same set of
numbers every morning — printed, sold, returned, ad lines, spend, revenue in —
and never dropped the habit, not when he moved cities and not when he went
blind, running the paper by telegram and one-page report. Then vs now: he owned
the channel and the audience arrived daily by subscription; you own neither, so
nothing here says how a modern slot gets read. The count transfers as a floor —
112 is the library's only hard number for how many instalments on one message
counts as enough. Move: commit your standing slot to a number of instalments on
one message, not to a date, and start logging the same two numbers every week
beside the count.
Source: *Pulitzer: A Life in Politics, Print, and Power*, James McGrath Morris.
`founders/2026-06-20-joseph-pulitzer-the-inventor-of-mass-media.md`

# F. The slot is running and nothing has come back

**F1. Walt Disney and the network that paid for his advertisement.** Situation:
a park under construction, no marketing budget, and an industry that treated
television as the enemy. Did: took a weekly television show named after the
park, in which every episode described what he was building rather than pitching
prices or dates. Happened: the network paid him roughly $50,000 to $70,000 an
episode — he was paid to advertise a product that did not exist yet — and it ran
for eight months before opening. Opening day produced the worst traffic jam in
county history, and the press reviews were brutal, but the audience that had
watched eight months of him describing the dream understood day one was not the
finished thing. Then vs now: nobody is going to pay you to run your own
advertisement, and the show was assembled from a back catalogue he already
owned. The pre-launch mechanic is what transfers: the slot began two quarters
before the product existed, and it bought patience as well as attendance. Move:
start your standing slot before the thing you are selling is finished, and use
the instalments to describe what you are building rather than to sell it.
Source: *Disney's Land: Walt Disney and the Invention of the Amusement Park That
Changed the World*, Richard Snow.
`founders/2024-05-14-how-walt-disney-built-his-greatest-creation-disneyland.md`

**F2. Jimmy Buffett and the same cities every year.** Situation: a musician
whose two genuine hits were roughly 37 years apart, with every incentive to
chase new markets. Did: returned to the same cities annually instead, and
launched a physical newsletter in 1985 so he held his best customers' names and
addresses himself rather than through a platform. Happened: attendance became
generational — fans brought the children they had had since the last visit. The
business grew more valuable nearly every year across the 37-year gap. He had
earlier been denied payment for 50,000 jukebox plays by people who simply
declined to pay, and responded by building channels he owned outright. Then vs
now: a tour gives a physical annual slot with an unmissable date attached; a
founder's version is a calendar entry nobody is compelled to attend. The finding
holds regardless — the same audience returned to at a fixed interval compounded
harder than a new audience found each year. Move: choose the audience you will
return to on a fixed interval for the next ten years, and get their contact
details onto a list you own before your next instalment ships.
Source: *Jimmy Buffett: A Good Life All the Way*, Ryan White.
`founders/2024-04-10-the-biography-of-jimmy-buffett.md`

**F3. Barnett Helzberg and the shareholder letter he reread.** Situation: an
owner deciding who to sell his jewellery business to, with no relationship to
the eventual buyer. Did: reread an annual shareholder letter that had been
published in the same form, on the same schedule, for years — a standing slot
publishing criteria and reasoning rather than an offer. Happened: his own deal
originated from that reread. Senra's framing is that those annual letters may be
the most successful content marketing in business history, and the file's
instruction is to publish your criteria and your reasoning where the people you
want to hear from will find them. Then vs now: the author already had a
reputation that made the letters worth opening, which a founder starting out
does not, so this understates how long the slot runs before anything arrives.
The mechanism is still the cheapest on this list — publish what you decide by,
on a schedule, and wait. Move: write down the criteria you actually use to
decide who you work with, publish it in your standing slot, and leave it there
instead of replacing it with an offer.
Source: *What I Learned Before I Sold to Warren Buffett: An Entrepreneur's Guide
to Developing a Highly Successful Company*, Barnett C. Helzberg Jr.
`founders/2024-01-09-what-i-learned-before-i-sold-to-warren-buffett.md`

# G. What is not on record

Every one of these was searched for and not found. State the relevant one in
every run, in the member's own words. It is a required output line, because a
corpus that has been searched can say what it does not contain, and nothing
reasoning from memory can.

**How to state it.** As an absence, never as a verdict. Holding no case of
repetition failing does not mean repetition always works. It means nothing is on
record either way, so the member should price the ruling accordingly — and it is
the reason the kill number exists at all.

**The words the member reads.** The ten items below are written for you, not for
them, so none of them is ever read out as written. Open with I have no
documented case of, name their own situation in plain words, then name the
closest case and how it differs. The words library, bank, card, corpus and
record belong to this file and stay in it, as does any flourish about being the
first: an absence is information, not a warning against the ruling.

1. **No case of a challenger asset run alongside a proven one with a stated run
   length or sample size.** Ogilvy records a 19-to-1 spread between two
   advertisements and 16 headlines written per advertisement, but nothing
   anywhere says how long a test must run before the result is believable.
   `founders/2024-03-24-david-ogilvy-confessions-of-an-advertising-man.md`
2. **No case that separates an asset's decline from its market's decline.**
   Nothing on record distinguishes your message tiring from your category
   shrinking, so the Decay Sheet cannot cite a precedent for that distinction.
3. **No case of a solo or two-person operation holding a weekly publishing
   slot.** Every standing slot in the library — Michelin's column, Pulitzer's
   paper, Disney's show, Jimmy Buffett's tour, Bugatti's catalogue — sits behind
   a newsroom, a network, a studio or a factory.
4. **No case of modern distribution mechanics.** Nothing on search, feeds,
   ranking, email deliverability or list growth appears in any file. How a
   standing slot comes to be read by strangers is absent from the entire corpus.
5. **No case of a founder abandoning a standing slot, and no record of what that
   cost.** Every slot in the library is one that was held for decades, so there
   is no counterfactual and no measured downside.
6. **No case records what broke downstream when volume was multiplied.**
   Ovitz's six-to-thirty-five records the output going up and never the
   fulfilment, inbox, support or delivery consequence. Michelin is a partial
   exception running the other way: the guide collected reader corrections at
   scale, so what came back is on record while the cost of handling it is not.
   `david-senra/2025-11-23-caa-co-founder-michael-ovitz-failure-is-not-an-option.md`
   `founders/2025-07-09-the-greatest-marketer-of-all-time-and-the-cofounder-of-the-michelin-family-dynas.md`
7. **No case of repetition failing.** Nothing shows a founder running one claim
   for years while the market never came. Survivorship on this shelf is total,
   so the ten-times pass has no documented failure to price against.
8. **No case names the instalment number at which repetition began producing
   sales.** Michelin's guide, Pulitzer's runs and Kamprad's sermon all record how
   long they went on and never when they started working.
9. **No case of a proven asset retired on a numeric threshold.** Assets in the
   library run until the founder dies, sells, or is stopped by war or law — none
   is killed by crossing a number, so there is no precedent for what happens
   after a kill threshold fires.
10. **No case of month-by-month measurement at founder scale.** Pulitzer's daily
    figures came from a counting room and Ogilvy's monthly review from an agency;
    nothing on record shows a one-person business producing the number series the
    Decay Sheet requires.
