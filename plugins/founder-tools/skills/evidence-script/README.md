# The Evidence Script

## What this is for

You talk to someone about your idea. They say it sounds great. You walk away
feeling good and you have learned nothing.

That is the most common thing that happens to a founder, and it is expensive,
because "sounds great" feels like progress. Months later you have built the thing
and nobody buys it, and when you go back through your notes you find that not one
person ever did anything. They were being nice. You were counting it as proof.

This tool does two jobs.

**Before a conversation**, it gives you a one-page script: the opening line, the
questions to ask in order, the one thing to close on, and the list of questions
that would ruin the result if you asked them.

**After a conversation**, it grades what happened. You paste your notes. It tells
you whether you got hard evidence, a soft signal, or politeness, and it writes the
one follow-up message that would turn what you have into something real.

The grading is not gentle. That is the point. If it flatters you, it is not
working.

---

## Install it

Nothing to do here. This tool installed with the rest of them in one step.
The classroom lesson **Install the tools** covers it once, in about three minutes.

## Using it

**Before a conversation, say something like:**

> I have a call Thursday with Dana Whitfield, she runs a three-van plumbing
> company. I'm thinking about building a scheduling tool for trades. I want to
> know if the scheduling mess is actually costing her money.

Takes about five minutes. You leave with a script you can print.

**After a conversation, say something like:**

> Had the call with Dana. Here are my notes: [paste them]

Takes about two minutes. You leave with a verdict and a message ready to send.

---

## The one rule that makes this work

**Write down what they said. Not what you felt.**

Right after the call, before you do anything else, type out the actual lines.
Their words, as close as you can get them. Include the boring parts. Include the
numbers.

Do not write "she seemed really interested." That is not a thing she said, and it
cannot be graded. The whole tool runs on their words.

---

## One thing it will refuse to do

It will not build you a script for a segment.

If you say "I want to talk to small business owners," it will stop and ask you for
a name. Not because it is being difficult. Because a segment cannot pay you,
cannot show you their spreadsheet, and cannot introduce you to anyone. Every
useful thing in the script depends on the person being real enough to have a last
name.

Give it one name and it will build the script.

# Worked example

## Before the call

**What the member typed:**

> Call Thursday with Dana Whitfield, owner of Whitfield Plumbing, three vans, six
> guys. I'm thinking about a scheduling tool for small trades businesses. I want
> to know if the scheduling mess actually costs her anything real.

**What came back:**

```
CONVERSATION: Dana Whitfield — Whitfield Plumbing, owner, 3 vans — Thursday
YOU WANT TO LEARN: whether scheduling costs her real money, and how much

OPEN WITH
Dana, thanks for the time. I'm not selling you anything today, and I don't
have anything to sell yet. You run three vans and six guys, which is exactly
the size I don't understand. I want to hear how a week actually goes for you.
Can you walk me through last week?

ASK, IN THIS ORDER

1. Walk me through last Monday. Minute by minute, from when you woke up.
   Receipt: Tobi Lütke walks a person's story, then zooms into a moment where
   something went wrong and asks for it minute by minute. (Tobi Lütke, on the
   David Senra podcast, 2026-01-18)

2. How many hours went into the schedule last month, and who did them?
   Receipt: From outside, a late delivery looks like traffic. Tony Xu found it
   actually breaks into about twenty steps, each with its own seconds of delay.
   (Tony Xu, on the David Senra podcast, 2026-03-29)

3. Show me the thing you use instead. The spreadsheet, the whiteboard,
   the notebook. Can you turn your laptop round?
   Receipt: DoorDash's four founders talked to roughly 300 Bay Area businesses.
   The one that changed things was a one-person bakery who showed them a
   three-inch binder of delivery orders she had turned down. (Tony Xu, on the
   David Senra podcast, 2026-03-29)

4. What's on your books today that only exists because of scheduling?
   Receipt: Sol Price built fedmart on one number — 5,000 fedco members were
   already driving a 200-mile round trip from San Diego to save money. From
   Robert Price's Sol Price: Retail Revolutionary and Social Innovator.

5. What did you try before this, and why did you stop using it?
   Receipt: Rose Blumkin's favourite evening activity was driving around
   checking the competition. At 96, out of the business, she hired a driver to
   take her to rivals' stores to count cars in their lots. From Karen Linder's
   The Women of Berkshire Hathaway.

6. What's actually going well with how you handle it now?
   Receipt: Robert Caro asked Johnson's brother for the happy childhood
   anecdotes and got the admission that there weren't any. It was the most
   revealing answer in the project. From Working.

7. Two more before I go.
   (Write your two down before Thursday.)
   Receipt: Robert Caro's pattern is to have everything he needs, then think of
   one more question that must be answered for the work to be complete. From
   Working.

CLOSE ON
Dana, one ask. I want to build the first version against your actual week,
not a guess. Two options: I sit with you next Sunday while you do the
schedule, or you send me your last four weeks of job sheets. Either one is
fine. If neither is, say so, that's useful too.

Receipt: John Mackey found a stranger mopping during a flood cleanup — a
customer who had taken a day off work because he needed the store to
survive. (John Mackey, on the David Senra podcast, 2026-01-04)

DO NOT SAY
- "Would you use a scheduling app?" — she will say yes. Everyone says yes to a
  hypothetical, and you will believe her.
- "Is scheduling a pain point for you?" — the answer is inside the question.
- "I'm building a tool that fixes this." — the moment she knows what you want
  to hear, you stop getting her week and start getting your pitch back.
- "This would save you so much time, right?" — she will agree out of manners
  and you will write it down as a finding.
- "I've been working on this for six months." — now she has a reason to be
  kind to you instead of accurate.
- "Just a quick question." — a small ask gets a small answer.

AFTER THE CALL
Write down what she said, not what you felt. Paste it back here and I'll
grade it.
```

## After the call

**What the member pasted:**

> Went well I think. She said the scheduling thing is a real headache and it
> sounds great, she'd definitely use something like that. She said she pays her
> sister-in-law $200 a month to do the schedule by hand on Sunday nights because
> she got sick of doing it herself. Showed me the spreadsheet, it's a mess, she's
> been using the same one for four years. Said she'd tried Jobber and dropped it
> after two months, too much setup. When I asked about the job sheets she said
> send me an email and we'll sort something out. I could tell she was really
> into it.

**What came back:**

```
PROBLEM:  HARD EVIDENCE
SOLUTION: POLITENESS
RULING:   POLITENESS

THE LINE THAT DECIDED IT
"When I asked about the job sheets she said send me an email and we'll sort
something out."

WHAT IT PROVES
That she is polite and does not want to say no to your face.

WHAT IT DOES NOT PROVE
Anything about whether she wants what you are building. She was asked for
four weeks of job sheets, which cost her nothing but ten minutes, and she
did not hand them over.

Receipt: Visiting Silicon Valley, Tobi Lütke never got real answers. He got
aspirations, because people wanted to seem smart. (Tobi Lütke, on the David
Senra podcast, 2026-01-18)

---

The problem side is genuinely strong and you should know why. She pays $200 a
month, every month, to a named human, to do this by hand on a Sunday. That is
$2,400 a year leaving her account because of the exact thing you want to
build. She also showed you the spreadsheet, which is real, and she named a
product she tried and dropped, which is your specification.

None of that is about you. She has not moved one inch in your direction.

"Sounds great" and "she'd definitely use something like that" are worth
nothing. Neither is "I could tell she was really into it" — that is you,
not her.

SEND THIS

Dana, thanks again. One thing I should have been clearer about.

You mentioned the $200 a month for Sunday scheduling. I want to build the
first version against your real week rather than a guess, so I only need one
thing: your last four weeks of job sheets. Photos of the spreadsheet are
fine.

If you can send those by Friday, I'll have something you can actually try
within two weeks. If that's a no, tell me and I'll stop asking.
```

Notice what the follow-up does. It does not re-pitch. It does not get louder. It
makes the ask smaller, names a date, and gives her a clean way out. That last part
is not politeness, it is how you find out.

---

## What you will get told that you may not enjoy

- "Sounds great" is not evidence.
- "I'd definitely use that" is not evidence.
- An offer to introduce you is not an introduction. Only a sent message counts.
- "I could tell she was interested" is not something anyone said.
- If five conversations in a row went well, that is a finding about your
  questions, not about your market.

---

## What it will not do

It will not tell you how many conversations count as validation. No such number
exists in the material behind this tool, and it will say so rather than make one
up.

It will not tell you to kill your idea. That call is yours.

It will not promise you an outcome.
