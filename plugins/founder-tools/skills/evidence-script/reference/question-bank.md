# The Question Bank

25 questions in eight groups. Every question was built from a documented
principle. The principle is real and cited. **The wording of the question is
ours.** Never tell a member that a founder asked this question. None of them did.

Adapt the wording to the member's situation. Keep the shape. Do not invent
questions that are not here.

**Attribution key**
- `PARAPHRASE` — never put in quotation marks. Name the book and author as given.
- `QUOTABLE` — may be quoted verbatim, must carry speaker, show and episode date.
- `BOOK NOTES` — cite as "book notes on *Title*". No author, no quotation marks.

---

## Group A — Money already spent

Use at least one. Never open with these.

**Q1. "What do you spend on this problem right now? Give me the number and what it's for."**

Use when: you have no idea whether the problem is priced.
Follow with: "What's that made of?" until you get line items.

> Receipt: As a teenager Michael Dell called distributors for per-hundred chip
> quotes, built the bill of materials, and compared it to the sticker price. The
> gap was the opportunity. (Michael Dell, on the David Senra podcast, 2025-10-12)
> `PARAPHRASE`

---

**Q2. "The last time you paid someone to fix this — who, when, how much?"**

Use when: they have already told you it is a problem and you want it dated.
This question converts an opinion into a transaction with a name on it.

> Receipt: Lyndon Johnson raged at staffers who reported what they thought a
> senator would do rather than what they knew. Robert Caro tells this in
> *Working*. `PARAPHRASE`

---

**Q3. "What's on your card or your books today that only exists because of this problem?"**

Use when: they say they do not spend anything on it. They almost always do.

> Receipt: Sol Price built fedmart on one number — 5,000 fedco members were
> already driving a 200-mile round trip from San Diego to save money. From Robert
> Price's *Sol Price: Retail Revolutionary and Social Innovator*. `PARAPHRASE`

---

**Q4. "What did the workaround cost you last quarter? Not the problem — the workaround."**

Use when: they have already described a workaround. Most people price the problem
and never price the thing they built to survive it.

> Receipt: Barnett Helzberg's framing of opportunity cost is that every activity
> you choose is priced in the activities you gave up. From *What I Learned Before
> I Sold to Warren Buffett*. `PARAPHRASE`

---

## Group B — Time already spent

**Open with one of these. Always.** You get the story first. The numbers fall out
of the story.

**Q5. "Walk me through the last time this happened. Minute by minute, from the start."**

The default opener. Do not accept a summary. Push for the specific instance, with
a date attached, and keep asking what happened next.

> Receipt: Tobi Lütke walks a person's story, then zooms into a moment where
> something went wrong and asks for it minute by minute. (Tobi Lütke, on the
> David Senra podcast, 2026-01-18) `PARAPHRASE`

---

**Q6. "How many hours went into it last month, and who did them?"**

Use when: the story is out and you need it costed. "Who did them" matters — a
task the founder does personally is priced differently from one an assistant does.

> Receipt: From outside, a late delivery looks like traffic or a slow kitchen.
> Tony Xu found it actually breaks into about twenty steps, each with its own
> seconds of delay. (Tony Xu, on the David Senra podcast, 2026-03-29) `PARAPHRASE`

---

**Q7. "How far out of your way do you already go to work around this?"**

Use when: you want a measure of how badly they want it solved, without asking
them how badly they want it solved. Distance travelled is the answer.

> Receipt: Whole Foods measured its own differentiation by what customers did,
> not what they said — people drove up to 100 miles and spent $300 to $400 on
> weekend stock-up trips. (John Mackey, on the David Senra podcast, 2026-01-04)
> `PARAPHRASE`

---

## Group C — Physical artifacts and workarounds

**Use exactly one. Never skip this group.** It is the highest-yield question in
the bank. People describe problems badly and build workarounds honestly.

**Q8. "Show me the thing you use instead. The spreadsheet, the folder, the notebook."**

The single best question here. Ask to see it on the screen, on the desk, in the
drawer. If the meeting is remote, ask them to share their screen.

> Receipt: DoorDash's four founders talked to roughly 300 Bay Area businesses.
> The one that changed things was a one-person bakery who showed them a
> three-inch binder of delivery orders she had turned down. (Tony Xu, on the
> David Senra podcast,
> 2026-03-29) `PARAPHRASE`

---

**Q9. "What did you try before this, and why did you stop using it?"**

Use when: there is an existing category and you need to know what already failed
for them. The reason they stopped is your specification.

> Receipt: Rose Blumkin's favourite evening activity was driving around checking
> the competition. At 96, out of the business, she hired a driver to take her to
> rivals' stores to count cars in their lots. From Karen Linder's *The Women of
> Berkshire Hathaway*. `PARAPHRASE`

---

**Q10. "Who else in the building already knows this is a problem?"**

Use when: you are inside a company and the person you are meeting is not the one
doing the work. Then go talk to that person.

> Receipt: To learn how Johnson campaigned, Robert Caro skipped the campaign
> manager and kept returning to the chauffeur, the one person with him all day,
> every day. From *Working*. `PARAPHRASE`

---

## Group D — Demand with the inducement removed

Use when a price exists, or a discount or free trial is on the table.

**Q11. "If there were no discount and no free trial, would you still want it?"**

> Receipt: Tony Xu chased organic demand and never bought demand — whether
> someone genuinely wants the product, unaided by discounts and marketing
> dollars, and whether they will tell a friend. (Tony Xu, on the David Senra
> podcast, 2026-03-29) `PARAPHRASE`

---

**Q12. "Who would you tell about this? Name them."**

Insist on a name. "My whole network" is a no.

> Receipt: Estée Lauder called her word-of-mouth campaign Tell a Woman. Customers
> sold her cream to each other before ever reaching her counter. From her memoir,
> *Estée: A Success Story*. `PARAPHRASE`

---

**Q13. "Will you introduce me to them this week?"**

The upgrade on Q12, and it belongs immediately after it. This is where the
conversation stops being free for them.

> Receipt: An ally of Estée Lauder's casually told a department store manager
> that Estée's cosmetics were why she looked well, then phoned Estée to make the
> introduction. From *Estée: A Success Story*. `PARAPHRASE`

---

## Group E — Commitment

Close on this group when nothing is built yet, or when a price exists.

**Q14. "What would have to be true for you to pay for this before it exists?"**

The answer is your build order.

> Receipt: Brunello Cucinelli's business began with a first order of 53 sweaters,
> a yarn supplier who let him pay after he got paid, and a customer who paid
> upfront on the strength of belief in him. From *The Dream of Solomeo*.
> `PARAPHRASE`

---

**Q15. "Would you put down a small amount now to hold a place?"**

Small is the point. A humiliatingly small real commitment beats a large
imaginary one.

> Receipt: Harrods first offered Estée Lauder a tiny slot buried among general
> toiletries rather than a place beside the good cosmetics. She took it, and used
> the sales to negotiate up. From *Estée: A Success Story*. `PARAPHRASE`

---

**Q16. "What date are you deciding on, and what happens between now and then?"**

Both halves. A date with no trigger event is a stall wearing a calendar.

> Receipt: James Dyson treated slowness as the signal. One partner stalled him
> eight months with endless handle redesigns and never intended to produce.
> Another firm understood the product and closed in three weeks. From *Against
> the Odds*. `PARAPHRASE`

---

**Q17. "What would you stop doing, or stop paying for, if this worked?"**

Use when: they say the problem is not that bad. This is the question that finds
out.

> Receipt: Barnett Helzberg's rule was to name explicitly, before your next
> commitment, what that commitment prevents you from doing. From *What I Learned
> Before I Sold to Warren Buffett*. `PARAPHRASE`

---

**Q18. "What does saying yes to me cost you internally? Who loses?"**

**Add this every time the person you are meeting loses something if you succeed.**
Their objection will arrive dressed as a product critique. It is a margin
problem, or a headcount problem, or a reputation problem.

> Receipt: Sam Walton offered Ben Franklin's executives the discounting concept
> and himself as the guinea pig. It required cutting their 25% wholesale margin
> to roughly 12%. They blew up at him. From Vance Trimble's *Sam Walton: The
> Inside Story of America's Richest Man*. `PARAPHRASE`

---

## Group F — Demonstration instead of description

Close on this group when a rough version exists.

**Q19. "Can I do it for you right now, on your actual problem? Five minutes."**

Not a demo of your product. The work, done on their thing, in front of them.

> Receipt: When a salon owner asked Estée Lauder to just leave the products
> behind, she refused and insisted on five minutes to apply them herself. That
> demonstration won her first retail space. From *Estée: A Success Story*.
> `PARAPHRASE`

---

**Q20. "Here's the rough version. Use it this week and tell me what broke."**

The rough version is allowed to be embarrassing. It is not allowed to be absent.

> Receipt: DoorDash's first product went live in 43 minutes — a $9 domain, a
> static page with eight scanned restaurant menus, and a Google Voice number that
> rang all four founders' cell phones. They drove the food themselves and
> collected payment with a Square dongle. (Tony Xu, on the David Senra podcast,
> 2026-03-29) `PARAPHRASE`

---

## Group G — Disconfirming

**At least one. Non-negotiable.** A script without one of these is a pitch with
question marks on it.

**Q21. "Tell me why you wouldn't buy this."**

Say it flat. Do not soften it into "what concerns might you have". Then say
nothing until they answer.

> Receipt: Jeff Bezos let customers see negative information about products.
> Vendors asked whether he understood what business he was in. His answer was
> that negative reviews cost sales in the short term, but helping customers decide
> well pays off. From *Invent and Wander: The Collected Writings of Jeff Bezos*.
> `PARAPHRASE`

---

**Q22. "What's actually going well with how you handle it now?"**

Sounds soft. Is not. The pause before the answer tells you where the truth is,
and a fluent answer tells you the problem is smaller than you hoped.

> Receipt: Robert Caro asked Johnson's brother for the happy childhood anecdotes
> and got the admission that there weren't any. It was the most revealing answer
> in the project. From *Working*. `PARAPHRASE`

---

**Q23. "What am I not doing that you'd want?"**

The third of Helzberg's three, and the one that produces usable answers.

> Receipt: Barnett Helzberg engineered honest feedback with three questions —
> what am I doing that you like, what am I doing that you dislike, and what am I
> not doing that you would like. From *What I Learned Before I Sold to Warren
> Buffett*. `PARAPHRASE`

---

**Q24. "What else do you need that nobody sells you?"**

Use late, once they trust you. The answer is often a better business than the one
you walked in with.

> Receipt: David Packard's team asked the Naval Research Laboratory what other
> instruments it needed. That question produced both new products and
> manufacturing knowledge HP did not previously have. From *The HP Way*.
> `PARAPHRASE`

---

## Group H — After it feels finished

**Q25. "Two more before I go." Then ask two.**

Always the last item on the script. Write the two down in advance so you actually
have them when the moment comes.

> Receipt: Robert Caro's pattern is to have everything he needs, and then think of
> one more question that must be answered for the work to be complete. From
> *Working*. `PARAPHRASE`

---

## Framing note for the opening line

Ask for advice, not feedback. Feedback invites a verdict on you. Advice invites
them to talk about their own situation, which is the only thing you can act on.

> Receipt: Recorded in book notes on *This Is Marketing* as a working principle.
> `BOOK NOTES`

## Supporting principles for the prep conversation

Use these to explain a choice in the script. They are not questions.

**Take the conversation yourself.** Dyson's position is that only by selling what
you made, meeting the objections and the failures in person, do you understand
what you built. From *Against the Odds*. `PARAPHRASE`
Tony Xu still works support every day. (Tony Xu, on the David Senra podcast,
2026-03-29) `PARAPHRASE`
Jason Fried sends new Basecamp signups a letter with his real email address and
signature, no AI and no assistant, and answered around 200 customer emails the day
before that conversation. (Jason Fried, on the David Senra podcast, 2026-02-15)
`PARAPHRASE`

**One idea per conversation.** Dyson's vacuum was also excellent at wet cleaning.
He never mentioned it, because it would have undercut the single claim. A buyer
can barely absorb one great idea. From *Against the Odds*. `PARAPHRASE`

**Specific beats adaptable.** Dyson pitching one adaptable craft to divers, oil
crews and the military converted nobody. Selling a purpose-built diving boat to a
diver worked. From *Against the Odds*. `PARAPHRASE`

**Meet them where they are already thinking about it.** Estée Lauder chose the
beauty salon because women were already in a self-improvement mindset. From
*Estée: A Success Story*. `PARAPHRASE`

**Rehearse the specific conversation.** Estée Lauder mentally rehearsed a heroic
sale to a large department store step by step before it happened. From *Estée: A
Success Story*. `PARAPHRASE`
Li Lu's approach was to out-prepare rather than out-talk. From *Moving the
Mountain*. `PARAPHRASE`

**Do not leave them empty-handed.** Estée Lauder shaved off bits of lipstick,
folded powder into wax envelopes, and handed samples to women who bought nothing.
Trying it at home in their own mirror is what converted them. From *Estée: A
Success Story*. `PARAPHRASE`

**Decide on the conversation at about 70%.** Bezos's rule is to decide at roughly
70% of the information you wish you had. Waiting for 90% is usually too slow, and
if you are good at course-correcting, being wrong costs less than being slow.
From *Invent and Wander*. `PARAPHRASE`

**Push past the point where you have the answer.** Li Lu, locked in a boarding
school, interrogated classmates about their weekends, interrupting and pressing
for detail, and built a permanent habit of extracting information from people.
From *Moving the Mountain*. `PARAPHRASE`

---

## What is not here

There is no customer-discovery methodology in this material. No interview
template, no survey design, no sample-size guidance, no B2B versus B2C split, no
pre-sale or letter-of-intent template, no pricing-test procedure.

If a member asks for one of those, say it is not here rather than inventing it.
