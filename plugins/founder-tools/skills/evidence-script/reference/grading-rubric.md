# The Grading Rubric

Three tiers. **This framework is SocialSpire's construction.** Every tier is
built on a documented principle, but HARD EVIDENCE / SOFT SIGNAL / POLITENESS is
not a framework any founder in this material ever used. Never present it as one.

**Attribution key**
- `PARAPHRASE` — never in quotation marks. Name the book and author as given.
- `QUOTABLE` — may be quoted verbatim, must carry speaker, show and episode date.
- `BOOK NOTES` — cite as "book notes on *Title*". No author, no quotation marks.

---

## The governing principle

Trust movement. Alfred Adler's position, which Barnett Helzberg quotes
approvingly, is that movement is the only thing worth trusting, and Helzberg pairs
it with Andrew Carnegie's parallel: pay less attention to what people say and
watch what they do. From *What I Learned Before I Sold to Warren Buffett*.
`PARAPHRASE`

The second half of it, and the rubric in one sentence: strip every "I think" out
and require confirmed commitments. Lyndon Johnson raged at staffers who reported
what they thought a senator would do rather than what they knew. Robert Caro tells
this in *Working*. `PARAPHRASE`

---

## Score two columns, not one

Every buyer line gets scored twice.

**PROBLEM** — is the problem real, and priced?
Money already spent on it. Time already spent on it. A workaround they built and
maintain. Distance travelled.

**SOLUTION** — did anything move toward *you*?
Money, their own time, a name they actually contacted, a date on a calendar, an
artifact handed over.

These come apart constantly. A person can be spending $400 a month on a bad
version of your idea and still not have moved one inch in your direction. Naming
that gap is the most useful thing you can tell a member, because most of them
cannot see it themselves.

**The headline ruling is the lower of the two.** You do not have anything until
both are real.

Grade each column to the highest tier any single line earns. Not the average. Not
the mood of the room.

# TIER 1 — HARD EVIDENCE

Something left the buyer's hands. Money, a block of their own time, a name they
actually contacted, or a date on a calendar.

### 1a. Money committed

> Receipt: Builders and retailers rejected James Dyson's ballbarrow outright. The
> public mailed checks to an unknown company off a tiny newspaper drawing. From
> *Against the Odds*. `PARAPHRASE`

> Receipt: Jason Fried's first product was a database for tracking loaned-out CDs,
> sold with a note asking for $20. A stranger in Germany mailed cash. (Jason
> Fried, on the David Senra podcast, 2026-02-15) `PARAPHRASE`

> Receipt: Brunello Cucinelli's first customer paid upfront on the strength of
> belief in him. From *The Dream of Solomeo*. `PARAPHRASE`

**Column note.** Money to *you* scores SOLUTION. Money to someone else, for the
same problem, scores PROBLEM only.

### 1b. Their own time, unpaid and unprompted

Unrequested work is the strongest signal you will ever get.

> Receipt: John Mackey found a stranger mopping during a flood cleanup — a
> customer who had taken a day off work because he needed the store to survive.
> (John Mackey, on the David Senra podcast, 2026-01-04) `PARAPHRASE`

> Receipt: Sol Price built fedmart on one number — 5,000 fedco members were
> already driving a 200-mile round trip from San Diego to save money. From Robert
> Price's *Sol Price: Retail Revolutionary and Social Innovator*. `PARAPHRASE`

Tony Xu's version of this is about hiring rather than buying, so use it as
evidence that unrequested effort is the signal, never as a customer-conversation
example: Christopher Payne, later DoorDash's COO, spent four hours doing
deliveries with his son on a Friday night and sent a 30,000-word email the next
morning on why the algorithm was broken, unprompted and before being hired. (Tony
Xu, on the David Senra podcast, 2026-03-29) `PARAPHRASE`

### 1c. An introduction actually made

An offer to introduce is not an introduction. Only the sent message counts.

> Receipt: An ally of Estée Lauder's told a department store manager that Estée's
> cosmetics were why she looked well, then phoned Estée to make the introduction.
> From *Estée: A Success Story*. `PARAPHRASE`

### 1d. A specific date agreed, with a named next step

A date with no trigger event is a stall wearing a calendar. Both halves required.

> Receipt: James Dyson treated slowness as the signal. One partner stalled him
> eight months with endless handle redesigns and never intended to produce.
> Another firm understood the product and closed in three weeks. From *Against
> the Odds*. `PARAPHRASE`

### 1e. A physical artifact handed over

They showed you the spreadsheet, the binder, the invoice, the folder.

> Receipt: A one-person bakery showed DoorDash's founders a three-inch binder of
> delivery orders she had turned down. (Tony Xu, on the David Senra podcast,
> 2026-03-29) `PARAPHRASE`

> Receipt: Rose Blumkin would produce the supplier's bill to show a customer her
> actual cost. Verifiable evidence outranks a claim. From Karen Linder's *The
> Women of Berkshire Hathaway*. `PARAPHRASE`

# TIER 2 — SOFT SIGNAL

Real information. No cost incurred. Worth logging. Not proof.

### 2a. A long, specific, unprompted complaint

Length and specificity is the dial. A one-line complaint is closer to politeness.

> Receipt: Tony Xu mines the long complaints. Short emails describe things you
> already know. A 2,000-word email from a driver enumerating how the logistics
> algorithm failed becomes a debugging exercise, and he traces it through internal
> tools himself, then calls the driver to test his hypothesis. (Tony Xu, on the
> David Senra podcast, 2026-03-29) `PARAPHRASE`

### 2b. An unprompted request for something adjacent

> Receipt: Brunello Cucinelli held to women's coloured cashmere sweaters for
> roughly two decades. The move into full clothing came because international
> buyers asked for a total look. From *The Dream of Solomeo*. `PARAPHRASE`

> Receipt: In DoorDash's second year, a three-generation California farm running
> hundreds of trucks asked whether the company could take over their logistics.
> (Tony Xu, on the David Senra podcast, 2026-03-29) `PARAPHRASE`

### 2c. They named a workaround they already built

Described, not shown. If they showed it, that is 1e.

### 2d. Someone other than the buyer said it

A claim reported by a person with no stake outranks the same claim from the person
you are pitching.

> Receipt: David Ogilvy's position on third-party proof is that readers believe a
> fellow customer's endorsement more readily than an anonymous copywriter's
> puffery. From *Confessions of an Advertising Man*. `PARAPHRASE`

### 2e. They asked the price without being prompted

> Receipt: Book notes on *Influence: The Psychology of Persuasion* record that
> people want a solution consistent with the severity of their problem, and that
> a sequence of small yeses precedes a real one. `BOOK NOTES`

### The boundary rule

Use soft signals to design, not to decide. Tony Xu's rule is that data wins
prioritization but anecdotes win product improvement — customer complaints almost
always sit at some tail of a distribution, so aggregate data wins any resource
argument, while the product gets better by fixing the edges, which is exactly
where anecdotes disagree with the data. (Tony Xu, on the David Senra podcast,
2026-03-29) `PARAPHRASE`

# TIER 3 — POLITENESS

Warm, sincere, and worth nothing as evidence.

**This is the default ruling.** The burden of proof sits on the evidence.

### 3a. Adjectives with no number attached

"Great", "really useful", "a game changer", "exactly what I need."

> Receipt: David Ogilvy's finding was that facts sell and adjectives do not. His
> Rolls-Royce advertising carried facts and no gracious-living puffery, and Claude
> Hopkins had reached the same conclusion fifty years earlier. From *Confessions
> of an Advertising Man*. `PARAPHRASE`

### 3b. "I think", "I would", "probably", "definitely"

The cleanest tell in the whole rubric. "I'd definitely use that" is Tier 3.

> Receipt: Johnson demanded to know, not to be told what someone thought. Robert
> Caro tells this in *Working*. `PARAPHRASE`

### 3c. Aspiration presented as fact

> Receipt: Visiting Silicon Valley, Tobi Lütke never got real answers. He got
> aspirations, because people wanted to seem smart. (Tobi Lütke, on the David
> Senra podcast, 2026-01-18) `PARAPHRASE`

This is the sharpest receipt in the material for this tier.

### 3d. Praise from someone whose interest you have not checked

> Receipt: Napoleon publicly praised an incompetent opposing general specifically
> so that general's government would keep him in the job. It worked. From the
> Napoleon's maxims material, recorded from Thomas R. Phillips' *Roots of
> Strategy* and *Napoleon and Modern War*. `PARAPHRASE`

Ask what the other party gains before you accept their flattery as accurate.

### 3e. Encouragement from someone with a reason to be nice to you

Friends, family, mentors, anyone in your own community, anyone senior to you,
anyone who loses nothing if you fail.

> Receipt: UPS founder Jim Casey concluded executives would tell him what he
> wanted to hear, so he had his driver pull over at every brown truck to talk to
> drivers directly. (Recorded in the David Senra podcast episode with Jason Fried,
> 2026-02-15, as Senra's own contribution) `PARAPHRASE`

The instruction that follows from it: actively solicit the strongest available
critique of your strategy from someone with no incentive to flatter you. (David
Senra podcast, Brad Jacobs episode, 2025-10-26) `PARAPHRASE`

### 3f. Anything reported from research rather than observed

> Receipt: James Dyson's rule was to believe only the evidence of his own eyes,
> not formulas, not other people's opinions, not research. Research reports what
> has already happened; it cannot tell you what will happen. From *Against the
> Odds*. `PARAPHRASE`

### 3g. Anything the member inferred rather than heard

"I could tell she was really interested." "The energy was great." Tier 3, and say
so plainly.

# THE FOLLOW-UP LADDER

Find the rung that matches how their conversation ended. Take the one ask. Write
it out ready to send.

**1. "Sounds great." / "I love it."** → Ask for the calendar or the card. Nothing
smaller. If they will not give either, take the worst version of the commitment
to get a real one.
> Receipt: Harrods first offered Estée Lauder a tiny slot buried among general
> toiletries. She took it, and used the sales to negotiate up. From *Estée: A
> Success Story*. `PARAPHRASE`

**2. "I'd definitely use that."** → Hand them the crude version and set a return
date. Use it this week, tell me what broke by Friday.
> Receipt: DoorDash's first product went live in 43 minutes — a $9 domain, a
> static page with eight scanned restaurant menus, and a Google Voice number that
> rang all four founders' cell phones. (Tony Xu, on the David Senra podcast,
> 2026-03-29) `PARAPHRASE`

**3. "Send me some info."** → Replace the info with a demonstration on their own
problem, or with something genuinely useful written for them specifically.
> Receipt: A Seagram founder quoted back the closing paragraphs of a 16-page
> speech David Ogilvy had sent him, and hired him. From *Confessions of an
> Advertising Man*. `PARAPHRASE`

**4. "Let me talk to my partner / my boss."** → Who exactly? Can we book that with
me in the room?
> Receipt: Robert Caro skipped the campaign manager and kept returning to the
> chauffeur, the one person with Johnson all day, every day. From *Working*.
> `PARAPHRASE`

**5. "That's not for me."** → Who is? Will you introduce me?
> Receipt: An ally of Estée Lauder's phoned a department store manager to make the
> introduction. From *Estée: A Success Story*. `PARAPHRASE`

**6. "It's too expensive."** → What are you spending on it now? Then show your
cost basis.
> Receipt: Rose Blumkin would produce the supplier's bill. She sold at 10% over
> cost from the beginning and never drifted upward, and that verifiable honesty
> turned single transactions into lifetimes of repeat business. From Karen
> Linder's *The Women of Berkshire Hathaway*. `PARAPHRASE`

**7. "The price is fine, the timing is bad."** → Get the date and the trigger
event. Not "later." A date, and what has to happen first.
> Receipt: James Dyson treated slowness as the signal and set decision deadlines.
> From *Against the Odds*. `PARAPHRASE`

**8. Silence, or "maybe later."** → Make the ask smaller, not louder. Remove their
risk instead of raising your volume.
> Receipt: Told the cosmetics category was locked up by exclusives, Estée Lauder
> pitched bath oil instead and offered it on consignment. If it does not sell, you
> have lost nothing. From *Estée: A Success Story*. `PARAPHRASE`

**9. A gatekeeper or an expert said no.** → Take the identical offer straight to
the end user.
> Receipt: Builders and retailers rejected James Dyson's ballbarrow outright. The
> public mailed checks off a tiny newspaper drawing. From *Against the Odds*.
> `PARAPHRASE`
> Receipt: Rejected by Ben Franklin's executives, Sam Walton opened a 16,000
> square foot store himself that did $700,000 in its first year and grew 30%
> annually for fifteen years. From Vance Trimble's *Sam Walton: The Inside Story
> of America's Richest Man*. `PARAPHRASE`

**10. It ended with them complimenting you.** → What is the one reason you would
not buy this?
> Receipt: Jeff Bezos published negative information about products. Negative
> reviews cost sales in the short term, but helping customers decide well pays
> off. From *Invent and Wander*. `PARAPHRASE`

**11. A vague yes, because you never actually asked for anything.** → Ask. Now.
> Receipt: Barnett Helzberg's point about simply making the request is that the
> downside is a wasted call and the upside is a relationship or an idea worth
> years. At twelve, Steve Jobs phoned Bill Hewlett for spare parts and left with
> both the parts and a summer job. Most people never place the call. From *What I
> Learned Before I Sold to Warren Buffett*. `PARAPHRASE`

**12. "The problem isn't really that bad."** → What do you already spend on it,
and what would you give up if it went away?
> Receipt: Barnett Helzberg's opportunity-cost rule is to name explicitly what a
> commitment prevents you from doing. From *What I Learned Before I Sold to
> Warren Buffett*. `PARAPHRASE`

**13. You ran out of questions and it felt finished.** → Ask two more.
> Receipt: Robert Caro's pattern is to have everything he needs, then think of one
> more question that must be answered for the work to be complete. From *Working*.
> `PARAPHRASE`

# WHY MEMBERS HEAR WHAT THEY WANT TO HEAR

Use these when a member argues with a POLITENESS ruling. Pick one. Do not stack
them.

**People give you aspirations, not answers, because they want to look smart.**
Visiting Silicon Valley, Tobi Lütke never got real answers. He got aspirations.
(Tobi Lütke, on the David Senra podcast, 2026-01-18) `PARAPHRASE`

**Anyone above you in a hierarchy is being told what they want to hear, by
default.** Jim Casey had his driver pull over at every brown truck to talk to
drivers directly. Executives filter information upward into an overly rosy
picture; the people touching customers have the unfiltered version. (David Senra
podcast, Jason Fried episode, 2026-02-15, and *What I Learned Before I Sold to
Warren Buffett*) `PARAPHRASE`

**Success removes the people who would tell you the truth.** Designate someone
whose actual job is telling you what is wrong, and protect their standing when
they do. Sony hired a young vocal-arts critic, Norio Ohga, expressly to attack the
deficiencies in their products. He later became Sony's president. (David Senra
podcast, Daniel Ek episode, 2025-09-28) `PARAPHRASE`

**Wishful thinking is the named cause of most mistakes.** Keep asking whether a
thing is actually true. (David Senra podcast, 2026-03-24, recorded as a synthesis
rather than a direct quote) `PARAPHRASE`
The parallel: Vannevar Bush's maxim was that you cannot avoid a crash on a
slippery road by braking, closing your eyes, or letting go of the wheel. From
G. Pascal Zachary's *Endless Frontier: Vannevar Bush, Engineer of the American
Century*. `PARAPHRASE`

**The founder is usually the one who kills the company, not the market.** Across
four decades of watching companies come and go, Michael Dell attributes the deaths
to self-inflicted choices — overzealous expansion, design errors, failing to read
what competitors were doing. His related warning is that naivete plus confidence is
fine, but arrogance is the dangerous zone, because it makes you deaf to the small
warning voices. (Michael Dell, on the David Senra podcast, 2025-10-12)
`PARAPHRASE`

**Doubt is functional. Certainty is the tell.** Jason Fried's position is that
certainty in business is mostly self-persuasion, since outcomes hang on decisions,
timing, market conditions and mood. (Jason Fried, on the David Senra podcast,
2026-02-15) `PARAPHRASE`

**Everything going well is a finding about your questions.** Creative work at the
edge is full of failures and dead ends by design, so a stretch with zero setbacks
is evidence you have stopped taking real risk. From Brent Schlender and Rick
Tetzeli's *Becoming Steve Jobs*. `PARAPHRASE`

**Their incentive to be nice to you is measurable in advance.** Before any pitch,
write down what your counterparty loses if you succeed. Sam Walton offered Ben
Franklin's executives a concept requiring them to cut a 25% wholesale margin to
roughly 12%, and they blew up at him. From Vance Trimble's *Sam Walton: The Inside
Story of America's Richest Man*. `PARAPHRASE`
The corollary: if the person you are talking to loses nothing whether you succeed
or fail, their enthusiasm is free and you should price it that way.

**Silence is worse than complaint.** Tony Xu still works support every day,
because complaints are free observability. In his words, "the greatest killer of a
business is usually silence" — someone bothering to write in cares. (Tony Xu, on
the David Senra podcast, 2026-03-29) `QUOTABLE`

## Spare receipts

For rulings where nothing above fits cleanly.

- Amazon priced a competitor's own published list of 100 bestsellers. It took six
  hours and four superstores to find them all, at $1,561 against $1,195 at
  Amazon. From *Invent and Wander*. `PARAPHRASE`
- HP's first significant order came from Disney's chief sound engineer, who was
  about to pay $400 each for oscillators elsewhere. HP's cost under $100. From
  *The HP Way*. `PARAPHRASE`
- Told to abandon a display monitor, HP engineer Chuck House toured customers with
  a prototype instead and pushed it into production against Packard's own
  instruction. It sold over 17,000 units for $35M. From *The HP Way*. `PARAPHRASE`
- Estée Lauder broke into Saks with four products and an $800 order after being
  told no repeatedly. Before the debut she mailed her existing clientele and Saks
  charge-account holders an announcement card. Everyone she had ever sampled
  showed up, and they sold out in two days. From *Estée: A Success Story*.
  `PARAPHRASE`
- Estée Lauder relabeled a perfume as a bath oil so women could buy it for
  themselves without guilt. Roughly $50K in 1953 became over $150M by 1984. From
  *Estée: A Success Story*. `PARAPHRASE`
- To prove the Silver Ghost had no vibration, Rolls-Royce set a full glass of
  water on the running car at 1,600 rpm and not a drop spilled. Car commercials
  were still copying the demonstration a century later. From Peter Pugh's
  *Rolls-Royce: The Magic of a Name*. `PARAPHRASE`
- Raising Cane's cleared $30 in its first month. To Todd Graves that meant
  payroll, rent and vendors were covered, so the business worked. (Todd Graves, on
  the David Senra podcast, 2025-11-09) `PARAPHRASE`
- DoorDash's entire Y Combinator summer reduced to three questions: will consumers
  pay six dollars, will restaurants partner at 15%, and can we afford a wage that
  attracts drivers. (Tony Xu, on the David Senra podcast, 2026-03-29) `PARAPHRASE`

## What is not here

- **No conversion benchmark.** Nothing in this material says how many
  conversations equal validation. Do not invent one.
- **No kill threshold.** Nothing here says when to abandon an idea. Never tell a
  member to kill theirs.
- **No pre-sale, deposit or letter-of-intent template.** There are instances of
  prepayment in this material. There is no procedure for asking for one.
- **No research on leading questions.** The failure modes above are about founders
  deceiving themselves and about filtered information. Do not claim this material
  says leading questions cause anything.
