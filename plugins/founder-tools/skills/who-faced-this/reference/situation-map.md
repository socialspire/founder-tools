# Situation Map

The routing table for WHO FACED THIS. Twenty-five structural situations. Each one
carries the plain-language phrasings a member actually types, the precedent cards,
and the moves those cards support.

**This file is the source. Nothing outside it may be used.** Do not add a founder,
a number, a date, a book or an anecdote from general knowledge. If a card you want
is not here, drop it.

---

## How to read a card

Every card has the same six lines.

- **Situation** — the position they were actually in.
- **Did** — what they actually did. Not what they believed.
- **Happened** — the outcome, with the number if there is one.
- **Source** — attribution, and which rule applies.
- **Then vs now** — where the gap between their world and the member's world matters.
- **Move** — the action this card supports.

## Attribution rules, non-negotiable

Two classes. The card tells you which.

**PARAPHRASE** — book-sourced. Never put it in quotation marks, even where the
underlying book quotes someone. Name the book and its author as written on the card.

**PODCAST** — may be paraphrased, and must carry the speaker, the show name and the
episode date: `(Michael Dell, on the David Senra podcast, 2025-10-12)`. Direct
quotation is allowed **only** for the strings in the Quote Bank at the bottom of this
file. Everything else from a podcast source is summary prose about what the guest
said, not the guest's words. Quoting it would be fabricating a quote.

Never state a corpus size, an episode count, or the name of the library.

# SITUATION 1 — CONCENTRATION RISK

**Structural name:** one buyer holds enough of your revenue that their decision is
your decision.

**Routes from:** biggest client, one customer pays for everything, if this account
leaves I'm done, concentration, whale, should I take this huge account.

**1.1 Les Schwab**
- Situation: early on, his single largest customer owed him roughly his entire net worth.
- Did: drew the rule that concentration in one large account is an existential risk rather than a milestone, and set a ceiling on it.
- Happened: that customer's bankruptcy would have ended the company.
- Source: PARAPHRASE. Les Schwab tells this in *Les Schwab: Pride In Performance*.
- Move: calculate what percentage of your net worth your largest receivable represents, and set a hard ceiling.

**1.2 Jason Fried, 37signals**
- Situation: Basecamp could sell contracts far larger than it does.
- Did: capped the price at $299 a month no matter how many seats, and turns down would-be $50k-a-month enterprise deals.
- Happened: he wants customers like the dots of TV static, all the same size, so losing ten random ones changes nothing. Durability comes from many small things rather than a few whales.
- Source: PODCAST. (Jason Fried, on the David Senra podcast, 2026-02-15)
- Then vs now: software costs almost nothing to serve one more customer. A service business with delivery capacity cannot cap price the same way, but it can cap share.
- Move: cap your top price or your top account share so no single customer becomes load-bearing.

**1.3 Jason Fried, on who the buyer is**
- Situation: enterprise software is bought by someone who then makes other people use it.
- Did: sells to the people who actually use the product.
- Happened: he attributes the fact that those products are hated to the split between buyer and user; when the two are the same person, they demand things that work.
- Source: PODCAST. (Jason Fried, on the David Senra podcast, 2026-02-15)
- Move: check whether the person signing your invoice is the person using the thing.

**1.4 Steve Jobs at NeXT — the inverse**
- Situation: a $30M IBM deal, and a problem with duplicating slides.
- Did: refused to fly to Dallas over it.
- Happened: it read as lack of commitment and the deal died.
- Source: PARAPHRASE. From Geoffrey Cain's *Steve Jobs in Exile*.
- Move: show up personally for any relationship worth more than a quarter of your revenue.

**1.5 Phil Knight and Onitsuka**
- Situation: Nike's entire early business ran through one Japanese supplier.
- Did: when the supplier cut him off, he opened the meeting saying they had them right where they wanted them, and reframed the loss as independence day.
- Happened: the team had collapsed at the news. He stalled the supplier, scared off replacement distributors, and found a new source.
- Source: PARAPHRASE. Phil Knight tells this in *Shoe Dog*.
- Move: run his four questions on the scenario where they leave — what do I know, what else do I know, what does the future hold, what's the next step.

**1.6 John Mackey — the mirror**
- Situation: Whole Foods entering markets held by large chains.
- Did: took a few customers from many chains rather than many from one.
- Happened: no single competitor registered a threat worth answering.
- Source: PODCAST. (John Mackey, on the David Senra podcast, 2026-01-04)
- Move: spread your impact across incumbents instead of concentrating it on one.

# SITUATION 2 — PARTNERSHIP BREAKDOWN

**Structural name:** the agreement you signed is about to be read by someone who
wants something different from what you want.

**Routes from:** co-founder wants out, partner isn't pulling his weight, arguing about
equity, buyout, falling out, betrayed.

**2.1 Carnegie and Frick — accumulation**
- Situation: two decades of small slights, resignations and second-guessing between two men among the wealthiest and most capable alive.
- Did: nothing, until it was too big to address.
- Happened: nothing went from zero to a hundred. Pressure accumulated until both behaved pettily and irreversibly.
- Source: PARAPHRASE. From Les Standiford's *Meet You in Hell*.
- Move: address the small grievance now, while it is still small enough to name.

**2.2 Carnegie and Frick — the clause**
- Situation: the Ironclad agreement was drafted to protect the partners if Carnegie died.
- Did: Frick signed it without objection.
- Happened: its clause forcing any partner to sell at book value on a three-quarters vote became the exact instrument Carnegie used to expel him. Carnegie himself was never subject to it.
- Source: PARAPHRASE. From Les Standiford's *Meet You in Hell*.
- Move: read your operating agreement as your most hostile future partner would, and fix the asymmetries.

**2.3 Carnegie and Frick — the valuation**
- Situation: Carnegie blocked revaluation for years.
- Did: kept book value fixed at $50 million.
- Happened: the company was earning $21 million a year and was conservatively worth $200 to $250 million. It later sold for $480 million. Carnegie moved to pay $1.5 million for Frick's interest — a stake the settlement valued at $31 million, and which became over $70 million after the merger. The attempt bought him a lawsuit, public exposure of his profits, and a permanently severed relationship. Westinghouse and other entrepreneurs urged him to settle rather than let a trial publish his numbers. He settled.
- Source: PARAPHRASE. From Les Standiford's *Meet You in Hell*.
- Move: schedule an independent valuation on a fixed cadence and write it into the agreement. Before any punitive decision, calculate what winning costs you and what conceding costs you.

**2.4 Tamara Mellon at Jimmy Choo**
- Situation: a co-founder she wanted rid of. Her father proposed simply purchasing his shares.
- Did: instead, 51% went to a private equity firm.
- Happened: ten years watching owners she could not outvote degrade the product, treated like an employee at the company she founded.
- Source: PARAPHRASE. Tamara Mellon tells this in her memoir, *In My Shoes*.
- Move: put a buy-sell clause in your operating agreement now, before a dispute is live.

**2.5 Tamara Mellon — the earlier mistake**
- Situation: a creative partner who had no interest in designing.
- Did: discovered he only wanted to keep making bespoke shoes, and took the creative role herself.
- Happened: she carried the job she thought she had hired for.
- Source: PARAPHRASE. From *In My Shoes* by Tamara Mellon.
- Move: verify your co-founder actually wants to do the job, not the title.

**2.6 John Mackey on time horizon**
- Situation: two co-founders, different endpoints.
- Did: the partner who only wanted money quit mentally once store one was profitable. The partner who wanted to be everywhere in America stayed for decades. Later, a co-founder declared the company blown when new stores opened at a loss.
- Happened: those stores grew into profitability exactly as predicted.
- Source: PODCAST. (John Mackey, on the David Senra podcast, 2026-01-04)
- Move: ask each co-founder what the company looks like in twenty years, and act on the mismatch now rather than at the next loss.

**2.7 Michael Ovitz on equal equity**
- Situation: CAA's founding rules included equal equity for all partners.
- Did: years later he needed a larger share and asked for it.
- Happened: his partners agreed logically and resented it emotionally. He called it hitting the self-destruct button with a sixteen-year fuse.
- Source: PARAPHRASE. Michael Ovitz tells this in *Who Is Michael Ovitz?*
- Move: build a vesting or reallocation mechanism now, while everyone still likes each other.

**2.8 Les Schwab's buyout**
- Situation: two partners holding 20% each, pushed by family who wanted cash out.
- Did: bought them out for $225,000 and $300,000.
- Happened: those stakes would have been worth roughly $12 million each by 1985, plus annual bonuses over a million, against $1.8 billion in eventual sales.
- Source: PARAPHRASE. From *Les Schwab: Pride In Performance*.
- Move: write a buy-sell agreement now that fixes the mechanism and the notice period.

**2.9 James Dyson buying out Jeremy Fry**
- Situation: Fry held 49% and wanted out. He hated the Amway litigation and his financial adviser thought the vacuum was going nowhere.
- Did: Dyson bought the stake for £45,000.
- Happened: he has owned 100% of Dyson since. Fry's children have never forgiven him.
- Source: PODCAST. (James Dyson, on the David Senra podcast, 2025-12-07)
- Move: treat any chance to consolidate ownership cheaply as a decision about the next forty years.

**2.10 The counter-card — Carnegie and Frick again**
- Situation: Frick resigned over a labor disagreement rather than comply with Carnegie.
- Did: refused to be a puppet.
- Happened: Carnegie concluded he was no puppet, gave him a bigger stake and made him chairman. The partnership got closer.
- Source: PARAPHRASE. From Les Standiford's *Meet You in Hell*.
- Move: before you remove a partner who fights you, check whether the fighting is the value.

**2.11 Delivering it**
- Situation: Charles Schwab — Carnegie Steel's president, not Les Schwab of cards 1.1 and 2.8 — had to tell Henry Clay Frick, his former mentor, that he was out.
- Did: refused to ambush him at a board meeting or mail a letter, and went to tell him directly.
- Happened: this card records the manner of the delivery, not a reported result. Say it that way.
- Source: PARAPHRASE. From Les Standiford's *Meet You in Hell*.
- Move: deliver the worst news yourself, in person.
- **Name him Charles Schwab every time. Never let this card read as Les Schwab.**

# SITUATION 3 — PRICE ATTACK

**Structural name:** someone has moved the price and you are deciding whether to
follow them down.

**Routes from:** undercut, price war, cheaper competitor, race to the bottom,
should I lower my prices.

**3.1 Rose Blumkin in court**
- Situation: competitors sued her for unfair trade practices over carpet priced at $3.95 against their $7.95.
- Did: told the judge she sold at 10% over cost and asked what was wrong with that. She would also produce the supplier's bill to show customers her actual cost.
- Happened: the case was thrown out and the judge bought carpet from her the next day. The press from the trial was worth more than she could have bought.
- Source: PARAPHRASE. From Karen Linder's *The Women of Berkshire Hathaway*.
- Move: treat a competitor's complaint about your pricing as validation, not a problem to solve.

**3.2 Herb Kelleher's bottle of liquor**
- Situation: a giant rival cut fares to kill Southwest.
- Did: matched the price, explained publicly what was being done to them, and offered a bottle of liquor to anyone paying full fare.
- Happened: 76% of passengers chose the gift and the rival abandoned the route.
- Source: PARAPHRASE. From Kevin and Jackie Freiberg's *Nuts!*
- Move: explain your actual competitive situation plainly to customers, and let them choose a side.

**3.3 Herb Kelleher's reframe**
- Situation: rivals charged $62 on a route where Southwest charged $15.
- Did: understood he was competing with cars and buses rather than with airlines, and priced against that.
- Happened: the price war became winnable because he was not in the same one.
- Source: PARAPHRASE. From *Nuts!* by Kevin and Jackie Freiberg.
- Move: write down the non-obvious alternative your customer actually chooses instead of you, and price against that.

**3.4 John Mackey refusing the axis**
- Situation: supermarkets answering Walmart.
- Did: they cut lighting, capital, labor and service. Mackey refused price entirely and took quality, service and product mix.
- Happened: the ones playing Walmart's game still lost.
- Source: PODCAST. (John Mackey, on the David Senra podcast, 2026-01-04)
- Move: list what your biggest competitor cannot do without contradicting itself, and build there.

**3.5 Andrew Carnegie knowing his cost**
- Situation: rivals charged $70 a ton for rails.
- Did: charged $65, because he could produce for under $50. He imported freight-hauling cost accounting into manufacturing.
- Happened: he was the only firm in the field with that discipline, so every undercut was a known margin rather than a hope. His dictum was to watch the costs and let the profits take care of themselves.
- Source: PARAPHRASE. From Les Standiford's *Meet You in Hell*.
- Move: build unit-level cost accounting before you build a sales team.

**3.6 Todd Graves refusing the cut**
- Situation: finance-side proposals to shave costs.
- Did: turned them down repeatedly, on the grounds that small cuts compound into a product nobody craves. He argues sales cure all woes: staffing shifts properly and buying the best inputs raises volume and repeat business.
- Happened: Raising Cane's runs second in average unit volume in quick service, with some competitors doing a third of its per-restaurant sales.
- Source: PODCAST. (Todd Graves, on the David Senra podcast, 2025-11-09)
- Move: kill the labor or ingredient cut that would protect margin at the cost of volume.

**3.7 Sol Price on the backlash**
- Situation: charging less than the industry tolerated.
- Did: cut prices anyway.
- Happened: California sued him over a 12% liquor markup. His first pharmacist faced expulsion from professional bodies, blocked wholesalers and death threats.
- Source: PARAPHRASE. From Robert Price's *Sol Price: Retail Revolutionary and Social Innovator*.
- Move: budget legal and PR resources for the backlash before you cut prices.

# SITUATION 4 — THE DEAL THAT COSTS YOU YOUR NAME

**Structural name:** a large buyer will pay you, on the condition that what you build
stops being yours.

**Routes from:** exclusivity, white label, their brand, drop my name, biggest order
of my life but the terms are bad.

**4.1 Akio Morita and Bulova**
- Situation: Bulova offered 100,000 radios — several times Sony's entire capitalization — on the condition the radios carried Bulova's brand.
- Did: refused. His answer was that fifty years ago Bulova's name was as unknown as his was then.
- Happened: he records that turning down Bulova, refusing OEM work, and spending on marketing during lean quarters were each obvious once the destination was fixed.
- Source: PARAPHRASE. Akio Morita tells this in *Made in Japan*.
- Move: decline the next revenue that requires stripping your name off the product.

**4.2 Ralph Lauren and Bloomingdale's**
- Situation: the order that would have changed his life, on one condition — his label off, their house label on, and an inch off the tie. He was broke, newly married, sleeping on a mattress on the floor.
- Did: packed up and walked out.
- Happened: he kept the brand. He also would not put his name on a lobby directory of hundreds of manufacturers.
- Source: PARAPHRASE. From Jeffrey A. Trachtenberg's *Ralph Lauren: The Man Behind the Mystique*.
- Move: turn down any deal that grows a brand you do not own, no matter how badly you need the revenue.

**4.3 Ralph Lauren guarding exclusivity**
- Situation: first year, almost no cash, and one of the fastest-growing specialty chains wanting to stock him.
- Did: turned it away, because its customers were the wrong image and the placement would have killed his exclusivity.
- Happened: he held the position.
- Source: PARAPHRASE. From *Ralph Lauren: The Man Behind the Mystique* by Jeffrey A. Trachtenberg.
- Move: guard exclusivity harder than volume.

**4.4 Estée Lauder taking the bad slot — the counter-card**
- Situation: Harrods offered a tiny slot buried with general toiletries, not next to the good cosmetics.
- Did: took it, then used the sales to negotiate up. Separately, told the cosmetics category was locked up by exclusives, she pitched bath oil instead and offered it on consignment — if it does not sell, you have lost nothing.
- Happened: she earned her way to better terms. The compromise she accepted was placement, not identity.
- Source: PARAPHRASE. Estée Lauder tells this in her memoir, *Estée: A Success Story*.
- Move: accept the worst version of the distribution deal to get in the door, but never the version that takes your name.

**4.5 Steve Stoute on perpetual rights**
- Situation: record deals that owned the artist's name and likeness outright.
- Did: he puts it to technology people as imagining a seed investor owning your intellectual property outright.
- Happened: Prince wrote "slave" on his face because Warner owned his name and likeness. Stoute's point is that for decades you needed a label to find an audience, and now that artists find audiences first, the justification for signing away rights is gone.
- Source: PODCAST. (Steve Stoute, on the David Senra podcast, 2026-06-21)
- Move: never trade name, image, likeness or IP in perpetuity for an advance. Build the audience before you negotiate with the gatekeeper.

**4.6 Dana White paying for his own distribution**
- Situation: the network passed on the reality show.
- Did: offered to fund the entire production and let them simply air it. It was explicitly the last investment his backers would make.
- Happened: it reduced the network to pure distribution with no downside, and left the company owning everything. He calls paying that bill the greatest thing that ever happened to them, because a network-funded show would have left them owning nothing.
- Source: PODCAST. (Dana White, on the David Senra podcast, 2026-05-10)
- Move: when a distributor hesitates, offer to carry the production cost in exchange for keeping the rights.

**4.7 Evan Spiegel's inversion list**
- Situation: acquisition interest in Snapchat.
- Did: noted that nearly every choice the company made was the inverse of the industry — ephemeral instead of permanent, private instead of public, no likes, camera-first instead of feed-first, AR instead of VR.
- Happened: an acquirer would have reverted all of them.
- Source: PODCAST. (Evan Spiegel, on the David Senra podcast, 2026-04-12)
- Move: write down which of your contrarian decisions the buyer would undo. That list is the real price.

# SITUATION 5 — CASH CRISIS

**Structural name:** the money runs out on a date you can name.

**Routes from:** can't make payroll, three weeks from empty, cash going the wrong
way, need money now, should I take a loan.

**5.1 Rose Blumkin's three-day sale**
- Situation: unable to pay her bills in 1950.
- Did: borrowed $50,000 privately, rented the Omaha City Auditorium for $200 a day, and ran a three-day sale.
- Happened: took in $250,000, repaid everything, and never borrowed again.
- Source: PARAPHRASE. From Karen Linder's *The Women of Berkshire Hathaway*.
- Move: design a single high-intensity sales event that could clear your worst liability in one week.

**5.2 Phil Knight selling forward**
- Situation: facing bankruptcy.
- Did: offered large retailers up to 7% discounts for non-refundable orders placed six months ahead. When paychecks bounced, he asked his box supplier — for whom Nike was most of the business — to front cash.
- Happened: it bought lead time, certainty, cash balances and more credit against those contracts. Separately, an employee and his parents forced $8,000, their entire life savings, on him during the worst period, refusing interest or paperwork. At IPO it was worth $1.6 million.
- Source: PARAPHRASE. Phil Knight tells this in *Shoe Dog*.
- Move: offer your biggest customers a discount in exchange for a binding advance commitment, then finance against it.

**5.3 Ralph Lauren's four moves**
- Situation: won a top design award on a Thursday and could not make payroll on Friday. Suppliers held 400 dozen shirts hostage.
- Did: pledged his personal $100,000 to the company, and executed the four moves a creditor named Dave Goldberg gave him — license out the cash-draining womenswear line, find more capital, defer $500,000 owed to a key supplier, renegotiate the Hilton buyout notes. He also asked the president of Bloomingdale's to advance him money on fall merchandise in June.
- Happened: he did all four and the finances reversed inside two years.
- Source: PARAPHRASE. From Jeffrey A. Trachtenberg's *Ralph Lauren: The Man Behind the Mystique*.
- Move: reduce your crisis to four specific executable moves and stop looking for one fix.

**5.4 Michael Dell's working capital**
- Situation: no capital available, at roughly $60M of revenue with almost nothing in the bank. Bankers had no reason to trust a 21-year-old.
- Did: compressed inventory, collected from customers faster, paid suppliers later. Lee Walker reframed the financing ask around the receivables — the question is whether blue-chip customers will pay, not whether the kid is trustworthy.
- Happened: competitors carried roughly 90 days of inventory across distributors and dealers. Dell got to about five. A negative cash conversion cycle means growth throws off cash, so you need far less outside money.
- Source: PODCAST. (Michael Dell, on the David Senra podcast, 2025-10-12)
- Move: reframe your financing ask around the strongest asset on your balance sheet.

**5.5 Tony Xu refunding everyone**
- Situation: month three, a Stanford football game spiked orders, every delivery ran an hour late, and they could not even shut the site off. The bank account was two to three weeks from empty.
- Did: within about fifteen seconds decided to refund everyone, then stayed up baking cookies and delivered them by 5 a.m.
- Happened: nobody had requested a refund. It cost roughly 40% of the account. Through the hard years he presented everything at all-hands including the cash line sloping toward zero.
- Source: PODCAST. (Tony Xu, on the David Senra podcast, 2026-03-29)
- Quotable: "the greatest killer of a business is usually silence" (Tony Xu, on the David Senra podcast, 2026-03-29)
- Move: refund your next service failure unprompted, and compute what that policy costs at current volume.

**5.6 Samuel Insull — the cautionary card**
- Situation: he had survived several financial panics.
- Did: his habit from early on was never pay cash when you could give a note. His companies borrowed money they never intended to repay except by perpetually rolling it over. Assuming this panic was ordinary, he expanded into it — $200M of new capital spending in 1930 alone.
- Happened: when credit vanished, only cash would settle the maturities. Leverage alone took him from $150M to less than zero. He died with empty pockets in a Paris subway station, unidentified for hours.
- Source: PARAPHRASE. From Forrest McDonald's *Insull*.
- Move: stress-test every obligation against a scenario where no one will refinance you.

# SITUATION 6 — NOBODY IS BUYING

**Structural name:** you built it, you shipped it, and the market did not respond.

**Routes from:** nobody's buying, launched and nothing happened, built the wrong
thing, should I pivot, no product-market fit.

**6.1 Adam Foroughi keeping the signal**
- Situation: an app-of-apps with essentially no users.
- Did: noticed the response rate to its recommendation pushes was extraordinary. Discarded the app, kept the recommendation algorithm, and shipped it as an advertising platform inside other people's apps in March 2012.
- Happened: that became AppLovin.
- Source: PODCAST. (Adam Foroughi, on the David Senra podcast, 2026-05-03)
- Move: look through a failing product for the single metric that outperformed, and rebuild around that alone.

**6.2 Adam Foroughi on being the wrong founder**
- Situation: after two advertising exits, he wanted direct-to-consumer.
- Did: built a dating app while married, and a fashion app while owning about four outfits.
- Happened: both failed. The markets were real; the teams were wrong for the products.
- Source: PODCAST. (Adam Foroughi, on the David Senra podcast, 2026-05-03)
- Move: before entering a consumer category, be honest about whether you are a credible user of the product.

**6.3 Akio Morita's tape recorder**
- Situation: the tape recorder failed with the public. Too expensive, read as a toy.
- Did: sold it to the Japanese Supreme Court, which had a stenographer shortage.
- Happened: it sold instantly. Same machine, different buyer. The category shifted the moment buyers saw a use rather than a novelty.
- Source: PARAPHRASE. Akio Morita tells this in *Made in Japan*.
- Move: list who has an expensive, urgent problem your product solves, and sell to them first.

**6.4 David Baszucki's placeholder**
- Situation: they already knew intuitively that the viral product was multiplayer, cloud, physically simulated, user-created 3D. They shipped a single-player puzzle-builder first, to buy runway.
- Did: within a couple of weeks it was obvious it was not going viral. They took a deep breath and spent six more months engineering the real thing.
- Happened: that became Roblox.
- Source: PODCAST. (David Baszucki, on the David Senra podcast, 2026-04-26)
- Move: give your placeholder product a two-week verdict date, and pre-commit to the rebuild.

**6.5 Evan Spiegel's first venture**
- Situation: a college-admissions tool. Eighteen months building a full-featured product.
- Did: lost to a competitor that had locked up the college counselors. He had no scalable path into users' hands.
- Happened: after that failure his criteria became: buildable quickly, and used by us with our friends.
- Source: PODCAST. (Evan Spiegel, on the David Senra podcast, 2026-04-12)
- Move: identify your distribution channel before writing the first line of code, and reject ideas that lack one.

**6.6 Steve Jobs at NeXT**
- Situation: advisors said universities could pay $3,000.
- Did: shipped at $10,500 — more than a year of private tuition — and clung to hardware for years while everyone around him could see the business was software.
- Happened: the pivot came only when bankruptcy left no alternative. The software business turned the first profit after nine years of losses.
- Source: PARAPHRASE. From Geoffrey Cain's *Steve Jobs in Exile*.
- Move: get your target price from buyers before you design, and treat it as a hard engineering constraint. Then ask what your customers are actually buying, and be willing to kill the part you are most attached to.

**6.7 James Dyson killing the car**
- Situation: he judged the industry forecast of 2% electric cars by 2030 to be plainly wrong, and he was right.
- Did: stopped anyway, about $750 million in. At low volume every component cost him roughly 30% more, while rivals could sell EVs at a loss to average down fleet emissions and keep selling profitable SUVs.
- Happened: asked what the car taught him, he says plainly: nothing. He calls it an awful thing to have done and refuses the consoling narrative.
- Source: PODCAST. (James Dyson, on the David Senra podcast, 2025-12-07)
- Move: stop laundering dead projects into learnings. Being right about the trend does not pay for being wrong about the unit economics.

# SITUATION 7 — YOU GOT COPIED

**Structural name:** a larger party has decided your idea is worth taking.

**Routes from:** copied, cloned, stole my idea, they launched the same thing,
knockoff, should I sue.

**7.1 Evan Spiegel and Poke**
- Situation: Facebook cloned Snapchat as Poke and promoted it from the top of the main app.
- Did: shipped faster and marketed harder rather than retreating.
- Happened: the clone died and Snapchat hit number one on Christmas Day. He describes the Poke launch as the best Christmas present he ever got, because the incumbent taught the whole market what the category was and sent users to the original.
- Source: PODCAST. (Evan Spiegel, on the David Senra podcast, 2026-04-12)
- Move: when a larger rival copies you, ship faster and market harder. They have just validated your category.

**7.2 Evan Spiegel on what cannot be copied**
- Situation: there is no moat in software, because copying is free.
- Did: invested only in what resists copying — communication network effects, a creator content ecosystem, an AR developer platform.
- Happened: ecosystems of people, not features.
- Source: PODCAST. (Evan Spiegel, on the David Senra podcast, 2026-04-12)
- Move: name the thing about your business that a competent copy would still not have.

**7.3 James Dyson refusing to sue**
- Situation: an ex-employee copied the Ballbarrow in America.
- Did: Dyson wanted to let him pave the way and then sell the original against him. His partners wanted vengeance.
- Happened: they spent heavily to no effect. Munger's line, which Senra relays: you cannot do a good deal with a bad person, so do not sue, move on.
- Source: PODCAST. (James Dyson, on the David Senra podcast, 2025-12-07)
- Then vs now: Dyson had a product he could keep improving. If the copy is of a contract or a trademark rather than a product, this card does not apply.
- Move: answer imitation with a better product, not a filing.

**7.4 Walt Disney answering a raid**
- Situation: Oswald's success brought bigger, better-funded studios in to steal his staff.
- Did: could not outbid them, so he invented a new character and forced the pace — sixteen drawings per movement, around 14,400 drawings for a ten-minute short.
- Happened: the new character was Mickey.
- Source: PARAPHRASE. From Paul Johnson's *Creators*.
- Move: when a rival copies you or poaches your people, respond by launching something they do not have yet.

**7.5 Gustav Söderström counter-positioning against Apple**
- Situation: Spotify against Apple. He confirms internal Apple messaging gave Spotify roughly six months.
- Did: picked three counter-positions, each traced to an Apple value rather than a temporary gap — freemium (betting Apple would never execute advertising well, given how much control it demands), personalization (betting a company philosophically resistant to data would never get good at recommendation), and ubiquity (betting Apple would always favor its own hardware and stay weak on Android and Samsung TVs).
- Happened: he assesses all three as having held. He is candid that people inside were frightened, that the period was brutal, and that survival came from structural reasons plus effort, not a clever maneuver.
- Source: PODCAST. (Gustav Söderström, on the David Senra podcast, 2026-06-07)
- Quotable: "My principle is just always be first." (Gustav Söderström, on the David Senra podcast, 2026-06-07)
- Move: list the things your largest competitor cannot do without contradicting its own identity, and make those your roadmap.

**7.6 John Mackey on retail having no patents**
- Situation: anyone can copy the product mix, the marketing, the service level, or hire away the people holding the intellectual capital.
- Did: competed on scale and speed of evolution.
- Happened: he names those as the only two defenses.
- Source: PODCAST. (John Mackey, on the David Senra podcast, 2026-01-04)
- Move: assume your model is copyable within a year and compete on execution velocity.

**7.7 Travis Kalanick on the copy lag**
- Situation: Didi copying Uber.
- Did: he describes the copying as almost an art form, executed with speed and ferocity, and frames the competitors as pure execution with nothing new created.
- Happened: the window Uber had was the time it took them to copy.
- Source: PODCAST. (Travis Kalanick, on the David Senra podcast, 2026-08-16)
- Move: assume your best idea will be copied competently, and plan what you ship during the copy lag.

**8.6 Lulu Cheng Meservey and Anduril's first year**

**8.7 Honda's Fujisawa building a channel from nothing**

- Happened: roughly 5,000 dealers almost overnight.
- Source: PARAPHRASE. From Sol Sanders' *Honda: The Man and His Machines*.
- Move: write directly to every plausible distributor with an offer to make them capable, not just an offer to sell.

# SITUATION 9 — FUNDING REFUSAL

**Structural name:** the people who allocate capital have priced you at zero. That is
a statement about their model, not necessarily about yours.

**Routes from:** every VC said no, the bank won't lend, I can't raise, nobody will
fund this.

- Situation: pitched a million dollars at a four-million pre-money — a quarter of the company. It was his third advertising business, with a decade of prior success.

- Move: write down what you would be forced to do well if the round never arrived, and do that instead.

**9.2 James Dyson and the Lloyds banker**
- Situation: venture investors told him the category was uninteresting and that they were funding fast-food restaurants, or that they might back it if he brought in an industry person to run it.
- Did: went to a clearing bank and put his house up again.
- Happened: £600,000 for tooling. The Lloyds roving business expert who won him the loan had gone home and asked his wife and mother about a bagless vacuum, and had noticed Dyson survived a five-year American lawsuit. He took that fight as proof of determination and escalated internally, during a housing crisis, after the bank had already refused.

**9.3 Todd Graves earning his own capital**

He later added In-N-Out to his pitch as living proof that single-product focus works.
- Happened: he funded the first version himself and eventually landed an SBA loan.
- Source: PODCAST. (Todd Graves, on the David Senra podcast, 2025-11-09)

**9.4 Phil Knight and the trading company**
- Situation: he kept the bank account near zero for a decade, plowing every dollar into more inventory.
- Did: got thrown out of bank after bank rather than order less.
- Happened: banks kicked him out for living on zero cash balances. The Japanese trading company reviewed the same books and concluded there are worse things than ambition.
- Source: PARAPHRASE. Phil Knight tells this in *Shoe Dog*.
- Move: take capital from people who recognize growth mechanics, and stop pitching those who score you on cash balances.

**9.5 Brian Armstrong leading with the graph**
- Situation: raising for Coinbase.
- Did: no deck. A daily-buy chart.
- Happened: one week, roughly $25M in — driven by a bank warning that one error would make them insolvent.
- Source: PODCAST. (Brian Armstrong, on the David Senra podcast, 2026-03-01)
- Move: find the one chart that proves demand, and lead with nothing else.

**9.6 John Mackey funding on proof points**
- Situation: a college dropout and his girlfriend, six months of retail experience, no business coursework.
- Did: read about the handful of existing natural food supermarkets in a trade publication and traveled to study them, so he could tell investors the model already worked in LA, Boston and San Diego.
- Happened: friends and family funded him. He suspects he could not have raised without those proof points.
- Source: PODCAST. (John Mackey, on the David Senra podcast, 2026-01-04)
- Move: collect working examples of your model elsewhere and lead with them.

# SITUATION 10 — DEMAND IS OUTRUNNING DELIVERY

**Structural name:** you are creating problems faster than you are solving them.

**Routes from:** can't keep up with demand, quality is slipping, growing too fast,
I'm the bottleneck, everything's on fire.

**10.1 Travis Kalanick's equation**
- Situation: Uber creating new problems in every new city.
- Did: states it as an equation — the rate of problem-solving must stay greater than or equal to the rate of problem creation. When it inverts, you are drowning, and the only move is to halt new problem creation until solving catches up.
- Happened: he adds that management capacity is the only real constraint on imagination, and that problems you create today come ashore in six months. Creating a problem is easy; understanding the shape of the problem-solving it will demand is the hard part.
- Source: PODCAST. (Travis Kalanick, on the David Senra podcast, 2026-08-16)
- Move: before committing to a new market or product line, write down the problems it will create and name who solves each one.

**10.2 Travis Kalanick's playbook**
- Situation: eight to ten hours of calls to launch one city.
- Did: never solved the same problem twice. Every problem solved on early city-launch calls went into the playbook.
- Happened: by city twenty the pricing call took five minutes and he stopped attending. A launch became a non-event.
- Source: PODCAST. (Travis Kalanick, on the David Senra podcast, 2026-08-16)
- Move: write down the fix the first time you make it, so the second occurrence costs you nothing.

**10.3 Todd Graves on latency**
- Situation: franchised to fund faster growth, with good, trusted operators.
- Did: found they ran at roughly 85 out of 100 against his 95. Persuading independent owners to adopt a tested improvement took months; his own restaurants adopted it overnight after a five-store test. He bought the franchisees back.
- Happened: sales and wages went up in every reacquired market.
- Source: PODCAST. (Todd Graves, on the David Senra podcast, 2025-11-09)
- Quotable: "Never sacrifice quality for speed." (Todd Graves, on the David Senra podcast, 2025-11-09)
- Move: time how long a system-wide change takes in your business, and treat that latency as a strategic cost.

**10.4 Todd Graves on handing work over**
- Situation: delegating when you operate at 95 out of 100 and a strong hire arrives at 85.
- Did: supplements until they reach 95, then eases off but stays informed enough to add value.
- Happened: he names the 95-to-85 gap as the reason you cannot simply hand it over.
- Source: PODCAST. (Todd Graves, on the David Senra podcast, 2025-11-09)
- Move: pick the one thing to hand off, and stay in it until the hire matches your standard rather than dropping it on them.

**10.5 James Dyson's channel switch**
- Situation: a hired sales manager talked them out of direct selling into wholesalers.
- Did: switched. Margins halved, the business went cash-negative, and word-of-mouth durability was lost. The board's answer to losing money on every sale was to expand, on borrowed money at 25% interest.
- Happened: they had to sell a third of the company.
- Source: PARAPHRASE. James Dyson tells this in *Against the Odds*.
- Move: fix unit economics before funding growth, and refuse debt that assumes a turnaround.

**10.6 Ralph Lauren's invisible loss**
- Situation: sales tripled from $3.8M to $8M and he believed he was profitable.
- Did: ran with no systems for accounting, credit, returns, or even sick days.
- Happened: he had actually lost about $45,000.
- Source: PARAPHRASE. From Jeffrey A. Trachtenberg's *Ralph Lauren: The Man Behind the Mystique*.
- Move: install a weekly cash and margin report before you chase the next revenue jump.

**10.7 Evan Spiegel killing bespoke work**
- Situation: Snap's engineers spent enormous time on custom integrations and measurement for very large advertisers.
- Did: cut it back.
- Happened: they are now doing far less bespoke work and serving many customers at scale instead.
- Source: PODCAST. (Evan Spiegel, on the David Senra podcast, 2026-04-12)
- Move: replace your most-requested custom deliverable with a standard product customers adapt to.

**10.8 Steve Jobs' NeXT factory**
- Situation: a self-run factory against a 16,000-unit annual target.
- Did: produced eight machines a day. Magnesium casing plus matte black paint meant machines failed the paint test and never shipped.
- Happened: the target was never reachable from that setup.
- Source: PARAPHRASE. From Geoffrey Cain's *Steve Jobs in Exile*.
- Move: outsource anything you cannot yet run at the scale your plan requires.

# SITUATION 11 — NO SIGNAL

# SITUATION 13 — SUPPLIER FAILURE

**Structural name:** an input you do not control has been withdrawn or repriced.

**Routes from:** my supplier ghosted me, my manufacturer raised prices, I got cut off,
my vendor is holding my inventory.

**13.1 Ingvar Kamprad and the boycott**
- Situation: IKEA's trade association organized a boycott to cut off suppliers, and banned the company from displaying prices at fairs.
- Did: when suppliers could not sell IKEA the same items, small design alterations made the products technically different. The domestic boycott pushed IKEA to find suppliers in Poland.
- Happened: the accident produced a proprietary style competitors could not match, and opened an entirely new supply base. Loyal suppliers ran night deliveries and stripped logos from vans to keep serving IKEA. Kamprad called it a crisis that became a non-crisis.
- Source: PARAPHRASE. From *Leading By Design: The IKEA Story* by Ingvar Kamprad and Bertil Torekull.
- Move: take your most severe current constraint and write down three capabilities it could force you to build. Qualify one supplier outside your usual geography before you need them.

**13.2 Ingvar Kamprad's preventive habit**
- Situation: supplier relationships in an industry that paid slowly.
- Did: paid suppliers within ten days while competitors took three or four months.
- Happened: when the boycott came, loyal suppliers ran night deliveries and stripped the logos from their vans to keep serving IKEA.
- Source: PARAPHRASE. From *Leading By Design: The IKEA Story*.
- Move: pay the supplier you cannot afford to lose faster than anyone else does.

**13.3 Les Schwab and the rubber companies**
- Situation: the American rubber companies gave better pricing to their own stores.
- Did: rather than absorb it, found Toyo Tire in Japan just as Japanese tires were going from zero market share to dominance. In 1966 he went fully independent — best quality at the lowest price, loyal to no manufacturer, which had never been tried in his industry.
- Happened: he rode that wave for decades.
- Source: PARAPHRASE. From *Les Schwab: Pride In Performance*.
- Move: map which of your suppliers has an interest in your failure, and start qualifying a replacement now.

**13.4 Phil Knight and Onitsuka**
- Situation: his only supplier cut him off.
- Did: reframed it as independence day, then stalled the supplier, scared off replacement distributors, and found a new source. He also mapped which vendors would fail without Nike, and treated the box supplier as a financing option in a crunch.
- Happened: Nike became its own brand.
- Source: PARAPHRASE. Phil Knight tells this in *Shoe Dog*.
- Move: work out which of your suppliers needs you more than you need them, and use that before you need to.

**13.5 Sol Price private-labelling around the block**
- Situation: fair-trade laws blocked price cuts on national brands.
- Did: built the FM private label at fedmart — detergent at half the national brand's price.
- Happened: that instinct is now Kirkland Signature.
- Source: PARAPHRASE. From Robert Price's *Sol Price: Retail Revolutionary and Social Innovator*.
- Move: private-label the category where your suppliers most restrict your pricing.

**13.6 Honda fixing terms instead of collections**
- Situation: Honda gave away machines on credit and never collected.
- Did: Fujisawa built the distribution network and made dealers pay upfront.
- Happened: the solution to unpaid dealers was to require payment up front, not to collect harder.
- Source: PARAPHRASE. From Sol Sanders' *Honda: The Man and His Machines*.
- Move: change your payment terms rather than adding collections effort.

- Did: insists it was a hugely enjoyable struggle because he had a genuine aim, and describes going in each day excited to see whether the next experiment was better.
- Happened: Senra records finding Dyson's book two years into a five-and-a-half-year stretch with no success, and says it kept him going.
- Source: PODCAST. (James Dyson, on the David Senra podcast, 2025-12-07)
- Move: if the daily process is not interesting to you, change the problem rather than your willpower.

**14.3 Ray Kroc's long night**
- Situation: started McDonald's at 52 and was called an overnight success.
- Did: answered that thirty years is a long long night.
- Happened: this card records his answer, not an outcome. Say it that way.
- Source: PARAPHRASE. Ray Kroc tells this in *Grinding It Out*.
- Move: stop treating your age or years in the wilderness as disqualifying, and count the accumulated skills that make you dangerous now.

**14.4 Walt Disney's valleys**
- Situation: world-famous in his thirties, and still struggling badly through his forties. Disneyland came 35 years into his career.
- Did: kept working at the same relentlessness.
- Happened: it produced multiple nervous breakdowns and years of depression.
- Source: PARAPHRASE. From Neal Gabler's *Walt Disney: The Triumph of the American Imagination*.
- Move: when you hit a low, treat it as the expected shape of the work rather than evidence you are wrong.

**14.5 Bruce Springsteen with nowhere to go**
- Situation: his label assumed he would quit and go back to a day job. Voted out of his first band for having a cheap guitar.
- Did: went home and taught himself a lead solo overnight, then spent years playing trailer parks, ice rinks, supermarket openings and drive-ins — anywhere that would let a five-piece band play cheap.
- Happened: he had no day job, no other skills, and nowhere to go, so they could not get rid of him.
- Source: PARAPHRASE. Bruce Springsteen tells this in *Born to Run*.
- Move: convert the next rejection into one specific skill you will acquire within thirty days.

**14.6 Todd Graves on the multiplier**
- Situation: advice to young founders.
- Did: imagine how hard starting will be, multiply by infinity, and if you are still in, you will succeed. Early on the restaurant closed at 3:30 AM and opened at 8 AM; he slept in an apartment behind it in roughly three-hour blocks.
- Happened: Cane's cleared $30 its first month. To Graves that meant payroll, rent and vendors were covered, so the business worked. He says most good ideas die because someone stopped, not because someone lost.
- Source: PODCAST. (Todd Graves, on the David Senra podcast, 2025-11-09)
- Move: define viability as covering obligations, and stop measuring early revenue against ambition.

**14.7 Arnold Schwarzenegger after a loss**
- Situation: came second.
- Did: made himself analyze exactly why, admitted his weaknesses, and spent a full year on things he had previously ignored entirely. After a loss in America he concluded the failure was in his preparation and vision, not his body. Before one contest he went down the list of rivals deciding he could not beat any of them until he reached eighth or ninth place, and calls that a loser's way of looking at it.
- Happened: he names the post-mortem as the mechanism.
- Source: PARAPHRASE. Arnold Schwarzenegger tells this in *Arnold: The Education of a Bodybuilder*.
- Move: after your next loss, write the honest post-mortem within 48 hours and commit a fixed block to the weakest input.

**14.8 The counterweight — Phil Knight on quitting**
- Situation: advice to entrepreneurs about never giving up.
- Did: calls the people who say it charlatans, and says giving up does not mean stopping.
- Happened: this card records a rule he states, not an outcome. Say it that way. Internal note, never write this line into the output: it is the stated limit on every persistence card above.
- Source: PARAPHRASE. Phil Knight tells this in *Shoe Dog*.
- Move: set a kill criterion for your current bet now, while you are still rational about it.

**15.3 Sol Price losing to his own distraction**

- Happened: the strategy went reactive and he got outrun.

- Happened: the firms that diversified later spun off the subsidiaries that had weakened the original product.
- Source: PARAPHRASE. Estée Lauder tells this in her memoir, *Estée: A Success Story*.

**15.5 Daniel Ek's decade test**

- Happened: he names that as the filter.
- Source: PODCAST. (Daniel Ek, on the David Senra podcast, 2025-09-28)
- Move: apply the decade test to your current roadmap and cut what fails it.

**15.6 Michael Dell's theory loop**
- Situation: deciding where to put weight.

- Happened: he names it as the operating loop.
- Source: PODCAST. (Michael Dell, on the David Senra podcast, 2025-10-12)

# SITUATION 16 — PUBLIC FAILURE

**Structural name:** something about you is now in front of people who did not choose
to look.

**Routes from:** bad review, someone posted about me, got called out, my launch
flopped in public, I embarrassed myself.

**Coverage warning: strong on institutional public failure, weak on the small-scale
version — a bad review, a client badmouthing you, a post that went wrong. Say so.**

**16.1 Henry Clay Frick doubling down — the failure case**
- Situation: after the Homestead catastrophe, Frick was shot in the neck.
- Did: refused anesthetic, had the wounds sewn shut, finished the letter he had been writing, and on his way to the ambulance told reporters the company's policy toward the union would not change and would win. He returned to work thirteen days later, having buried an infant son in between.
- Happened: the physical courage was extraordinary. The reputational damage was terminal, and it began the end of the partnership. Carnegie's carefully constructed image as the workingman's friend could not survive what was done in his company's name.
- Source: PARAPHRASE. From Les Standiford's *Meet You in Hell*.
- Move: after a public failure, change the policy that produced it instead of restating it. Test whether your public positioning survives a reporter watching your actual operations for a week.

**16.2 Brian Armstrong after the hit piece**
- Situation: coverage he considered inaccurate and uncorrected.
- Did: stopped routing information through intermediaries and published his position on his own channel.
- Happened: his claim is that once the hit piece lands and nothing catastrophic follows, you stop optimizing for optics and start doing what you think is right.
- Source: PODCAST. (Brian Armstrong, on the David Senra podcast, 2026-03-01)
- Move: write down what you would do differently if the worst public story about you had already run. Then pick one owned channel and publish your position there first, every time.

**16.3 Dana White naming a bad night**
- Situation: a night's product disappointed the audience.
- Did: said so, rather than insult the audience's own eyes. He traces it back to promoters who told fans they had just seen something great when they plainly had not.
- Happened: his filter on criticism is whether the critic has ever built or risked anything, or ever had anyone depend on them for a paycheck. The reading that this candour is the source of his following is David Senra's, not White's — attribute it to Senra if you use it.
- Source: PODCAST. (Dana White, on the David Senra podcast, 2026-05-10)
- Move: name your last shipped failure out loud before a customer has to.

**16.4 Rose Blumkin letting the attack run**
- Situation: an unfair-trade trial brought by competitors over carpet priced at $3.95 against their $7.95.
- Did: went to court and told the judge she sold at 10% over cost, and asked what was wrong with that.
- Happened: the case was thrown out, the judge bought carpet from her the next day, and the press from the trial was worth more than she could have bought. Do not write that she chose to let it run — the record shows she fought it and won, not that she declined a settlement.
- Source: PARAPHRASE. From Karen Linder's *The Women of Berkshire Hathaway*.
- Move: let a public attack run rather than settling it quietly, when the facts favor you.

**16.5 Dietrich Mateschitz feeding the rumors**
- Situation: tales spread that Red Bull's taurine came from bull testicles, or that it was spiked with amphetamines.
- Did: let the gossip run unchecked and posted a page of the rumors on the company's own website.
- Happened: bans in several European countries created a black market smuggling cans from Austria into Germany. In the first three months of legal sale, Germany bought 33 million cans.
- Source: PARAPHRASE. From Wolfgang Fürweger's biography of Dietrich Mateschitz.
- Move: list the strangest things people say about your product and put them somewhere public instead of issuing a denial.

**16.6 Michael Ovitz rebuilding the base**
- Situation: lost a ninth-grade class election.
- Did: analysed why he lost and who his friend group was, then spent two years deliberately building constituencies across different parts of a 3,800-student school and practising public speaking.
- Happened: won vice president, then president by wide margins.
- Source: PODCAST. (Michael Ovitz, on the David Senra podcast, 2025-11-23)
- Move: diagnose the structural cause of the loss and spend the following quarter fixing it.

# SITUATION 17 — PRICING NERVE

**Structural name:** your price is a positioning decision and you are treating it as
a confidence problem.

**Routes from:** I'm undercharging, should I raise my prices, scared they'll leave,
feel guilty charging more.

**The library does not say "raise your prices" as a rule. It says price is a
positioning decision you make deliberately and then hold. Cards 17.7 and 17.8 exist
so you can go the other way when that is the honest answer.**

**17.1 Michael Ovitz returning the cheque**
- Situation: sent a $10 million payment for advertising work.
- Did: returned it with a note suggesting a discussion.
- Happened: the revised check was $31 million.
- Source: PARAPHRASE. Michael Ovitz tells this in *Who Is Michael Ovitz?*
- Move: reopen one underpriced arrangement this week with a friendly, unrelenting counter.

**17.2 Michael Ovitz and the Coca-Cola cheque**
- Situation: Coca-Cola sent a $3M cheque assuming an invoicing error. The commercial had genuinely cost about $30,000, and CAA had billed that.
- Did: voided and returned the cheque.
- Happened: it created the opening for a far larger conversation about what CAA itself was worth.
- Source: PODCAST. (Michael Ovitz, on the David Senra podcast, 2025-11-23)
- Move: separate your cost-plus pricing from your value pricing, and be explicit about which one you are charging.

**17.3 Dietrich Mateschitz pricing to create a category**
- Situation: every carbonated drink on the shelf was cheaper.
- Did: priced Red Bull at $2 a can, far above all of them. His reasoning was that a 15% premium would have made him a premium soft drink rather than a different category altogether.
- Happened: it read as a different category.
- Source: PARAPHRASE. From Wolfgang Fürweger's biography of Dietrich Mateschitz.
- Move: set your price at the level that forces buyers to categorize you separately, not at a modest markup over the incumbent.

**17.4 Ralph Lauren above the ceiling**
- Situation: ties sold for $3 or $4.
- Did: started his at $7.50 wholesale, retailing at $15, and argued a better product does not split a fixed market — it expands it.
- Happened: he defended the price with the story.
- Source: PARAPHRASE. From Jeffrey A. Trachtenberg's *Ralph Lauren: The Man Behind the Mystique*.
- Move: add one concrete quality upgrade, then raise price above the category ceiling and defend it with the story.

**17.5 James Dyson's washing machine**
- Situation: the washing machine cost far more to build — two drums, two motors, a gearbox — and launched above competitors. Marketing said a lower price would sell more volume.
- Did: he listened and lowered it.
- Happened: sales did not move and losses grew. He believes he should have raised the price instead, and says it was the last time in his life he listened to them on that question.
- Source: PODCAST. (James Dyson, on the David Senra podcast, 2025-12-07)
- Move: reserve pricing and specification decisions for the people who built the thing.

**17.6 Evan Spiegel charging the passionate few**
- Situation: features the general roadmap could never justify.
- Did: shipped them as Snapchat+ at about four dollars a month, for the most passionate users.
- Happened: roughly 25 million subscribers, about a billion dollars of run rate, growing about 60% year over year. He wishes he had started it earlier.
- Source: PODCAST. (Evan Spiegel, on the David Senra podcast, 2026-04-12)
- Move: charge your most passionate users for the features your mass-market roadmap keeps deprioritizing.

**17.7 Rose Blumkin — the other direction**
- Situation: a permanently low markup.
- Did: sold at 10% over cost from the beginning and never drifted upward. Asked for her business plan, she gave five words: sell cheap and tell the truth.
- Happened: she built an enormous business on it.
- Source: PARAPHRASE. From Karen Linder's *The Women of Berkshire Hathaway*.
- Move: if low price is your position, hold it deliberately and say the number out loud.

**17.8 Sol Price — the other direction**
- Situation: competitors ran 36 to 50% markups.
- Did: ran 12%.
- Happened: he built an enormous business on it, and absorbed the backlash described in card 3.7.
- Source: PARAPHRASE. From Robert Price's *Sol Price: Retail Revolutionary and Social Innovator*.
- Move: pick your markup as a strategy, not as a reaction.

**17.9 Akio Morita on the launch budget**
- Situation: a Sony president wanted to underspend on a launch campaign.
- Did: Morita threatened to fire him.
- Happened: his position was that saving marketing money looks like profit but is really liquidating brand assets built in prior years.
- Source: PARAPHRASE. Akio Morita tells this in *Made in Japan*.
- Move: set the launch budget by the five-year customer value, not the first quarter's return.

# SITUATION 18 — SOMEONE WANTS TO BUY YOU

**Structural name:** a number has appeared, and it is doing your thinking for you.

**Routes from:** acquisition offer, should I sell, someone wants to buy my business,
the number is life-changing.

**Never tell a member whether to take an offer. Give the cards and the questions the
cards produce.**

**18.1 Estée Lauder's million dollars**
- Situation: offered a million dollars in the 1950s.
- Did: refused.
- Happened: the market cap is now around $30B.
- Source: PARAPHRASE. Estée Lauder tells this in her memoir, *Estée: A Success Story*.
- Move: value the offer against the trajectory, not against your current bank balance.

**18.2 Adam Foroughi walking from $600M**
- Situation: 2015, around $50M of EBITDA, growing 100% year over year. An all-cash offer reached $600M.
- Did: walked, because his number was closer to a billion. With investors on a board he might have faced pressure to take it.
- Happened: market cap at the time of the interview was roughly $140B. He adds two consequences: your business grows between the offer and the close, and once part of the team knew he had refused that much money and could calculate their share, he owed them a path to liquidity.
- Source: PODCAST. (Adam Foroughi, on the David Senra podcast, 2026-05-03)
- Move: write down your walk-away number before a term sheet exists, so the size of the number cannot move you.

**18.3 Evan Spiegel's inversion list**
- Situation: acquisition interest.
- Did: identified the contrarian decisions a buyer would revert. Separately, investors let him and his co-founder sell about ten million dollars of stock very early, which covered a house and a family.
- Happened: that removed financial pressure from every later decision.
- Source: PODCAST. (Evan Spiegel, on the David Senra podcast, 2026-04-12)
- Move: write down which of your contrarian decisions the buyer would undo. That list is the real price.

**18.4 Todd Graves on what private equity buys**
- Situation: offers to founders who have reinvested everything.
- Did: argues private equity is expert at packaging an offer a tired founder cannot refuse, often $5 to $10M, and that the price is control. Once financial return is the governing goal, prices rise at the wrong time, quality slips, wages and bonuses get trimmed, and the business stops being special.
- Happened: Graves' own position is that once you take the check the business stops being yours to protect.
- Second source, keep it separate: the two named examples — Trader Joe's founder Joe Coulombe, whose autobiography spends 90% of its pages in love with the business and closes by admitting he was not true to himself and regretted selling, and Paul Orfalea of Kinko's, who had the money but could not bear to walk into a store — are David Senra's evidence in that episode, not Graves'. If you use them, say so. Never put them in Graves' mouth.
- Source: PODCAST. (Todd Graves, on the David Senra podcast, 2025-11-09). For the Coulombe and Orfalea examples: (David Senra, on the David Senra podcast, 2025-11-09).
- Move: read one founder's account of life after the exit before you sign one.

**18.5 Walt Disney refusing**
- Situation: distributors wanted to acquire the studio.
- Did: refused. He wanted to be the king of animation, not another producer.
- Happened: this card records a position he held, not an outcome the book reports. Say it that way.
- Source: PARAPHRASE. From Neal Gabler's *Walt Disney: The Triumph of the American Imagination*.
- Move: decide in advance what you would refuse to sell, so the offer does not decide for you.

**18.5b Walt Disney's own scorecard — a different book, cite it separately**
- Situation: he went bankrupt and lost his first company around 22, and was later cheated by the syndicate that bought his shorts.
- Did: rebuilt, and kept control the second time.
- Happened: late in life he named two achievements as his proudest — keeping control of his company, and Disneyland.
- Source: PARAPHRASE. From Paul Johnson's *Creators*.
- Move: write down what you would want to have kept control of in forty years, and check whether today's deal protects it.
- **Never cite this alongside 18.5 or 25.1. Different book, separate citation.**

**18.6 Daniel Ek's mission test**
- Situation: acquisition offers for Spotify.
- Did: considered them, but never for money, since he already had what he thought he needed. He would have sold to a company that could further the mission and cared as much as they did.
- Happened: he never found one, so they kept going.
- Source: PODCAST. (Daniel Ek, on the David Senra podcast, 2025-09-28)
- Move: write your acquisition test in mission terms now, before an offer creates the pressure.

**18.7 Tamara Mellon's one point**
- Situation: majority ownership.
- Did: names the gap between 50% and 51% as the gap between partner and employee.
- Happened: every owner she had was optimizing EBITDA for a resale three years out. Cheaper leather, unrefurbished stores, no marketing — each defensible on a spreadsheet, each a withdrawal from the brand.
- Source: PARAPHRASE. Tamara Mellon tells this in her memoir, *In My Shoes*.
- Move: ask any prospective investor how long they intend to hold, and treat an answer that is all about the exit as a product-quality risk, not just a financial one.

# SITUATION 19 — SOMEONE IS TRYING TO SHUT YOU DOWN

**Structural name:** a party with more process than you is using process instead of
product to beat you.

**Routes from:** I'm being sued, the association blocked me, a big competitor is
coming after me legally, a regulator is on me.

**Never give legal advice. Every card here is a management precedent. Say that out
loud in the output, exactly as the library does.**

**19.1 Herb Kelleher escalating**
- Situation: two courts ruled against Southwest and the original funding was gone in legal fees.
- Did: offered to defer his fees and pay court costs personally, and framed the company as a crusade against opponents trying to shut it down.
- Happened: won at the state supreme court. The rivals were later indicted for conspiring to destroy the company.
- Source: PARAPHRASE. From Kevin and Jackie Freiberg's *Nuts!*
- Move: escalate rather than settle when a competitor is using process rather than product to beat you.

**19.2 Brian Armstrong building the record**
- Situation: regulatory ambiguity at Coinbase.
- Did: his team went in roughly thirty times offering full disclosure and asking to be told the standard. Being told to consult their own lawyer, then receiving enforcement, is what made the record usable later. He escalated twice — the courts, then legislation. Very few companies sue their regulator, so he found financial-services CEOs who had and won, and looked at precedents like SpaceX's contract challenge.
- Happened: his estimate is $50 to $100M in legal costs, versus pressure on the stock he puts in the $10 to $20B range over a couple of years. The case ended with no fine and no change to the business. He is direct that pre-IPO Coinbase probably could not have afforded this, and that peers without the balance sheet folded or moved offshore.
- Source: PODCAST. (Brian Armstrong, on the David Senra podcast, 2026-03-01)
- Then vs now: he could afford it and says most people cannot. This is not legal advice.
- Move: put every request for guidance from a regulator, platform or large partner in writing, and keep the replies.

**19.3 Michael Ovitz's bluff**
- Situation: a threatened lawsuit from the industry leader, against a broke 28-year-old.
- Did: implied he could route the matter to a Justice Department antitrust probe. It was a bluff.
- Happened: they backed off. That single letter produced what he called a siege mentality that defined the firm for twenty years.
- Source: PARAPHRASE. Michael Ovitz tells this in *Who Is Michael Ovitz?*
- Move: inventory the asymmetric pressure you can apply — regulatory, reputational, relational — before you concede on legal firepower. Then turn the near-death moment into a stated company value rather than a story you tell once.

**19.4 Les Schwab against his franchisor**
- Situation: his franchisor threatened to seize his equipment, which would have bankrupted him.
- Did: told them to take it to court. He was bluffing.
- Happened: a loss in court would have jeopardized their contracts with 1,100 other franchisees.
- Source: PARAPHRASE. From *Les Schwab: Pride In Performance*.
- Move: before conceding to a bigger party, work out what a public loss would cost them across their whole book.

**19.5 Ingvar Kamprad and the trade-association boycott**
- Situation: the industry body cut off his suppliers and banned him from showing prices at fairs.
- Did: altered designs and found a new supply base in Poland.
- Happened: he called it a crisis that became a non-crisis. See card 13.1.
- Source: PARAPHRASE. From *Leading By Design: The IKEA Story*.
- Move: write down three capabilities the blockade could force you to build.

**19.6 Dietrich Mateschitz routing around the ban**
- Situation: blocked in Germany for over a year.
- Did: EU formation meant approval in one member state carried automatically to another, so he got approved via Great Britain — a market he did not want.
- Happened: Germany opened the next day.
- Source: PARAPHRASE. From Wolfgang Fürweger's biography of Dietrich Mateschitz.
- Move: find the regulatory or platform shift underway in your industry and route around your blocker through it.

**19.7 Soichiro Honda refusing protection**
- Situation: manufacturers petitioning the government for import restrictions.
- Did: he was the only one who declined. His reasoning was that high quality goods know no national boundaries.
- Happened: he competed on the product.
- Source: PARAPHRASE. From Sol Sanders' *Honda: The Man and His Machines*.
- Move: drop any argument that your position depends on rules rather than on your product being better.

# SITUATION 20 — NO CREDENTIALS

**Structural name:** you are treating a missing résumé line as a missing capability.

**Routes from:** I don't have the background, I'm not qualified, I don't know anyone
in this industry, who am I to do this, imposter.

**20.1 James Dyson on six months**
- Situation: he was not a qualified engineer.
- Did: built his first cyclonic prototype out of cereal packets and masking tape, before he understood why it worked.

**20.2 Soichiro Honda sleeping in the factory**

- Did: lived in the factory and slept on a straw mat on the floor until he did know. And he built his reputation on the jobs older shops had already given up on.
- Happened: taking the abandoned job became a founding principle of the company.
- Source: PARAPHRASE. From Sol Sanders' *Honda: The Man and His Machines*.

**20.3 Jimmy Iovine sweeping the studio**

- Source: PODCAST. (Jimmy Iovine, on the David Senra podcast, 2026-02-01)

**20.4 Lulu Cheng Meservey being underqualified on purpose**

- Did: learned by getting it wrong in front of serious people — too many Power Rangers references in one meeting, and a meeting with a retailer where it was clear she thought corporate communications meant announcing a summer sale.
- Happened: Anduril then handed a first-year company's defining moment to someone objectively underqualified, when they could have hired anyone.
- Source: PODCAST. (Lulu Cheng Meservey, on the David Senra podcast, 2026-08-12)

**20.5 Steve Stoute going to Queensbridge**

- Did: went to the Queensbridge projects, which have no front door, and started asking. The first person he asked went to get a gun; a bystander vouched for him, and that person turned out to be Nas's brother.
- Happened: he is also candid that he was one of roughly fifty people who recognized Nas as an undervalued asset. The insight was not unique. He got chosen.
- Source: PODCAST. (Steve Stoute, on the David Senra podcast, 2026-06-21)
- Move: identify the physical place your target actually is, and show up instead of looking for an introduction. Stop protecting your insight as proprietary and compete on execution and relationship.

**20.6 Ralph Lauren's cold call**
- Situation: an unknown with a nervous voice.
- Did: phoned the menswear editor at Playboy.
- Happened: got the first national magazine feature on his ties, which produced a small but influential following. Senra flags never having sent a cold email as his own mistake.
- Source: PARAPHRASE. From Jeffrey A. Trachtenberg's *Ralph Lauren: The Man Behind the Mystique*.
- Move: send three cold messages this week to the people whose attention would change your trajectory.

**20.7 Daniel Ek on being an outsider**
- Situation: he says he feels like an outsider every moment — in his childhood in the Swedish projects, among founders, and in Silicon Valley as a non-American.
- Did: tried to mimic Jobs, Bezos, Gates and Schultz, and walked away disillusioned each time because none of it fit. So he reasons from principles to whatever actually works for him.
- Happened: the consequence is that he cannot fully adopt anyone's lessons.
- Source: PODCAST. (Daniel Ek, on the David Senra podcast, 2025-09-28)
- Move: name the archetype you actually are, then stop consuming advice written for a different one.

# SITUATION 21 — RAISE OR BOOTSTRAP

**Structural name:** you are deciding whether to trade the range of endings you can
have for the speed you can go.

**Routes from:** should I raise, an investor is interested, do I need funding, can I
do this without money, term sheet, dilution.

**21.1 Jason Fried on collapsing the endings**
- Situation: outside capital.
- Did: names what it does — collapses many possible endings into one: get very large or fail. Founders then blast past what would have been a fine business into something no longer good enough for their investors. He sold his teenage software to a few thousand people at $20 and cleared roughly $20k a year with no overhead.
- Happened: his point is that heavy overhead forces you to find a far larger audience that shares your taste.
- Source: PODCAST. (Jason Fried, on the David Senra podcast, 2026-02-15)
- Move: list every ending still available to you today, and reject any deal that deletes most of the list. Then compute how many customers your current cost base requires, and cut costs until that number feels findable.

**21.2 John Mackey on the seven-year clock**
- Situation: VC money at Whole Foods.
- Did: took it, credits it for getting Whole Foods to Northern California, and is glad he did. Then went public in 1992 specifically so the VCs, who held 34%, could not gain control in another round.
- Happened: his verdict is that VCs run a blockbuster model chasing 100x, so they push businesses to scale faster than the business wants. Good companies get wrecked; founders get diluted in down rounds or replaced by professional management. The warning sign he names is being told not to worry about your burn rate. That is what precedes the cram-down.
- Source: PODCAST. (John Mackey, on the David Senra podcast, 2026-01-04)
- Move: model what happens to your ownership in a flat or down round before you accept the next term sheet.

**21.3 James Dyson on who the money comes from**
- Situation: the Ballbarrow failure.
- Did: he is emphatic that debt was not the mistake. The mistake was investors and directors who did not understand what a startup costs emotionally and operationally. When he restarted with the vacuum, he deliberately went back to Fry — someone who had actually done it.
- Happened: he calls not letting Fry fund the Ballbarrow a genuinely stupid decision, and notes the irony that he took money from others anyway, just from people less equipped to help.
- Source: PODCAST. (James Dyson, on the David Senra podcast, 2025-12-07)
- Move: filter capital by whether the person on the other side has built and shipped. And separate the desire for autonomy from the desire to look self-made.

**21.4 Adam Foroughi bootstrapping by choice**
- Situation: pre-product-market-fit.
- Did: bootstrapped the first 18 months by choice — before the VC rejections, not because of them. He wanted freedom to pivot repeatedly without managing investors, and only raised once he knew the company would be an advertising business.
- Happened: eight months from launch to a million dollars a month of run rate, barely profitable. That ramp made the November 2012 round easy: $4M at a $25M pre-money convertible note, taken mostly from people he already knew, with his own money marked in.
- Source: PODCAST. (Adam Foroughi, on the David Senra podcast, 2026-05-03)
- Move: fund your own pre-product-market-fit period so that pivoting costs you nothing politically.

**21.5 Tamara Mellon starting small**
- Situation: launching Jimmy Choo.
- Did: launched on $150,000, a tiny shop at $15,000 a year in rent, two desks in a windowless basement, and one employee beyond the founders.
- Happened: first-year sales were $250,000 and the company was profitable from day one. It never needed outside money again. The only reason outsiders got in was a partner selling his stake.
- Source: PARAPHRASE. Tamara Mellon tells this in her memoir, *In My Shoes*.
- Move: set your fixed costs low enough that profitability arrives before outside capital does.

**21.6 David Baszucki's untouched floor**
- Situation: funding Roblox.
- Did: took under $10 million of equity to cash-flow breakeven and never dipped below that initial $10 million, keeping padding from secondaries as insurance. He deliberately connected with funds capable of riding decades, avoiding anyone for whom Roblox was an early, career-defining bet.
- Happened: the raise functioned as a floor rather than a budget.
- Source: PODCAST. (David Baszucki, on the David Senra podcast, 2026-04-26)
- Move: ask each prospective investor how long they can hold, before you ask what they will pay.

**21.7 Dietrich Mateschitz spending only earnings**
- Situation: two founders put in $500,000 each.
- Did: beyond one small loan in year two, funded all expansion from profit. No dividends until 1999 — well over a decade in which he lived on his managing-director salary while every profit went back into expansion.
- Happened: profitable from year three onward, every year since.
- Source: PARAPHRASE. From Wolfgang Fürweger's biography of Dietrich Mateschitz.
- Move: cap your growth spending at trailing profit, and refuse the facility that would let you outrun it.

# SITUATION 22 — EXTERNAL DISASTER

**Structural name:** something outside your control removed your ability to operate,
and the only question left is what you do first.

**Routes from:** flood, fire, storm, shutdown, force majeure, outage, something
outside my control ended my quarter.

**22.1 Todd Graves and Katrina**
- Situation: Katrina knocked out 21 of 28 restaurants, with no cash coming in and every lender and landlord still owed.
- Did: reopened 30 days after Katrina where competitors took 90 to 120. Fed first responders first, ran on generators shipped in from around the country, and worked with the state on boil-water procedures that did not yet exist. He explained exactly how the company was financed to his team, and gave three reasons to reopen: save the business, give displaced crew somewhere to earn, and feed people returning to New Orleans.
- Happened: they had whole markets to themselves. Sales went to record levels and funded large crew bonuses. Watching the levees break, he resolved never to leave the company financially strapped again.
- Source: PODCAST. (Todd Graves, on the David Senra podcast, 2025-11-09)
- Move: decide in advance what it would take to be first to reopen, and pre-position for it.

**22.2 John Mackey and the Austin flood**
- Situation: an eight-foot flood with sewage and tetanus shots, nine months after the first store opened.
- Did: during cleanup Mackey found a stranger mopping — a customer who took a day off because he needed the store to survive.
- Happened: that customer is the card.
- Source: PODCAST. (John Mackey, on the David Senra podcast, 2026-01-04)
- Move: find the customers who would volunteer for you, and design for them before the mainstream.

**22.3 Rose Blumkin turning disasters into assets**
- Situation: a 1961 fire, then a 1975 tornado that leveled the store and the post office next door.
- Did: ran a smoke-damaged fire sale after the fire. Bought the post office ground after the tornado and expanded onto it.
- Happened: the fire sale drew a two-block line.
- Source: PARAPHRASE. From Karen Linder's *The Women of Berkshire Hathaway*.
- Move: when something breaks, ask what it just made available to you before you ask what it cost.

**22.4 Soichiro Honda extracting material**
- Situation: earthquake fires, then firebombing.
- Did: wooden spokes burned, so he made metal ones and patented them. Firebombing dropped fighter-plane fuel tanks that supplied him scarce metal.
- Happened: the spokes were the first of over a hundred personal patents.
- Source: PARAPHRASE. From Sol Sanders' *Honda: The Man and His Machines*.
- Move: take the thing that just went wrong and list what it made newly available to you.

**22.5 Dana White during the shutdown**
- Situation: his reasoning was that a sports network's accountants would eventually rank their obligations and cut his first, so continuing was survival rather than opportunism.
- Did: built what he describes as the only genuine isolation bubble in sports at the time — staff living on site for months, repeated testing, chartered flights. Even though his broadcaster had committed to pay him regardless, he ran events anyway. He was not personally exposed to loss while staff faced layoffs and salary cuts, so he offered up his own compensation instead.
- Happened: he delivered outsized numbers as the only game available, and the business grew sharply.
- Source: PODCAST. (Dana White, on the David Senra podcast, 2026-05-10)
- Move: cut your own compensation before you cut anyone else's.

**22.6 Andrew Carnegie expanding into the panic**
- Situation: the Panic of 1873 — a quarter of railroads bankrupt, a sixth of the workforce jobless.
- Did: launched his first steel mill into it. Frick spent the same downturn buying out failing competitors.
- Happened: the mill was built for 25% under projection because materials and labor had collapsed in price. Frick reached a quarter of the region's coke ovens by 1882.
- Source: PARAPHRASE. From Les Standiford's *Meet You in Hell*.
- Move: write your acquisition and hiring list now, so it is ready the moment the market turns down.

# SITUATION 23 — EVERYONE SAYS IT WON'T WORK

**Structural name:** you are treating a consensus of opinions as a consensus of
evidence.

**Routes from:** everyone says it's a bad idea, my industry says this can't work, the
experts told me no, my family thinks I'm crazy.

**23.1 Todd Graves' worst grade**
- Situation: his business plan got the worst grade in the class. The professor praised the detail and rejected the concept. Every expert told him a chicken-fingers-only restaurant could not work in Louisiana, and the industry told him a single item would kill repeat visits and expose him to the veto vote — one person in the car forcing everyone elsewhere.
- Did: built it anyway.
- Happened: his frequency now matches any quick-service competitor.
- Source: PODCAST. (Todd Graves, on the David Senra podcast, 2025-11-09)
- Quotable: "It's not simple. It's focused." (Todd Graves, on the David Senra podcast, 2025-11-09)
- Move: convert your most credible no into a specific thing you will prove within a year.

**23.2 James Dyson reading the incumbent's incentives**
- Situation: the standard rejection was that if a better vacuum were possible, Hoover and Electrolux would have made it.
- Did: he did not keep pitching them.
- Happened: Senra's synthesis via Munger on incentives is that it is very hard to sell a bagless vacuum to companies earning around $500 million a year selling bags. Dyson agrees, adding that they also simply did not want to change.
- Source: PODCAST. (James Dyson, on the David Senra podcast, 2025-12-07)
- Quotable: "The entrenched professional will always resist longer than the independent consumer." (James Dyson, on the David Senra podcast, 2025-12-07)
- Move: map who loses revenue if your idea works, and stop pitching them.

**23.3 Phil Knight's zero questions**
- Situation: presented the Japan-imports thesis to his business-school classmates.
- Did: got zero questions. Pure boredom. His father wanted respectability and called the business jackassing around.
- Happened: it became a $1B+ company. Knight names ignoring his father's advice as his most consequential decision.
- Source: PARAPHRASE. Phil Knight tells this in *Shoe Dog*.
- Move: stop using peer reaction as a signal. Ship the first version to a real customer instead of a room of evaluators. And identify who in your life is optimizing for your respectability rather than your outcome.

**23.4 Michael Ovitz on articulate opposition**
- Situation: every idea he ever pursued.
- Did: proceeded through articulate opposition from multiple people every time. Starting an agency at 26 against 180 incumbents was called impossible. So was signing movie stars. So was moving into advertising.
- Happened: he names articulate opposition as the constant, not the signal.
- Source: PODCAST. (Michael Ovitz, on the David Senra podcast, 2025-11-23)
- Quotable: "Fear is the killer and enemy of business." (Michael Ovitz, on the David Senra podcast, 2025-11-23)
- Move: write down the strongest objection to your plan, decide whether it is fatal or merely discouraging, and proceed accordingly.

**23.5 Akio Morita and the Walkman**
- Situation: nobody inside Sony wanted it. Engineers, marketers and executives all said a recorder that cannot record will not sell.
- Did: took personal responsibility for it and shipped it. Told nobody would buy a player without recording, he pointed to millions of car stereos that also record nothing.
- Happened: he put his own name on the decision.
- Source: PARAPHRASE. Akio Morita tells this in *Made in Japan*.
- Move: name the one project only you believe in and put your own name on the decision. For each internal objection, find the market that already proves it wrong.

**23.6 Dietrich Mateschitz's one trusted judgment**
- Situation: nearly everyone called Red Bull a bad idea.
- Did: brought it to his friend Kastner, whose judgment he respected.
- Happened: Kastner said that pursued consistently it would succeed, and understood him well enough to know he was a perfectionist.
- Source: PARAPHRASE. From Wolfgang Fürweger's biography of Dietrich Mateschitz.
- Move: find the one person whose judgment you trust and bring them the idea the crowd rejects.

**23.7 Arnold Schwarzenegger's father**
- Situation: he announced he would go to America and be an actor. Bodybuilding was the least popular sport in the country — 20 or 30 practitioners nationally.
- Did: went anyway.
- Happened: his father wanted to take him to a doctor.
- Source: PARAPHRASE. Arnold Schwarzenegger tells this in *Arnold: The Education of a Bodybuilder*.
- Move: stop treating "nobody else is doing this" as a reason to stop.

# SITUATION 24 — THE WORK IS EATING YOUR LIFE

**Structural name:** the cost of the work has moved outside the business.

**Routes from:** burning out, never see my family, can't switch off, costing me my
relationship, exhausted.

**These are precedent cards only. Name what the person did and stop. Never write a
move that reads as medical, psychological or therapeutic advice. If the member
describes a mental health crisis, say plainly that this tool is not for that and
that they should talk to someone qualified.**

**24.1 Brian Armstrong on what actually kills founders**
- Situation: his YC batch.
- Did: watched batchmates break down within three to four years in visible physical ways. Each burnout patch of his own was resolved by delegating more, dropping a piece of the job, or reducing direct reports. Working right up to bed produced stressful dreams and poor rest.
- Happened: burnout, not competition, killed most of his peers. His view is that you can burn the candle at both ends for years, but the impact you want requires decades.
- Source: PODCAST. (Brian Armstrong, on the David Senra podcast, 2026-03-01)
- Move: schedule a semiannual review of what to hand off, and actually hand one thing off.

**24.2 Michael Ovitz's 10%**
- Situation: warned at 33 that he could work 10% less with no professional cost.
- Did: ignored it.
- Happened: looking back he estimated 20% — roughly three years of life reclaimed over thirty. His verdict on the hardest years is that he would have been much happier had he not been so determined to seem all-knowing.
- Source: PARAPHRASE. Michael Ovitz tells this in *Who Is Michael Ovitz?*
- Move: cut one recurring commitment that produces status rather than results. Tell one trusted person the thing you are currently pretending you have handled.

**24.3 Tony Xu's two fixed rituals**
- Situation: the worst stretch at DoorDash.
- Did: kept exercise constant — marathon training then, something else now — along with date nights. There was no single technique for managing his psychology. These were the things that held.
- Happened: he also says what gets you through the next day is wanting a teammate to succeed, rather than thinking about the company.
- Source: PODCAST. (Tony Xu, on the David Senra podcast, 2026-03-29)
- Move: protect one daily physical ritual and one relationship ritual as non-negotiable.

**24.4 Bruce Springsteen**
- Situation: he ran for decades — literally driving through his hometown at night without leaving the car — until it reached critical mass at 32.
- Did: concealed severe anxiety attacks from his new wife, and calls it a mistake. It built a psychological wall at the moment he was trying to let someone in. Twenty-five years with the same doctor.
- Happened: his own verdict is that there are no permanent victories, just this day and a hesitant belief you can change. Four hundred pages of obsession resolve into one line: work is work, life is life.
- Source: PARAPHRASE. Bruce Springsteen tells this in *Born to Run*.
- Move: tell one person the real state of things this week instead of the managed version.

**24.5 Phil Knight's one regret**
- Situation: his sons.
- Did: vowed repeatedly to change, kept the promise briefly, and fell back into the routine every time.
- Happened: the morning after the IPO left him worth $178 million, his dominant feeling was regret that he could not do it all again.
- Source: PARAPHRASE. Phil Knight tells this in *Shoe Dog*.
- Move: put the non-negotiable family hours in the calendar as fixed blocks, and treat breaking them as breaking a customer commitment.

**24.6 Ray Kroc's colleague — the hardest card here**
- Situation: a colleague who missed every one of her sons' birthdays and graduations for the company.
- Did: Senra pushes back hard on Kroc over this.
- Happened: she was later pushed out by the same man who asked for it. The partner who lost faith and sold his stock forfeited a fortune.
- Source: PARAPHRASE. From Ray Kroc's *Grinding It Out*.
- Move: work before your kids wake and after they sleep if you must, but do not miss the birthday party.

# SITUATION 25 — YOU DON'T OWN YOUR OWN WORK

**Structural name:** the value you created sits inside someone else's contract.

**Routes from:** I signed a bad contract, who owns the IP, they own my brand name,
the client owns everything I made, I gave away too much.

**25.1 Walt Disney losing Oswald**
- Situation: his contract gave him no rights to his own creations.
- Did: the intermediary who held the distribution contract eventually decided he did not need Disney at all.
- Happened: he lost his characters, his staff and his company to a middleman. The theft forced the train ride on which he drew Mickey Mouse.
- Source: PARAPHRASE. From Neal Gabler's *Walt Disney: The Triumph of the American Imagination*.
- Move: audit every contract for who owns the IP, the customer relationship, and the renewal rights.

**25.2 James Dyson and the Ballbarrow patent**
- Situation: he put the Ballbarrow patent in the company's name.
- Did: lost the patent along with the company when he was pushed out.
- Happened: the accidental rescue was that those same partners had already rejected the vacuum cleaner, so it was never theirs to take.
- Source: PODCAST. (James Dyson, on the David Senra podcast, 2025-12-07). The same fact appears as PARAPHRASE in *Against the Odds* by James Dyson if you prefer the book citation. Never both.
- Move: check today who legally owns the IP you personally invented.

**25.3 Ray Kroc's default clause**
- Situation: excitement over the opportunity led him to accept terms that put him in default from his very first store. He needed written, signed, registered approval for every deviation.
- Did: the brothers approved by phone and never mailed anything, leaving him permanently in breach. He later paid $2.7 million to end their half-percent royalty.
- Happened: the buyout looked ruinous and cost roughly $14 million all-in, yet retaining that slice was worth millions annually within years.
- Source: PARAPHRASE. Ray Kroc tells this in *Grinding It Out*.
- Move: refuse any clause whose performance depends on your counterparty bothering to act. Calculate the lifetime cost of every ongoing percentage you pay, and get a buyout number.

**25.4 Dana White buying the rights back**
- Situation: all they acquired initially was three letters, an old cage, and a handful of fighter contracts. Merchandise, library, video games and DVD rights had been sold off by the prior owner to stay alive.
- Did: repurchased the lot for low single-digit millions.
- Happened: he contrasts it with the Bruce Lee estate, which has never been able to buy its film rights back at any price.
- Source: PODCAST. (Dana White, on the David Senra podcast, 2026-05-10)
- Move: buy back the rights to your own work now, while your leverage is low and so is the price.

**25.5 Steve Stoute on optionality clauses**
- Situation: standard record deals trapped any group member's future solo career with the same label.
- Did: Wu-Tang signed the group in one place while leaving each member free to sign individually elsewhere. Separately, Ryan Coogler would only place *Sinners* with a studio that let the rights revert to him, which was unprecedented at that scale.
- Happened: the Wu-Tang structure was a genuine breakthrough that others immediately copied. *Sinners* made roughly $400 million and led the awards field. Every other director now wants that deal.
- Source: PODCAST. (Steve Stoute, on the David Senra podcast, 2026-06-21)
- Move: read the clause that governs what happens when you succeed, and cap its reach before you sign. Point to the precedent someone else already won when you ask for a term your industry calls impossible.

**25.6 James Dyson's licensing trap**
- Situation: a Japanese licensee.
- Did: took a back-end royalty with a guaranteed minimum instead of money up front.
- Happened: the licensee's product reached $12M a year in sales while he collected only the $60,000 guaranteed minimum, because reported sales were unverifiable from another country.
- Source: PARAPHRASE. James Dyson tells this in *Against the Odds*.
- Move: take the money up front rather than trusting a royalty you cannot verify.

# CROSS-CUTTING CARDS

Attach to any situation. Use these to build a synthesized move when the situation
cards give you the diagnosis but not the next step.

**X1 — Phil Knight's four questions.** What do I know? What else do I know? What does
the future hold? What's the next step? He used exactly this to plan the Onitsuka
breakup. PARAPHRASE, *Shoe Dog* by Phil Knight.

**X2 — Reduce the crisis to four moves.** See card 5.3. PARAPHRASE, *Ralph Lauren: The
Man Behind the Mystique* by Jeffrey A. Trachtenberg.

**X3 — Detach before an irreversible decision.** Senra's read on Carnegie and Frick is
that focus narrowed until neither man could see past what was in front of his face.
Move: impose a mandatory delay between drafting a retaliatory decision and executing
it. PARAPHRASE, *Meet You in Hell* by Les Standiford.

**X4 — Sleep on the decision to quit.** Dana White was told to find a buyer and came
back with a low seven-figure estimate. The next morning his partner reversed himself
and told him to keep going. PODCAST. (Dana White, on the David Senra podcast,
2026-05-10)

**X5 — Control what is inside your control.** Through three years of hostile narrative
and failed fundraising, Tony Xu says obsessing over markets, press or investor
rejections would have been ruinous. Move: write down the two or three variables you
actually govern and refuse to discuss the rest in meetings. PODCAST. (Tony Xu, on the
David Senra podcast, 2026-03-29)

**X6 — State survival as an AND list, not an OR list.** Xu gathered roughly 20 to 25
area leaders: keep growing and taking share, get more profitable, and don't run out
of cash. No "or" in any of it. PODCAST. (Tony Xu, on the David Senra podcast,
2026-03-29)

**X7 — Decide in advance what would make you quit.** Mackey: decide in advance what
event would actually make you quit, so ordinary disasters don't get a vote. Graves:
decide now what would make you quit, and remove that exit. PODCAST. (John Mackey, on
the David Senra podcast, 2026-01-04) and (Todd Graves, on the David Senra podcast,
2025-11-09)

**X8 — Know when to give up.** Knight calls those who tell entrepreneurs to never give
up charlatans, and says giving up doesn't mean stopping. Move: set a kill criterion
for your current bet now, while you're still rational about it. PARAPHRASE, *Shoe Dog*
by Phil Knight. This is the counterweight to every persistence card in this file.

**X9 — Do the unglamorous distribution work yourself.** Anonymous at the time, Dana
White would walk into the local media superstore and move his own titles onto the
front display. He and his commentator alternated radio cycles, waking at 3 a.m. on
the west coast to hit east coast drive time, for years. PODCAST. (Dana White, on the
David Senra podcast, 2026-05-10)

**X10 — Excellence is the capacity to take pain.** This is Senra's maxim, which
Kalanick endorses and extends with the marathoner at mile 21. Attribute the maxim to
Senra, never to Kalanick. PODCAST. (Travis Kalanick, on the David Senra podcast,
2026-08-16)

**X11 — Money is a byproduct of service.** Attributed in every instance to Henry Ford
via Senra. It appears across the material and it is the spine of the synthesized move
whenever a member's question is really about money.

# TIER B CARDS

Lower confidence on surrounding context. The text is exact; the wider context was not
confirmed. Use only if a situation needs them, and never as the lead card.

**B1 — Michael Ovitz on partnership rate, not verdict.** Burned by partners who were
also close friends, he frames it as three people out of a thousand — a rate, not a
verdict. Move: judge a bad outcome against your full sample size before you close
yourself off. Also: ask each co-founder what they want the company to be in ten
years, and act on the mismatch rather than around it. Cite as the Founders Podcast
episode "Dinner With Michael Ovitz". Do not name a book. Routes to Situation 2.

**B2 — Cornelius Vanderbilt on a partner who cheats.** His own attorney and business
partner betrayed him, returned, and betrayed him again. Move: structure deals so that
no counterparty can act unilaterally on control, cash or share issuance. Routes to
Situation 2. Do not name a book.

**B3 — Joseph Pulitzer refusing to share power.** Every partnership ended the same way.
The account records that one did not work with Pulitzer — for him surely, against him
often, but not with him. Move: decide now which decisions you will genuinely share,
and say so before the partnership starts. Routes to Situation 2. Do not name a book.

**B4 — Brunello Cucinelli financing on customer payments.** With no money and no
collateral, his business ran entirely on timely payments — a first order of 53
sweaters, a yarn supplier who let him pay after he got paid, and a customer who paid
upfront on the strength of belief in him. Routes to Situation 5. Do not name a book.

**B5 — John Malone as anti-model.** Treating poor customer relations and price
increases as a byproduct of good business was viable only because of a regulated
monopoly, and decades of it produced cord-cutting the moment an alternative existed.
Move: audit which of your margins come from genuine value delivered versus from
customers having nowhere else to go. Routes to Situation 17 as a cautionary card. Do
not name a book.

**B6 — Vannevar Bush and a mass resignation.** When a whole section resigned in
protest, he told them to get back to work and that he would look into it. Both
happened, because they knew who was in charge. Routes to Situation 12. Thin. Do not
name a book.
