# Who Faced This

You are in a situation that feels specific to you. It almost never is.

Type what is happening to you in plain words. You get back three to five people who
were in the same position, what they actually did about it, what happened to them,
and one thing for you to do this week.

It does not give you a reading list. It gives you a move.

---

## What it is actually for

The useful part is not the famous names. It is the first line of the answer, where
the tool tells you what kind of situation you are in.

Most people describe events. "My biggest client is leaving." "The shop said no." "My
partner wants out." The tool names the structure underneath: that is concentration
risk, that is a gatekeeper refusal, that is a partnership that accumulated instead of
exploding.

Once you know which one it is, the right response is usually obvious. Most of the
time you were stuck because you had the wrong name for the problem.

---

## Install it

Nothing to do here. This tool installed with the rest of them in one step.
The classroom lesson **Install the tools** covers it once, in about three minutes.

## Use it

Type this, then your situation in your own words:

> who faced this — my biggest client just gave notice and they were about 60% of my
> revenue

You do not need to phrase it well. Half a sentence is fine. Dictated is fine.

**What happens next.** The tool comes back with one line naming what kind of
situation this is, and asks you to confirm. If it got it wrong, say so and it
re-matches. Then it gives you the cards and the move.

About two minutes end to end. One message from you, one question back, then the
answer.

## A real one

**What the member typed:**

> I finally got a meeting with a local shop to stock my product and they passed.
> Third one this month. I'm starting to think the product is just wrong.

**What came back:**

---

THIS IS: a gatekeeper refusal. Three people who control access to your customer said
no. That is a verdict on your access, not on your product, and until you have put it
in front of an actual buyer you do not know which one you have.

**Estée Lauder — rejected by the store she needed**
Was in: Harrods turned her down, and department store counters were the only route to
her customer.
Did: Got a magazine to write that her cosmetics were coming soon to London, before she
had any London distribution. Then made three trips with repeated press rounds.
Result: Customers started asking for her by name, and the buyer moved her to a
prestigious counter.
Source: Estée Lauder tells this in her memoir, *Estée: A Success Story*.

**James Dyson — laughed out of every hardware store**
Was in: Hardware stores and garden centres laughed at the big red ball on his
ballbarrow.
Did: Skipped them entirely and sold direct, off small newspaper ads.
Result: Consumers bought it straight off the ads. His own nuance: he would have
listened to a genuine design flaw. It was rejection without a stated reason that
convinced him he had something.
Source: James Dyson, on the David Senra podcast, 2025-12-07.

**Michael Ovitz — one year of Thursdays**
Was in: One literary agent controlled the material he needed and would not deal with
him.
Did: Called every Thursday at 10am for a full year.
Result: One manuscript. It became a hit miniseries and opened the floodgates.
Then vs now: He was working one person who held a lot. If your gatekeepers are
interchangeable, spend the year on customers instead.
Source: Michael Ovitz tells this in *Who Is Michael Ovitz?*

**Lulu Cheng Meservey — when going indirect is not available**
Was in: Anduril's first year. Palmer Luckey had just been pushed out of Facebook over
his political beliefs and was a pariah in tech and press, so pitching a hostile
reporter on hearing him out was not a real option.
Did: Went to the users instead. Quantico and the Marines, Camp Pendleton, National
Defense University, the CIA. Physically walking in, speaking to people, showing them
the thing.
Result: That was the first year's communications strategy.
Source: Lulu Cheng Meservey, on the David Senra podcast, 2026-08-12.

**YOUR MOVE THIS WEEK**
Go back to one of the three shops and ask for the specific objection. Not feedback.
The reason. Then get the product into ten end customers' hands yourself this week, by
any route that does not need a buyer's permission.
That is Dyson's card plus Estée Lauder's: demand the stated reason, and build the
demand the shop will have to notice.
By Friday you will know whether you have a product problem or an access problem. Those
need opposite responses and right now you are guessing between them.

## What it will not do

It will tell you when it does not have anything. That is the point of it.

There are real holes. It has almost nothing on freelance and agency problems — rate
setting, trading hours for dollars, a retainer getting cut. Nothing on Instagram,
TikTok, SEO, ads or any platform mechanic. Nothing on hiring paperwork or firing
procedure. Nothing on how to set a first price when you have no customers yet.

It will not give you legal, tax, financial or medical advice, and it will not tell you
whether to sell your business or whether to quit. It gives you the questions those
decisions actually turn on and leaves the decision with you.

And a precedent is not a promise. These are records of what people tried and what
followed. Where the gap between their situation and yours matters, the card says so
in one line.

If it does not have a precedent for you, it says so rather than reaching for the
nearest famous name. A stretched answer is worse than no answer.
