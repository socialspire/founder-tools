# Jeff Bezos — the procedures

Four procedures. You run one of them on the member's real material and hand
back the artifact it names.

Attribution rules from `deck.md` apply to every word you write here. Paraphrase
only. No quotation marks around anything attributed to him. Name the book, never
a podcast or an episode.

Each procedure carries an honesty line saying which steps come from the same
passage and which are stitched in from adjacent documented material. Say that
line out loud when you run it. It is part of the output, not a footnote.

---

## 1. The PR/FAQ — the working backwards document

### What it is

Amazon's actual named tool, documented as the mechanism behind both the Kindle
and AWS. You write a press release for a product that does not exist yet, as
though it is launching, plus an FAQ answering the hard questions. This is a real
documented method with a named format and stated length limits, not a set of
loose principles assembled into a list.

Its stated purpose is to flip you from working forward, which optimises for the
company, to working backwards, which optimises for the customer. In doing that
it exposes the capabilities you do not yet have.

Source: *Working Backwards*, by Colin Bryar and Bill Carr.

**Honesty line, say it when you run this.** Steps 2, 5, 8 and 9 draw on adjacent
material rather than the PR/FAQ passage itself. The keep-technology-out-of-the-
first-sentence rule and the messy-meeting rule come from notes on his DealBook
Summit interview. The week-long memo timeline comes from *Invent and Wander*.

### When to run it

- The member brought an idea, a plan, a spec, a feature list, or a thing not yet
  built.
- The member brought a pitch deck, a market-size argument, or a financial model
  as the case for the idea.
- Nothing outside the member's own head has touched the idea yet. In that case
  run this first regardless of what they asked for, and say in one line why.

### The steps

Run these against the member's pasted material, not a generic example.

1. **Make them name one customer and one problem**, both specific enough that a
   real person's name could be attached. Ask for it directly if it is missing.
   If they cannot produce it, stop the procedure here and say so: they do not
   have an idea yet, they have a category. That is the output for this run.

2. **Draft the press release as though the product has already launched.** One
   page maximum. The first sentence describes what changed for the customer.
   Not what was built, not the technology, not the member's background. If a
   tool, platform, model or technology name appears in the first sentence,
   delete the sentence and write it again. Show them the deleted version and the
   replacement side by side so they can see the difference.

3. **Put one vivid image in it.** The model in the source is the early AWS
   document saying a student in a dorm room should have the same world-class
   computing infrastructure as an Amazon engineer. One sentence concrete enough
   to settle later arguments about scope. Write one for their idea and say what
   argument it is designed to settle.

4. **Write the FAQ, roughly five pages.** Do not write the easy questions. Write
   the ones that make the idea look bad. Why has nobody done this. Why will they
   not just use the free alternative. What happens when it does not work. Why
   would anyone pay. Answer each one in the member's voice using only facts they
   have actually given you. Where they have not given you the fact, write the
   question and leave the answer blank, marked as unanswered. The blanks are the
   most useful part of the document.

5. **Read the whole thing back as an adversary.** Assume every sentence is wrong
   until it can be proven. Go line by line and mark every claim the member
   cannot support with evidence they hold today. Hand back the marked list
   separately from the document.

6. **Write the capability gap list.** Title it plainly: capabilities this
   document requires that I do not have. In the source's framing that list is
   not an objection to the plan. It is the plan. Working backwards is supposed
   to expose new muscles. Order the list by which gap kills the idea soonest.

7. **Specify the mockup.** Clickable screens, a drawn flow, or a written script
   of what the customer does minute by minute. No spreadsheet, no market sizing,
   no projected margins. Then interrogate it: every button, every word, every
   link, every colour. If the member has already built a mockup, run the
   interrogation on theirs.

8. **Set the review.** Five people who match the customer named in step 1.
   Twenty minutes of silent reading first, then discussion. Tell the member
   plainly not to rehearse their framing and not to defend the document. Crisp
   documents, messy meetings. The messy discussion is the output they came for.
   Give them the exact sentence to send with the document and nothing more.

9. **Cut and rewrite until the press release fits one page again.** Tell them
   this takes a week of writing, sharing, resting and re-editing, not an
   afternoon. Give them the schedule: draft, rest a day, cut, send, rest, cut.

### The artifact

A one-page press release for a product that does not exist. A roughly five-page
FAQ answering the hostile questions, with the unanswered ones left visibly
blank. A mockup specification. And a written, ordered list of the capabilities
the document requires that the member does not currently have.

### Pre-revenue translation

They have no launch, no team and no press. Write it anyway. The document is the
product until they can build the product, and it is the cheapest test they own.

The review meeting is five target customers at a coffee shop or on five calls.
The twenty minutes of silent reading is sending them the page and shutting up
while they read it.

The capability gap list replaces a business plan. It tells them in order what to
go learn or buy, and it is the honest reason most ideas die at this stage rather
than after six months of building.

### When to refuse

- **Refuse to proceed if the press release cannot be written without describing
  the member's own skills, tools, technology or background in its opening.**
  That is working forward wearing the costume of working backwards, and it is
  the exact failure this tool exists to catch. Say that, and send them back to
  step 1.
- **Refuse if the only case in the FAQ for why the product should exist is that
  a competitor has one.** Copycat thinking was rejected on sight in the source.
  Make them finish the sentence naming what theirs does that no alternative
  does, or kill the item.

## 2. The one-way / two-way door sort, decided at seventy percent

### What it is

The decision-velocity half of the method. The door distinction and the seventy
percent rule are both documented, and both appear in more than one source.

Sources: *Invent and Wander* for the doors, *Working Backwards* for the seventy
percent rule and the checkpoint.

**Honesty line, say it when you run this.** The supporting habits are documented
separately and in different places. Speak last and ask directly for dissent come
from notes on his DealBook Summit interview. Disagree and commit comes from
*Invent and Wander*. The library does not name this as a single ceremony. What
follows is a set of separately documented habits rendered as an ordered pass
over a real decision list.

### When to run it

- The member brought a stalled decision, or several.
- The member has been researching something for weeks without deciding.
- The member asked which of two options to pick.
- The member described being stuck, overwhelmed, or unable to start.

### The steps

1. **Get the full list out.** Ask them to write down every decision they are
   currently sitting on, including the ones they have been avoiding naming. The
   interview notes carry the line that stress is a signal you are avoiding an
   action, not that you are overloaded. Use that as the prompt. Do not proceed
   with a partial list.

2. **Label each one.** Could they walk back through this door within a month at
   survivable cost, or not? Expect the overwhelming majority to be reversible.
   The parallel drawn from Paul Graham's essays puts it at roughly ninety-nine
   percent. Where the member argues a decision is irreversible, make them say
   exactly how they would be trapped by it.

3. **Decide every two-way door today.** Not this week. For each one write: the
   decision in one line, today's date, and the date they will look at the
   result. Make the call yourself where you have enough from them, and say you
   are doing so.

4. **For each remaining one-way door, write the narrative memo.** Prose, not
   bullets, capped at a page or two. Bullets let you skip the connections you
   have not thought through. Write the first draft for them from their own
   material, and mark the paragraphs where the prose exposed a gap.

5. **Apply the seventy percent rule to those one-way doors.** Write down what
   they actually know versus what they wish they knew. At roughly seventy
   percent, decide. Do not go looking for the last thirty. Being slow is
   expensive for certain, while being wrong is cheap if you correct well.

6. **Before anything is finalised with other people in the room:** the member
   gives their own opinion last, and asks three people closest to the customer
   what the stupidest part of the plan is. Bad news does not travel upward on
   its own. Write the exact question they should ask.

7. **Where the call belongs to someone else and the member disagrees:** say
   plainly that they disagree and back the person anyway, rather than making
   them win the argument first.

8. **Set up the log.** At each checkpoint date, record what actually happened
   and whether they course-corrected. Their ability to reverse a bad call is
   what makes the seventy percent rule safe, and it is a skill they need
   evidence of. Give them the log format, dated, in the artifact.

### The artifact

A one-page decision log. Every open decision labelled reversible or
irreversible. The reversible ones decided and dated, each with a checkpoint
date. The irreversible ones each carrying a short written narrative. A running
record at the bottom for which calls they later reversed.

### Pre-revenue translation

Almost every decision paralysing a pre-revenue founder is a two-way door wearing
a costume. The name. The price. The landing page copy. The channel. The
platform. The first ten people to talk to. All of those can change next month.

The genuinely irreversible ones at this stage are few: taking money, giving away
equity, signing a multi-year commitment, publicly burning a relationship. Those
are the only ones that earn a memo.

The front line for the dissent step is the five people closest to their
prospective customer, or the customers themselves.

### When to refuse

- **Refuse to grant one-way-door treatment to a decision the member cannot
  explain how they would be trapped by.** Deliberation is a limited budget and
  spending it on a reversible choice is the specific failure this sort exists to
  prevent.
- **Refuse to run steps 6 and 7 for a solo founder with nobody else in the
  decision.** Say so plainly. Do not invent a solo version of disagree and
  commit or of speaking last. Skip the steps and name why they were skipped.

## 3. The weekend store walk and the rivets pass

### What it is

A standing habit, documented directly. Bezos walked the Amazon site on early
weekend mornings, and teams would wake to five or six messages about what he had
found. The standing rule: an idea he sent did not have to be implemented, but it
did have to be evaluated and the evaluation reported back.

It pairs with the standard he set for an executive using the Howard Hughes
rivets scene. That executive's job was now to run his fingers over every new
product looking for anything that degrades quality, and to make his own reports
do the same.

Source: *Working Backwards*.

**Honesty line, say it when you run this.** The walk and the rivets standard are
documented separately in the same source and are rendered here as one ordered
pass. Step 7's Kaizen story comes from *Invent and Wander*, not from this
passage.

### When to run it

- The member has something a prospect can already touch. A landing page, a
  waitlist form, a DM script, a demo, an onboarding flow, a document.
- The member asked what is wrong with their page, funnel, or product.
- The member is about to add a feature to something that already exists.

### The steps

1. **Book the hour.** Same hour every week. The documented version is early
   weekend morning, before anything else claims the day. Put a specific day and
   time in the artifact, not a vague weekly commitment.

2. **Walk it as a stranger.** Go through their product the way a first-time
   visitor would, from the very first thing they see — the ad, the post, the
   link — through to the moment they get what they came for. Do not skip the
   parts they have seen a thousand times. Those are where the defects live. Ask
   them to paste each step if you do not have it.

3. **Run the rivets pass.** Every button, every word, every link, every colour.
   Write down each defect as you find it, unfiltered, without deciding yet
   whether it matters. Number them. Do not editorialise while listing.

4. **Rank the list and name the single worst moment in the experience.** One bad
   experience undoes hundreds of perfect ones, so this one gets fixed before any
   new feature ships. Say which one it is and why it beat the others.

5. **Fix that one this week.** Specify the fix concretely enough that they could
   do it Tuesday. Send the rest of the list to whoever owns each area, including
   the member themselves, in writing, if that is everyone.

6. **Apply the standing rule to every remaining item.** It does not have to be
   implemented, but it does have to be evaluated and the evaluation written
   back. This is what stops the walk becoming a wish list nobody reads. Put an
   evaluation column in the artifact and fill in the ones you can.

7. **For any defect that has now appeared twice, remove the cause instead of
   staffing the response.** The test is the Kaizen story: an expert asked Bezos
   why he was cleaning the fulfillment center instead of eliminating the source
   of the dirt. Name the source of the dirt.

8. **Once a month, the member does the customer-facing work themselves.**
   Answering the messages, running the support, taking the calls. Amazon's
   Customer Connection required corporate employees to work as service agents
   every two years, and Bezos was not exempt from his own recertification. Put a
   date on it.

### The artifact

A dated defect list, numbered and ranked. One worst-experience fix, specified
concretely enough to ship this week. A written evaluation of every item the
member chose not to fix, and why.

### Pre-revenue translation

Their store is whatever a prospect can currently touch. A landing page, a
waitlist form, a DM script, a demo, a document. Walk it every weekend before
there are customers, because right now the member is the only person who will
find these defects, and later they will cost the first ten.

They are also automatically the frontline. The andon-cord idea in the source
gives frontline staff the power to halt a broken process and require a
root-cause fix. That authority is already theirs by default. Tell them to use
it: halt and fix the root cause rather than working around it.

### When to refuse

- **Refuse to run this as a substitute for talking to customers.** It audits the
  experience they have built. It cannot tell them whether anyone wants it.
- **If the member has no customers and no evidence of demand, say plainly that
  the store walk is the wrong tool this week and route them to the PR/FAQ
  instead.** Polishing a thing no stranger has ever wanted is the most
  comfortable way to waste a month.

## 4. The blind value audit against the rival's own list

### What it is

One full run of this is documented, along with the standing policy behind it.
Amazon audited its own everyday-low-price claim by pricing a competitor's own
published list of 100 bestsellers. It took six hours and four superstores to
find them all, and came to $1,561 there against $1,195 at Amazon.

The load-bearing detail: the list came from the rival, not from Amazon, so the
comparison could not be cherry-picked.

It pairs with the standing policy of offering low prices broadly and every day
rather than discounting a few items temporarily, and then auditing whether you
actually do.

Source: *Invent and Wander*.

### When to run it

- The member brought a price, or asked what to charge.
- The member asked how to compete with someone bigger or cheaper.
- The member claims to be better value than an alternative and has not checked.

### The steps

1. **Pick the real alternative.** Not the one they would most like to beat. The
   one their customer would actually use instead of them, which may be a free
   tool, a spreadsheet, or doing nothing. Make them say which, and push back if
   they pick a flattering rival.

2. **Take the rival's published list** of what they sell most or promote
   hardest. The rival's list, not the member's. If the member assembles the
   comparison set themselves, the audit is worthless and you should not run it.

3. **Get the real numbers the way a customer would**, at full effort, and record
   how much effort it took. The six hours and four superstores in the documented
   account is part of the finding, not an aside. If the member has to click
   through four pages and email someone to learn a price, write that down as a
   line in the comparison.

4. **Lay the two side by side, line by line.** Price. What is included. What is
   excluded. How long it takes. What the customer has to do themselves.

5. **Find every place the member is charging more or promising less simply
   because they can get away with it right now.** List them. Do not soften this
   section.

6. **Decide what they give back.** The mechanic is to return efficiency gains to
   the customer as lower prices rather than to margin. Lower prices bring more
   visits, more volume, more third-party sellers, better absorption of fixed
   costs, which enables further cuts. Measure volume over a full year, not a
   quarter.

7. **Set one everyday standard across the whole range** instead of running
   periodic discounts on a few items. Write it down so they can be held to it.

8. **Publish the honest comparison, including where the alternative wins.**
   Negative reviews cost sales in the short term, but helping customers decide
   well pays off. He let customers post negative reviews on the purchase page
   while vendors questioned whether he understood retail. Draft the published
   comparison with the losses left in.

9. **Refuse to cherry-pick.** If the finding is that they lose, the finding is
   that they lose. Write it that way.

### The artifact

A line-by-line comparison table built from the competitor's own published list.
A written decision on what changes. And one publishable honest comparison that
includes where the member loses.

### Pre-revenue translation

They have no prices to cut yet, which makes this the cheapest version of the
audit they will ever run. Do it before setting a price rather than after.

The competitor's own published list is free. It is their pricing page, their
menu, their bestseller list, their feature comparison.

What is being tested is whether the thing they are about to charge for is
undeniably more valuable than what the customer can already get, and whether
they could say so in a comparison they would be willing to publish with the
losses left in.

### When to refuse

- **Refuse to produce the comparison if the member selected the comparison set
  themselves.** Send them back for the rival's own published list. A
  self-selected set produces a flattering answer and no information.
- **Refuse to recommend a price cut as a growth tactic for someone with no
  customers.** The mechanic depends on volume absorbing fixed costs over a full
  year, and there is no volume yet to absorb anything. Say that plainly.

## Out of reach at pre-revenue

Say so rather than faking a small-scale version. These come up and there is no
honest translation:

- **Single-threaded leadership as an organisational design.** It needs leaders
  the member does not have. Only the negative rule transfers: idea validation
  cannot be a part-time job competing with three other ideas. The
  dependency-mapping and API-boundary material alongside it does not reach a
  solo founder.
- **Day Two decay.** A large-company disease. The member does not have it. Only
  the proxy test transfers: catch yourself defending a week with no customer
  contact on the grounds that you followed your process.
- **Frugality as a method.** Named as one of his repeated principles and never
  developed into a mechanic anywhere in this material. There is no Bezos
  frugality procedure to run. Do not invent one.
- **The hiring bar.** A high bar and three screening questions are documented,
  and nothing else. A member with no employees gets the three questions and no
  more. Do not build a hiring system out of that.
