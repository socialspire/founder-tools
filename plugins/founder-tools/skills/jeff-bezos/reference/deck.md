## Thesis

A documentation machine bolted to a decision-speed machine.

The first half: you are forbidden to reason forward from what you are already
good at. You define the customer's experience first, write it up as though it
has already launched, and only then reason back to what has to be built. That
reliably exposes capabilities you do not have and forces you to go get them.
The written artifact is the thinking. Slides are banned outright for serious
internal decisions, because bullet lists let you gloss over ideas and hide how
things connect, and a short narrative under a hard page limit does not.
Spreadsheet-and-market-sizing pitches got sent back in favour of a mockup of
the actual experience.

The second half: most decisions are two-way doors. Sort them, clear the
reversible ones fast, decide at roughly seventy percent of the information you
wish you had rather than waiting for ninety, and schedule the checkpoint.

Around those two mechanics sits a small handful of principles he restated for
over twenty years, and a refusal reflex: no undifferentiated market entry, no
copycat product, no mediocre either/or, no rehearsed meeting, no proxy standing
in for a result. Difficulty was the filter, not the warning sign. The whole
apparatus exists to make speed and invention the default instead of
coordination and copying.

## Principles

**1. Define the customer outcome first, then reason backwards to what to build — never forwards from what you already do well.** *(Working Backwards; the skills-forward description comes from Invent and Wander)*
The contrast is drawn sharply. Working forward optimises for the company.
Working backwards optimises for the customer. It is described as the mechanism
by which value compounds: the longer you do it, the more capabilities you
accumulate and the more you can invent, which the source calls exercising new
muscles. The failure mode is the skills-forward company, which keeps asking
what else it can do with what it already has, never develops fresh
competencies, and eventually finds those skills outmoded.
→ Take the thing you are currently building and rewrite its description as a
customer outcome with your own skills removed from the sentence. Then list what
that outcome now requires of you.

**2. Write the launch announcement for the thing before you build the thing.** *(Working Backwards)*
The named tool is the PR/FAQ. A press release for a product that does not exist
yet, written as though it is launching, plus an FAQ answering the hard
questions behind it. The format is credited with forcing the Kindle team onto a
great screen, easy buying and downloading, huge selection and low prices,
rather than onto whatever Amazon happened to be capable of building.
→ Write the one-page launch announcement for your idea this week, before you
write a line of code or spend a dollar on it.

**3. Set a hard length limit and treat the limit itself as the thinking tool.** *(Working Backwards; the week-long memo detail comes from Invent and Wander)*
One page or less for the press release, roughly five for the FAQ. The
restriction is not administrative tidiness. The stated purpose is that the
limit develops better thinkers and communicators, who then build better
products. The companion warning: Amazon's weak six-page memos came from people
believing a great one takes a few hours, when a great memo is written, shared,
set aside and rewritten over a week or more.
→ Cap your next proposal at one page and cut until it fits. Budget a week of
drafting and resting, not an evening.

**4. Ban slides for any serious decision and write a narrative instead.** *(Working Backwards)*
He mandated short written narratives in a single email. The reasoning: a
four-page memo is harder to write than a twenty-page deck, because narrative
structure forces you to work out what matters most and how the pieces connect.
Bullet lists let you skip past ideas, flatten relative importance, and hide the
interconnections you have not actually thought through.
→ Convert your pitch deck or bullet plan into flowing prose sentences, and note
every place the prose exposes a connection you had not worked out.

**5. Read your own document as an adversary — assume every sentence is wrong until you can prove it.** *(Working Backwards)*
This is how he pulled insights out of documents everyone else had already read.
He challenged the content rather than the writer's motive, and he was usually
among the last in the room to finish reading. The instruction that follows is
to mark every claim you cannot verify.
→ Reread your landing page or plan line by line and underline every sentence
you could not defend to a hostile stranger with evidence you actually hold.

**6. Sort every decision into one-way doors and two-way doors, and never run one process for both.** *(Invent and Wander; the ninety-nine percent figure is a parallel drawn from Paul Graham's essays, not from Bezos)*
Most decisions are reversible. The error aging companies make is treating
reversible decisions as permanent. The parallel puts the proportion at roughly
ninety-nine percent reversible. Two-way doors deserve speed. One-way doors
deserve analysis.
→ List every decision you are sitting on, label each reversible or not, and
clear every reversible one before the end of the day.

**7. Decide at roughly seventy percent of the information you wish you had, then schedule the checkpoint.** *(Working Backwards)*
Waiting for ninety percent is described plainly as being slow. The trade: if
you are good at recognising and reversing a bad call, being wrong costs less
than you think, while being slow is expensive for certain. The paired action is
not just to decide but to book the date on which you will look at the result
and correct.
→ Name the decision you have been researching for two weeks, make it today, and
write the date you will check whether it was wrong.

**8. Refuse to enter a market where you cannot say how you are meaningfully different.** *(Invent and Wander)*
The documented instance is physical retail. An ancient, well-served category
where he had no idea how to make the experience meaningfully different, so he
stayed out of it for fifteen years. The kill rule that follows: being able to
enter a market was never on its own a reason to enter it.
→ Kill any idea on your list whose only argument is that the market is large or
that you are capable of entering it.

**9. A competitor having it is not a reason to build it — name the unique value or kill the item.** *(Working Backwards)*
He repeatedly rejected proposals as copycat thinking, insisting every product
carry a genuinely unique value proposition, on the grounds that the company
existed to create experiences that did not yet exist.
→ Write one sentence per feature or offer naming what it does that no
alternative does, and delete every one where you cannot finish the sentence.

**10. When handed a choice between two mediocre options, reject the frame and specify the outcome that requires a capability you do not have.** *(Working Backwards)*
Presented with slow-and-free versus fast-and-expensive, he refused the trade.
Through a customer lens the answer was fast and free, even though fulfillment
could not yet deliver it. The sharp line: most skills-forward companies stop at
the point of admitting they are not capable, and this is exactly where Amazon
started.
→ Write down the either/or you have accepted about your product, state the
outcome the customer actually wants, and list what you would have to learn to
deliver it.

**11. Bring a mockup, not a spreadsheet — a half-baked mockup is evidence of half-baked thinking.** *(Working Backwards)*
He rejected MBA-style pitches built on market sizing, financial models and
projected margins, and asked instead where the mockups were. Then he
interrogated every button, word, link and colour, and asked how the service
beat iTunes. The artifact under review is the experience itself, not the
forecast.
→ Build a clickable or drawn mockup of the actual customer experience before
your next conversation about the idea. Leave the market-size slide at home.

**12. One bad customer experience undoes hundreds of perfect ones, so fix the worst one before you add anything new.** *(Working Backwards)*
This is the reason the operating rule from day one was that it has to be
perfect. The instruction that follows is direct: find the single worst
experience in your product and fix it before adding any new feature. It is a
sequencing rule, not a quality slogan.
→ Name the single worst moment in your current customer experience and fix that
this week instead of shipping the next feature on your list.

**13. Nobody wants your technology. They want what it enables. Keep the technology out of the first sentence.** *(no book — notes on his DealBook Summit interview with Andrew Ross Sorkin)*
The analogy: when the world was being wired, nobody asked for electricity. They
asked for lighting, and the appliances came later. The same source argues that
when the basics of a category are still missing you are early, and early is the
best place to be, because the equivalent of the off switch and the wall outlet
has not been invented yet.
→ Rewrite your headline and first paragraph so no tool, platform, model or
technology name appears until the reader already knows what changes for them.

**14. Separate math decisions from judgment decisions, and stop demanding proof for the second kind.** *(Invent and Wander)*
The documented case is pricing. Price elasticity data said raise prices,
because short-term volume never pays for a cut. But that data only covers the
short horizon, and nobody can numerically estimate what consistently lower
prices do over ten years. He labelled it a judgment call and went against the
math. The corollary: math-based decisions command wide agreement while
judgment-based ones get debated, and that is the nature of the category rather
than a defect.
→ Split your open decisions into two columns, math and judgment, and stop
trying to find data for the judgment column.

**15. Study individual anecdotes in full, not survey averages — and be the person who personally loves the product.** *(Invent and Wander; the data-and-anecdotes pairing comes from Working Backwards)*
A remarkable customer experience starts with heart, intuition, curiosity, play,
guts and taste. None of that shows up in a survey. Good inventors study many
anecdotes rather than only aggregate scores, and the product owner has to
personally love the offering. Data and anecdotes are powerful together, and
where they disagree, the disagreement is itself the signal.
→ Read fifty individual customer complaints, reviews or messages in full before
you look at another aggregate number.

**16. Pick a handful of principles and repeat them for twenty years until the organisation is built around them.** *(Working Backwards; the 1997-letter and self-selection details come from Invent and Wander)*
The handful he chose: customer obsession, invention, frugality, ownership, bias
for action, high standards, and under-promise and over-deliver. He never
stopped restating them. He appended the 1997 shareholder letter to every letter
that followed. The companion instruction is to publish the operating philosophy
plainly, so the wrong customers, hires and investors self-select out.
→ Write your five non-negotiable principles on one page, publish it where
prospective customers can read it, and stop restating anything else.

*Scale caveat: frugality is named as one of the repeated principles and never
developed into a mechanic. There is no documented frugality procedure in this
material — only the word in the list. Do not manufacture one.*

**17. Give the most important thing exactly one owner, full time — the surest way to fail at inventing is to make it someone's part-time job.** *(Working Backwards)*
Single-threaded leadership meant one leader per project, focused on that
project alone, with a team focused the same way. It is tied to Thiel making
every PayPal employee responsible for one unique thing, and it also reduced
internal conflict. The paired instruction: strip the second and third
priorities off your most important owner.
→ Name the one thing you are actually trying to prove this month and remove
every other project from your own calendar until it is answered.

*Scale caveat: the organisational version needs leaders you do not have. Solo,
the only thing that transfers is the negative rule — your idea validation
cannot be a part-time job competing with three other ideas. Pick one. The
dependency-mapping and API-boundary material alongside this does not reach you
yet.*

**18. Do not outsource the thing you need to become good at.** *(Working Backwards)*
Brick-and-mortar retailers outsourced their first e-commerce sites and moved
quickly, but lost the ability to innovate and differentiate, because they could
only pick from a vendor's menu. Building the Kindle in-house cost Amazon more
money and more time, and left them with skills they could reuse forever. The
discipline that keeps this from becoming dogma: classify each project as
differentiating or commodity, and buy the commodities.
→ List everything you plan to hand to a freelancer or agency, mark which items
are the actual source of your difference, and do those yourself even though it
is slower.

**19. Invest in the things that will not change, precisely because they feel boring.** *(no book — a recorded conversation with Jason Fried of 37signals, carrying advice Bezos gave Fried and David Heinemeier Hansson, relayed by Fried)*
Nobody will wake up in ten years wishing Amazon were slower, pricier, worse at
service, or narrower in selection. Those constants are worth investing in
precisely because they can start to feel boring.
→ Write down the three things your customers will still want in ten years and
check whether any of your current week's work is aimed at them.

**20. Day One is a posture you defend on purpose. Day Two is stasis, then irrelevance, then decline.** *(Invent and Wander)*
Day Two is a decay a large company can coast through in slow motion for decades
before the bill arrives. The named starter defences: customer obsession,
skepticism of proxies, eager adoption of external trends, and high-velocity
decision-making. The specific proxy warning is that process quietly becomes a
stand-in for the result. A junior leader defends a bad outcome by saying the
process was followed. An experienced one uses the bad outcome to fix the
process.
→ Find the one routine you are following because it is your routine rather than
because it produced a result, and either change it or stop running it.

*Scale caveat: the decay described here is a large-company disease and you do
not have it yet. What reaches you pre-revenue is the proxy test alone — catch
yourself defending a week of no customer contact on the grounds that you
followed your process.*

## What he refused

- Physical retail for fifteen years, despite an enormous and well-served
  market, because he had no idea how to make the experience meaningfully
  different. *(Invent and Wander)*
- Internal proposals he judged to be copycat thinking. He would not ship
  anything whose case rested on a competitor already having it, and demanded a
  genuinely unique value proposition for every product. *(Working Backwards)*
- The choice he was offered between slow-and-free shipping and
  fast-and-expensive shipping. He rejected the frame itself and specified fast
  and free, even though fulfillment could not yet do it. *(Working Backwards)*
- MBA-style pitches built on market sizing, financial models and projected
  margins. He asked where the mockups were, then interrogated every button,
  word, link and colour, and asked how the service beat iTunes.
  *(Working Backwards)*
- PowerPoint for serious internal decisions. Banned in a single email, decks
  replaced by short written narratives. *(Working Backwards)*
- A rehearsed meeting. When a team admitted rehearsing, he told them never to
  do it again — he wanted to be part of the sausage-making and see the ugly
  bits, not a pitch. *(no book — DealBook Summit interview notes)*
- Fighting Apple in music, where Amazon could not win. He looked past the
  category entirely, to ebooks and a reader device. *(Working Backwards)*
- A copycat crash program after Jobs demoed iTunes for Windows and predicted
  Amazon would be the last place selling CDs. He appointed a single-threaded
  leader for digital, reporting directly to him, instead. *(Working Backwards)*
- Letting the new digital media business sit inside the physical media
  business. The bigger business always wins attention, so the new one would
  never be the priority. *(Working Backwards)*
- Slack in an executive's quality standard. He made it that executive's job to
  run his fingers over every new product looking for anything that degrades
  quality, and to make his own reports do the same. *(Working Backwards)*
- A decision he knew was wrong taken for good press, and a right decision
  skipped out of fear of blowback. *(no book — DealBook Summit interview
  notes)*
- Overselling the odds when raising money. Fifty meetings to sell twenty
  percent for a million dollars, telling people he thought there was a seventy
  percent chance they would lose their money. *(no book — DealBook Summit
  interview notes)*
- Additional equity and a larger salary, on the grounds that he was already
  aligned. He kept a roughly eighty thousand dollar salary. *(no book —
  DealBook Summit interview notes)*
- Being talked out of Fulfillment by Amazon by the many people who tried. He
  held it as one of the few convictions he would not trade, while changing his
  mind readily on nearly everything else. *(no book — DealBook Summit interview
  notes)*
- Hiding negative information from customers. He let them post negative reviews
  on the purchase page while vendors questioned whether he understood retail,
  accepting lost sales in the short term for better decisions and more trust in
  the long term. *(Invent and Wander)*

## Not in these sources

These are the specific famous things this material does not contain. Decline
them plainly. Do not reconstruct them from memory.

- **That Amazon started as an online bookstore**, or any account of what Amazon
  originally sold at launch. Books appear as one of the categories on which
  prices were cut, and there is a mention of a compelling book-buying
  experience. Nothing states that Amazon began as a bookstore. If this skill
  says it, the claim came from general knowledge and not from the source.
- **The regret minimization framework.** The phrase does not appear. Do not
  attribute it to him.
- **The founding story in any form.** Quitting a hedge fund, D. E. Shaw, the
  drive to Seattle, the garage, the door desk, a 1994 founding date, a 1997
  IPO, the company's original name, or how the name Amazon was chosen. None of
  it is here.
- **The two-pizza team rule.** Not here. What is documented is single-threaded
  leadership and dependency removal, which is a different mechanic. Use that.
- **The Bar Raiser hiring program.** Not here. What is documented is a high
  hiring bar and three screening questions: will you admire this person, will
  they raise the group's average effectiveness, and on what dimension might
  they be a superstar.
- **The line about your margin being my opportunity.** Not here in any form.
- **The flywheel as a napkin sketch or a famous diagram.** Across this material
  the word appears once, as an instruction to map your own, and the growth loop
  is described in prose. No napkin, no canonical diagram.
- **Any acquisition.** Whole Foods, Zappos, Twitch, MGM, Audible, Ring. No
  Amazon acquisition by Bezos is recorded here.
- **The Washington Post purchase and anything about media ownership.**
- **Blue Origin, beyond a single point.** Only two things are recorded: he funds
  it with Amazon stock so it carries less financing risk than early Amazon did,
  and he has wanted to move polluting industry off Earth since writing about it
  in his high school newspaper. No rockets, no vehicle names, no launches, no
  space tourism, no rivalry with anyone.
- **Stepping down as CEO**, the 2021 transition, his successor, or the
  executive chairman role.
- **His personal life.** Marriage, divorce, settlement, partner, philanthropy,
  any foundation, any richest-person ranking. There is one wealth passage — his
  roughly $200B stake against Amazon's roughly $2.3T market cap, meaning
  roughly $2.1T of value went to other people — used to make a point about
  value created for others. Nothing else.
- **Amazon's fourteen leadership principles as a list.** The material says only
  that they arguably collapse into customer obsession, and separately names a
  different handful he repeated. Do not enumerate the fourteen.
- **Frugality as a developed mechanic.** Named as one of the repeated
  principles and nothing more. No door desks, no expense rules, no frugality
  stories. If asked for the Bezos frugality method, say this library does not
  contain one.
- **Amazon revenue, profit, headcount, market share, Prime subscriber count, or
  Prime pricing.** The only figures here are: the Kindle at $399 holding 200
  books and selling out in under six hours; over a hundred million Echo units;
  an 80% stock decline; eleven years of losses; the $1,561 versus $1,195 price
  comparison; roughly 70% of revenue being physical media at the time of the
  digital-media split; and Amazon's roughly $2.3T market cap. Nothing beyond
  those.
- **Anything about running a business at pre-revenue scale**, with no employees
  or no company. This limit is stated explicitly in the source material.
  Nothing on solo founders. Nothing on legal structure, tax, or modern
  acquisition channels. Everything documented is written from inside a large or
  well-funded company. Every translation to a solo pre-revenue founder in this
  skill is an adaptation, not something the library says he did. Say that when
  it matters.
