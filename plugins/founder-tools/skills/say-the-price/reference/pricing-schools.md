# The Three Pricing Schools

The library does not agree with itself about pricing. Three genuinely opposed
philosophies each produced enormous companies. Anyone who tells you there is one
right answer has read one of them.

What separates them is not taste. It is conditions. Each school won where specific
things were true, and lost where they were not. This file is the conditions.

**Attribution rules apply throughout.** Everything here is paraphrased. Nothing is
in quotation marks. Material from the Founders Podcast extractions names the book
and author. Material from David Senra's interviews with living founders names the
show and the episode date.

## SCHOOL A — Public, flat, the same for everyone

### The doctrine

Cap the top price so no single account becomes load-bearing, and build for the
base instead of the outliers. Durability comes from many small customers, not a
few large ones.

Jason Fried wants his customers to look like the dots of TV static, all the same
size, so losing ten random ones changes nothing. He concedes 37signals leaves money
on the table and does not optimize pricing. His response to that gap is
indifference. He calls enough a legitimate finish line.

The mechanism that makes flat pricing safe is cost. Your costs are the only
competitor you can actually fight. Rivals will price and ship whatever they want
regardless of what you do. Your cost structure is the one variable fully under
your control.

The most usable version of this for a founder with no customers yet: low costs
shrink the number of people who have to like you. Compute how many customers your
current cost base requires, then cut costs until that number feels findable.

Rose Blumkin's version was a fixed markup rather than a cap. Ten percent over cost
from the beginning, never drifting upward, permanent rather than promotional. She
would produce the supplier's bill to show a customer her actual cost.

Sol Price's version was structural simplicity. Keep policy simple enough that you
stop thinking about it. Opening in a lower-cost city, he paid the same wage as
everywhere else. Same work, same pay, decision closed.

DHH runs the same underlying logic on the cost side. Costs are inside your control
and revenue is not, so attack costs religiously. He admits real satisfaction from
killing a two-thousand-dollar monthly line item. Senra supplies the Carnegie
formulation: prices and profits are cyclical, cost savings are permanent.

### Conditions under which School A won

- Many small customers of similar size, none of them load-bearing.
- A low or controllable cost base, so a findable number of customers is already
  enough.
- The buyer and the user are the same person. Fried's point is that enterprise
  software is bought by someone who then makes other people use it, which is why
  those products are hated. When buyer and user are the same human, they demand
  things that work.
- The founder wants durability and independence more than maximum extraction.
- Verifiable honesty is available as a differentiator. You can actually show your
  cost.

### Receipts

> Basecamp is capped at $299 a month no matter how many seats, and Jason Fried
> turns down would-be $50,000-a-month enterprise deals to keep it that way. He
> wants every customer the same size so losing ten of them changes nothing. (David
> Senra podcast, 2026-02-15)

> Rose Blumkin sold at ten percent over cost from the beginning and never drifted
> upward. Competitors sued her over carpet priced at $3.95 against their $7.95. She
> told the judge she sold at ten percent over cost and asked what was wrong with
> that. The case was thrown out and the judge bought carpet from her the next day.
> (The Women of Berkshire Hathaway, Karen Linder)

> Jason Fried sold his teenage software to a few thousand people at twenty dollars
> and cleared roughly twenty thousand a year with no overhead. (David Senra
> podcast, 2026-02-15)

> Les Schwab's biggest early customer owed him roughly his entire net worth. Their
> bankruptcy would have ended the company. (Les Schwab: Pride In Performance, Les
> Schwab)

### The honest counter-evidence

The library is not unanimous. In the art market, publicly reported prices come
mostly from auctions, estimated at under twenty percent of the total market, and
that opacity creates room to operate. The same file pairs this with Ogilvy's line
that pricing is guesswork.

Do not pretend this does not exist. It is documented, and it argues against
publishing your number.

> Publicly reported art prices come mostly from auctions, estimated at under twenty
> percent of the market. (How Larry Gagosian Reshaped The Art World, Patrick Radden
> Keefe, New Yorker profile)

## SCHOOL B — Premium, and never discount

### The doctrine

Brunello Cucinelli took his cornerstone from Theodore Levitt: developed economies
cannot win on average products, because cheaper countries will always learn to
make the mediocre thing for less. The only defensible position is high quality. He
called this the cornerstone of his entire entrepreneurial mindset.

His target was what he called absolute luxury. Expensive but never overpriced.
Priced high because Italian craftsmanship genuinely cost that much, not because
the market would tolerate the number. His test is the sharpest one in the library:
justify your price by naming the specific cost of quality behind it. If you cannot,
your price is a tax on your customer rather than a signal.

Ralph Lauren's version was to build value in and then raise the price. He argued a
better product does not split a fixed market, it expands it.

Hans Wilsdorf's version carries the qualifier that makes School B honest. Quality
was a survival strategy, not a virtue. Being the most precise watch in the world
was the only thing that could make customers demand his name instead of the
retailer's. Choose the single quality dimension your customers can verify, and be
measurably best at it. Premium positioning in that file always rests on something
the buyer can check.

The Louis Vuitton version, from the Arnault biography: do the opposite of what
low-quality competitors do. Rivals owned prestige storefronts and outsourced
production cheaply. Henry Racamier inverted it, manufacturing in France for
uncompromising quality and selling into the growing Asian market. The usable form
is to find the corner every competitor is cutting, refuse to cut it, and charge for
the difference. (The Taste of Luxury: Bernard Arnault and the Moët-Hennessy Louis
Vuitton Story, Nadège Forestier and Nazanine Ravaï)

John Mackey's version: compete on the axis your competitors have abandoned.
Supermarkets answered Walmart by cutting lighting, capital, labor and service,
playing Walmart's game and still losing. He refused price entirely and took
quality, service and product mix.

Charlie Munger's version is the one that matters most to a founder with no name
yet. Specialization is a barrier to entry. Do what everyone does and you compete
head to head forever. Specialize and you are removed from the crowd. The harder
the specialty is to acquire, the higher the wall, which is why the shop that only
works on one make of car charges double and gets it.

Seth Godin's version: price is a signal. Lowering your price makes you less
trusted. People pay more for things that are different, not for more of the same
thing.

### Conditions under which School B won

- Your cost base cannot win a price fight, and the low-cost producer will always
  learn to make the average thing for less.
- The quality difference is real and the customer can verify it.
- Competitors are all cutting the same corner.
- The buyer's own status or identity is part of what they are buying. Ralph Lauren
  sold a way of life and the garment was the entry ticket.
- You have a name that occupies space in the customer's mind with no direct
  substitute.
- You are narrow enough that comparison is hard.

### Receipts

> Ties in Ralph Lauren's market sold for three or four dollars. His started at $7.50
> wholesale and retailed at $15. His argument was that a better product does not
> split a fixed market, it expands it. (Ralph Lauren: The Man Behind the Mystique,
> Jeffrey A. Trachtenberg)

> Munger and Buffett say they did not understand brand power until See's Candies,
> where they found they could raise prices annually and nobody objected. They named
> the category consumer monopolies, and learning it changed the whole enterprise.
> (The Tao of Charlie Munger, David Clark)

> Larry Gagosian's first instruction to a new painter was that her work was too
> cheap for his buyers. (How Larry Gagosian Reshaped The Art World, Patrick Radden
> Keefe, New Yorker profile)

> Rolex ran an intensive campaign costing over twelve thousand pounds a year, for
> years, in a category where watchmakers did not advertise to the public at all.
> It inverted the power relationship with retailers and gave Hans Wilsdorf control
> over wholesale pricing. (Rolex Jubilee Vade Mecum, Vol. 1, Hans Wilsdorf)

> Bloomingdale's offered Ralph Lauren the order he needed on one condition: his
> label off, their house label on, and an inch off the tie. Broke, newly married,
> sleeping on a mattress on the floor, he packed up and left. (Ralph Lauren: The
> Man Behind the Mystique, Jeffrey A. Trachtenberg)

### The failure mode of this school

Premium without verifiability is a claim, not a position. If the buyer has to take
your word for the quality, you are asking them to pay for a story. Wilsdorf's whole
mechanism was the verification.

## SCHOOL C — Low price as strategy

### The doctrine

Sol Price ran a twelve percent markup against competitors at thirty-six to fifty
percent, and it was not generosity. It was an alignment machine. The profit came
from membership fees instead. He found customer demand more sensitive to price than
to selection, which inverts retail's conventional wisdom. He also took what he
called the intelligent loss of sales: stock only the best-value version, and treat
the customers who walk as a deliberate, acceptable loss.

Henry Ford reduced the price first and then forced the cost down to meet it. He
treated no cost as fixed. Set the price where you believe the volume appears, then
go invent the manufacturing that makes it possible. He targeted a minimum of waste
and a minimum of profit per car, depending on volume for the total.

Note on Ford: the library file itself carries the caveat that he held documented
antisemitic views and that only his business method is extracted. Use the method,
keep it brief, and do not lionize the man.

Sam Walton's version: a simple idea taken to a ridiculous extreme beats a clever
idea executed loosely. Buy low, sell low, every day, with service. Munger's read is
that dominant systems push one or two variables to an almost absurd maximum. And
cost discipline is what lets you survive your own mistakes. You can make many
mistakes and still recover if you run efficiently, or be brilliant and go under if
you are not.

Jeff Bezos returned efficiency gains to customers as lower prices to start a
virtuous cycle. Lower prices bring more visits, more volume, more third-party
sellers, better absorption of fixed costs, enabling further cuts. He took this from
a single coffee with Costco's Jim Sinegal. His discipline: offer low prices broadly
and every day rather than discounting a few items temporarily.

Herb Kelleher's version: a lower cost structure is a market-expansion machine, not
just a margin. And know what you compete with, not just who. And the discipline
that keeps low price from becoming a death spiral is to chase profit rather than
market share. He preferred four percent of a market with profits to ninety percent
without.

### Conditions under which School C won

- You hold a structural cost advantage nobody can copy quickly. A single aircraft
  type. A process advantage compounded over twenty years. Fewer product lines.
- Demand is genuinely elastic, and the cut expands the total market rather than
  splitting it.
- You make the profit somewhere other than the markup.
- You can hold the low number permanently rather than promotionally.

### The rule that separates School C from panic

In every documented case, the low price came after a cost advantage, or the founder
committed to inventing the cost advantage to meet a price he had already set.

**Nobody in the library won by lowering a price because a prospect pushed back.**

### Receipts

> Sol Price ran a twelve percent markup against competitors' thirty-six to fifty
> percent, and made the profit on membership fees instead, twenty-five dollars a
> year for access to your own warehouse. (Sol Price: Retail Revolutionary and Social
> Innovator, Robert Price)

> Rivals charged sixty-two dollars on a route where Southwest charged fifteen,
> because Herb Kelleher understood he was competing with cars and buses rather than
> with airlines. (Nuts!: Southwest Airlines' Crazy Recipe for Business and Personal
> Success, Kevin and Jackie Freiberg)

> Cutting fares into one region took Southwest's annual passengers from 123,000 to
> 325,000. The increase alone exceeded the entire prior market. (Nuts!, Kevin and
> Jackie Freiberg)

> Southwest flew one aircraft type, which put it at eighty-four employees per
> aircraft against an industry range of a hundred and fifteen to a hundred and
> sixty. (Nuts!, Kevin and Jackie Freiberg)

> Henry Ford treated no cost as fixed. Cutting from two thousand and one thousand
> dollars to seven hundred and fifty and six hundred took him from roughly fifteen
> hundred cars sold to roughly eighty-five hundred. (My Life and Work, Henry Ford)

> Told to raise the price of the Costco hot dog and soda, Jim Sinegal refused and
> told the man to figure it out. It is still $1.50, a price inherited from Price
> Club and held for decades. (Sol Price: Retail Revolutionary and Social Innovator,
> Robert Price)

> Amazon priced a competitor's own published list of a hundred bestsellers. Six
> hours and four superstores to find them all, at $1,561, against $1,195 at Amazon.
> (Invent and Wander: The Collected Writings of Jeff Bezos)

### The warning that goes with this school

Expect to be attacked for charging too little. Sol Price was sued by California over
a twelve percent liquor markup. His first pharmacist faced expulsion from
professional bodies, blocked wholesalers, and death threats.

And one cautionary note the library is explicit about: Samuel Insull inverted his
industry's pricing logic, treating a product everyone sold as a luxury as a
necessity to be sold low, and priced so low that customers could not justify going
without him. He then destroyed fifty-three years of that work by treating borrowed
money as a permanent resource. The collapse is attributed to leverage rather than
to pricing. Do not present Insull as a pricing success story without that context.
(Insull: The Rise and Fall of A Billionaire Utility Tycoon, Forrest McDonald)

## The three deciding questions

One test per school. Run them in this order.

**1. Where is your cost position?**
No structural cost advantage means School C is not available to you. The lower-cost
producer will always learn to make the average thing for less.
(Cucinelli, via Levitt)

**2. Can the customer verify your quality difference?**
If not, School B is a claim rather than a position.
(Wilsdorf)

**3. Would one lost customer break you?**
If yes, School A's cap logic is the priority regardless of what level you price at.
(Fried)

## Failure modes the library documents

### Pricing from fear

**Nobody owning the number.** Rose Blumkin's husband sold clothing at exactly what
he paid for it. She stepped in at forty-three with the ten percent rule and the
business became the business.

**Letting the least confident person in the room set it.** Dyson's washing machine
cost far more to build, with two drums, two motors and a gearbox, and launched
above competitors. Marketing said a lower price would move more volume. He listened,
sales did not move, and losses grew. He believes it should have gone up, and says it
was the last time in his life he listened to them on that question. (David Senra
podcast, 2025-12-07)

**Underselling because nobody has told you otherwise.** Gagosian's first instruction
to a new painter was that her work was too cheap.

**Fear is visible and it repels.** Fried's point: PR review, legal review and terror
of having an opinion produce businesses that are afraid of themselves. People would
rather deal with people. (David Senra podcast, 2026-02-15)

### Pricing off competitors

**Copying the leader with a slight tweak seeds nothing.** DHH's first attempt at
Linux tried to look like a cheaper Mac or Windows, on the theory that familiarity
would win converts. It did not. Nobody wants a discount clone. (David Senra
podcast, 2026-07-26)

**Playing the giant's game on the giant's axis.** Supermarkets answered Walmart by
cutting lighting, capital, labor and service, and still lost. (David Senra podcast,
2026-01-04)

**Reactive strategy is a symptom of losing.** Price Club stopped following a plan
and started responding to competitors' moves.

**Price competition is what makes a business bad.** Munger's view: bad businesses
sit in intensely competitive industries that fight on price, which crushes margins,
kills cash flow, and shortens lifespan. He and Buffett learned this owning a
department store, a windmill maker, a textile mill and an airline.

**Be terrified of customers, not competitors.** Bezos's framing, because competitors
never send you money.

### Discounting at the first resistance

**Lowering your price makes you less trusted.** (This Is Marketing, Seth Godin)

**Shaving the fee is looking through the wrong end of the telescope.** Ogilvy's
clients haggled over his fifteen percent while the real leverage sat in the
eighty-five percent they spent on media.

**Small cuts compound into a product nobody wants.** Todd Graves calls it death by a
thousand cuts. (David Senra podcast, 2025-11-09)

**Discount as an event is the mistake. A permanent structure is not.** Blumkin's ten
percent never drifted. Bezos offered low prices broadly and every day rather than
discounting a few items temporarily.

**Growth without cost visibility is how good companies die.** Ralph Lauren's sales
tripled from $3.8 million to $8 million and he believed he was profitable. He had
actually lost about forty-five thousand dollars, with no systems for accounting,
credit, returns, or even sick days.

### Never stating the number

**Hiding behind the euphemism.** Peers called themselves gallerists. Gagosian
insisted on dealer, because the softer word hid the fact that money has to change
hands.

**Vagueness reads as inoffensive, and inoffensive does not sell.** Ogilvy's rule:
you cannot bore a customer into buying, you can only interest them.

**Rationing your own visibility while you are still unknown.** Ogilvy's defense of
his self-promotion was that being poor, unknown and in a hurry justified it, and
that behaving more professionally would have cost him twenty years he did not have.

**Screening prospects out on appearance before they can say yes.** A salesperson
told Estée Lauder not to waste time on a barefoot woman in a San Antonio store. She
did the makeover anyway. The woman bought two of everything, and her relatives came
back the next day.

## The value floor, assembled

Seven steps. Every one is documented.

1. **Name the buyer's outcome in their words, not your deliverable.** Henry Ford's
   position was that business exists to deliver service and profit is a byproduct.
   He used the word service and its variants nearly two hundred times in one book.
   (My Life and Work, Henry Ford)

2. **Name the one number that proves they got it.** Adam Foroughi's operating idea
   at AppLovin is to make your customer an arbitrager: they put in a thousand
   dollars and know with certainty they made more than a thousand back, on the
   schedule their own business requires. Rewrite your primary internal metric so it
   cannot improve unless your customer's economics improve. (David Senra podcast,
   2026-05-03)

3. **Total what they currently spend on the problem, including hidden cost.** Ford
   argued horses did not earn their keep once you counted feeding, tending and
   cleanup. Put that number in your pitch. (My Life and Work, Henry Ford)

4. **Name the specific cost of quality behind your number.** If you cannot, the
   number is a tax on your customer rather than a signal. (The Dream of Solomeo,
   Brunello Cucinelli)

5. **Test the word undeniable against your own answer.** If the honest answer is
   arguably rather than undeniably, fix the product. (Invent and Wander, Jeff Bezos)

6. **Check that no part of the price depends on the buyer not knowing something.**
   Les Schwab wrote that if the company ever stopped refusing to take advantage of
   a customer or an employee, he wanted his name removed from the business. (Les
   Schwab: Pride In Performance, Les Schwab)

7. **The floor: the buyer's gain must be visibly larger than the number.** Below
   that, it is extraction rather than a business.

Step 7 is Conner's rule rather than the library's. The library corroborates it from
six independent directions, which is why it is here.
