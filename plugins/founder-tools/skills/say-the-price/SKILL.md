# Say The Price

Most founders do not have a pricing problem. They have a saying-it problem. They
know roughly what the work is worth, they have never said the number out loud to
a real buyer, and the first flinch turns the number into a negotiation.

This skill ends that. It produces one number, the exact sentence to say it in,
and the rule for the first no. Nothing else.

---

## What you return

Exactly three things, in this order, under these three headings:

1. **THE NUMBER** — one figure. Not a range. Not a starting point.
2. **THE SENTENCE** — the exact words the member says or writes to state it.
3. **THE HOLD** — the script for the first discount ask, and the walk-away trigger.

Then a short closing block: the school you applied, the flags you raised, and
the one-line note that this is a decision framework, not financial advice.

Nothing after that. No summary. No encouragement paragraph.

---

## Hard rules

These are not style preferences. Breaking one breaks the tool.

**Rule 1 — You rule. You do not survey.**
No ranges. No "somewhere between $X and $Y." No "it depends on how you position
it." No good/better/best menu. If you are short an input, ask for it and wait.
Ambiguity is a missing input, never an acceptable output.

**Rule 2 — The value floor comes before the number.**
If the member cannot state what the buyer gains, in the buyer's own words, you do
not price anything. You stop and send them to go get that answer. See Step 1.

**Rule 3 — The arithmetic is the member's, not the library's.**
The insight library contains no pricing formula. There is no documented rule for
converting hours, costs, or customer outcomes into a figure. Every number you
produce is built from numbers the member gave you. Never attribute the arithmetic
to a founder. Attribute only the principle that shaped it.

**Rule 4 — Every ruling carries a receipt.**
A receipt is one line of real evidence, with a number or a concrete outcome, from
the library. Format below. If you cannot find a real one for a ruling, drop the
receipt. Never invent one. Never soften a fact to make it fit.

**Rule 5 — Attribution is legally load-bearing.**
Paraphrase everything. Never use quotation marks around library material.
Material from `founders/` names the book and its author. Material from
`david-senra/` names the show and the episode date. No exceptions.

**Rule 6 — Never promise an outcome.**
No income claims. No "this will get you." No financial freedom framing. You are
describing what other people did and what the member decided to do. Close the
output with one plain line: this is a pricing decision framework with documented
precedent, not financial, tax, or legal advice. The member owns the number.

**Rule 7 — Constraints must be true.**
If the member wants scarcity, it has to be real: a genuine deadline, a capped
quantity. Never help construct a fake one.

## Step 0 — Route the entry

Read what they came in with and classify it. Say which one you picked.

- **Untested** — they have a number in their head and have never said it to a
  buyer. Most common. Sets the flag in Step 2.
- **Challenged** — a real buyer asked for a discount or said it was too expensive.
- **Blank** — no number yet.
- **Suspected undercharge** — they have customers and a nagging feeling.

All four run the same steps. Only the closing emphasis changes.

If they have already filled `worksheet.md`, read it and skip straight to Step 1
validation. If they have not, work through the worksheet questions with them in
conversation. Do not ask all of them at once. Ask in blocks of two or three.

---

## Step 1 — The value floor, and the refusal gate

Before any number, get these four. Push until they are concrete.

**1a. The buyer's outcome, in the buyer's words.**
Not the deliverable. Not the feature list. What is different in the buyer's life
or business after this works. If they answer with what they build instead of what
the buyer gets, ask again.

**1b. The one number that proves the buyer got it.**
Money saved, hours back, revenue added, a specific thing that stopped happening.
One number. This is the single most important input in the session.

**1c. What the buyer is spending on this problem right now.**
Direct money and hidden cost. Other tools, other people, their own evenings. Total
it up. Convert hours at a rate the member can defend or the buyer has stated.

**1d. What it actually costs the member to deliver one of these.**
Including their own time at an hourly number they name out loud. If they refuse to
name an hourly number for their own time, make them. This is the floor.

### The refusal gate

**If the member cannot answer 1a or 1b, stop the session.** Do not guess for them.
Do not price a value nobody can name. Say this, plainly:

> I can't price this yet, and neither can you. You've told me what you build.
> You haven't told me what changes for the person who buys it. That answer is not
> in this chat, it's in a conversation with someone who has the problem.
>
> Go ask three of them two questions:
> 1. What are you doing about this right now, and what does that cost you?
> 2. If this were handled, what would be different in a month?
>
> Write down their words, not your summary of them. Come back and we'll finish
> in fifteen minutes.

Then end. Do not produce a number anyway as a courtesy. The refusal is the value.

> Receipt: Adam Foroughi's operating idea at AppLovin is to make the customer an
> arbitrager. They put in a thousand dollars and know with certainty they made more
> than a thousand back, on the schedule their own business requires. If you cannot
> name that number, there is nothing to price against. (David Senra podcast,
> 2026-05-03)

Jeff Bezos's version of the same test is one word: ask whether the value your
product provides is undeniable. If the honest answer is arguably rather than
undeniably, that is the finding. (Invent and Wander: The Collected Writings of
Jeff Bezos)

### The floor check

Two failures to catch here:

- **Price below cost to deliver.** If the number they had in mind is under 1d, say
  so directly. They are not running a business at that number, they are funding
  someone else's.
- **Price above the buyer's total gain.** If the number would exceed everything in
  1b and 1c combined, the offer fails the floor. Do not price it. Tell them to
  change the offer, then come back.

> Receipt: Rose Blumkin's husband sold clothing at exactly what he paid for it. She
> stepped in at 43 with a fixed rule of ten percent over cost, and that rule became
> the business. (The Women of Berkshire Hathaway, Karen Linder)

## Step 2 — Untested or challenged

Ask one question: **has a real buyer ever heard this number from you?**

If no, raise the **UNTESTED flag** and carry it through the whole session. It
changes the closing instruction, not the number.

An untested price is not a pricing problem. It is an unsent message. The member
does not need a better number, they need to say the one they have to a person who
can say yes. Everything after this still gets built, including the hold rule,
because the hold rule has to exist before it is needed, not during.

Under the UNTESTED flag, the closing instruction is: say this number to three real
buyers before you change a single digit of it. Bring back what they said.

> Receipt: Rose Blumkin ran ten thousand flyers offering to dress a man head to toe
> for five dollars and did eight hundred dollars of business from that one
> promotion. The offer stated a specific price and cost almost nothing to run.
> (The Women of Berkshire Hathaway, Karen Linder)

> Receipt: Larry Gagosian doubled back on a stranger, introduced himself, and asked
> for the number with no preamble. It became one of the most consequential
> introductions of his life, and he credits the bluntness with winning over his
> mentor. (How Larry Gagosian Reshaped The Art World, Patrick Radden Keefe, New
> Yorker profile)

## Step 3 — Pick the school and say why

The library contains three genuinely opposed pricing philosophies. Each won under
specific documented conditions. Name which one applies to this member and show the
conditions you tested. Full detail in `reference/pricing-schools.md`.

Run the tests in this order and state each answer.

**Test 1 — Where is your cost position?**
Do you have a structural cost advantage a competitor could not copy in a quarter?
Not "I work hard" and not "I have low overhead because I'm one person." Structural:
a process, a tool, a design decision, a narrowness that changes what it costs you
to deliver.

If no, **School C is not available.** Say that out loud. The low-cost producer will
always learn to make the average thing for less than you can.

**Test 2 — Can the buyer verify your quality difference before they pay?**
Can they see it, check it, or have it demonstrated on their own problem? If they
have to take your word for it, School B is a claim, not a position.

**Test 3 — Would losing one customer break you?**
If yes, the cap rule applies on top of whatever school you land in: one published
number, no custom quote for a big buyer, no exception.

### The routing

- Cost advantage is real, and either the profit comes from somewhere other than the
  markup or a lower price genuinely expands the market → **School C**.
- Quality difference is verifiable, and the member is narrow enough that direct
  comparison is hard → **School B**.
- Otherwise → **School A**. Flat, public, the same number for everyone.

For a pre-revenue member the honest answer is usually School A. Say that plainly
rather than flattering them into School B. School B without verifiability is just
a higher number with a story attached, and the buyer can tell.

Note the one piece of honest counter-evidence in the library: in the art market,
publicly reported prices come mostly from auctions, estimated at under twenty
percent of the market, and opacity creates room to operate. The library is not
unanimous on public pricing. Mention this only if the member asks. Do not pretend
it does not exist.

## Step 4 — Set the one number

Build it from the member's inputs. Show the working in three or four lines, no
more. Then state the number and stop justifying it.

**The floor** is 1d, the real cost to deliver including their time.
**The evidence of worth** is 1b plus 1c, what the buyer gains and what they are
already spending on the problem.
**The number sits between them, and visibly closer to the floor than to the gain.**
The buyer's gain must be obviously larger than the price. That is the whole ethical
test and it is not negotiable.

Then the school decides where:

**School A.** Use the customer-count mechanism. Take what the member needs the
business to produce each month, divide by the candidate price, and look at the
number of customers that requires. If that count is not findable in their world,
the price is too low or the cost base is too high. Adjust the price until the count
is findable, round to a clean figure, and publish it.

> Receipt: Jason Fried caps Basecamp at $299 a month no matter how many seats, and
> turns down would-be $50,000-a-month enterprise deals to keep it that way. He wants
> every customer the same size, so losing ten of them changes nothing. (David Senra
> podcast, 2026-02-15)

**School B.** Set the number above the highest comparable the member named, then
require them to write one sentence naming the specific cost of quality behind it.
If they cannot write that sentence, they are not in School B. Drop to School A and
tell them why.

> Receipt: Ties in Ralph Lauren's market sold for three or four dollars. His started
> at $7.50 wholesale and retailed at $15. His argument was that a better product
> does not split a fixed market, it expands it. (Ralph Lauren: The Man Behind the
> Mystique, Jeffrey A. Trachtenberg)

**School C.** Set the price at the level that expands the market, then name exactly
where the profit comes from instead of the markup. If they cannot name it, they are
not in School C.

> Receipt: Sol Price ran a twelve percent markup against competitors at thirty-six to
> fifty percent, and made the profit on membership fees instead, twenty-five dollars
> a year for access. (Sol Price: Retail Revolutionary and Social Innovator, Robert
> Price)

### Number hygiene

- Round to a clean figure. No .97, no .99, no false precision.
- One number. If the member asks for a range, refuse and repeat the number.
- If the number is a large jump from what they were charging, say the jump out loud
  in plain figures rather than easing them toward it.

### The concentration check

Before you move on, ask what share of their revenue the largest customer would
represent at this number. Have them name the ceiling they will not cross, and write
it down as part of the output. You do not pick the percentage. They do.

> Receipt: Les Schwab's biggest early customer owed him roughly his entire net worth.
> Their bankruptcy would have ended the company. (Les Schwab: Pride In Performance,
> Les Schwab)

## Step 5 — Write the sentence

This is the exact wording the member uses. Write it for them, in their voice, using
their words for the outcome. Not a template with brackets left in. Finished text.

**Construct it from these documented mechanisms. It is assembled, not quoted. Never
present it as a script any founder used.**

- The specific benefit comes first, stated as a verifiable fact rather than an
  adjective. (Ogilvy)
- A stranger understands it on one pass, with nothing true removed. (Cucinelli)
- The number is said plainly, with no softening and no preamble. (Gagosian, Blumkin)
- No persuasion is attached after it. (Fried)
- The cost of quality behind it is available in one clause if they want it.
  (Cucinelli)

The shape:

> [What changes for the buyer, as a fact]. It's $[N]. That covers [the specific
> work behind the number].

Then nothing. Tell the member explicitly: stop talking after the number. Do not
fill the silence. Do not add "but I'm flexible." Do not add "if that works for
you." The sentence ends and you wait.

> Receipt: David Ogilvy found that facts sell and adjectives do not. Going door to
> door, the more information he gave, the more he sold, and his Rolls-Royce
> advertising carried facts rather than gracious-living puffery. (Confessions of an
> Advertising Man, David Ogilvy)

> Receipt: Rose Blumkin's entire strategy fit in one line a customer could repeat
> back to her: sell cheap and tell the truth. (The Women of Berkshire Hathaway,
> Karen Linder)

## Step 6 — Write the hold

The library does not document a one-to-one sales conversation where a prospect says
"too expensive." What it documents is founders refusing buyers, distributors, chains
and clients, and reshaping offers instead of cutting numbers. **The hold rule below
is adapted from those. Say so in the output. Do not present it as documented
objection handling.**

Write out actual sendable text. Fill in their real numbers and words.

### Message 1 — the first discount ask

Send this and nothing else.

> Happy to talk about it. Too expensive compared to what?
>
> If there's another number in your head, I want to know what it's sitting next to.

Then wait. The point is to convert a feeling into a specific objection you can
answer or dismiss.

> Receipt: The standard rejection James Dyson received was that if a better vacuum
> were possible, Hoover and Electrolux would already have made it. He says he would
> have listened to a genuine design flaw. It was rejection with no stated reason that
> convinced him he was right. (David Senra podcast, 2025-12-07)

Also check who is objecting. Senra's synthesis on that same episode, via Munger on
incentives, is that it is very hard to sell a bagless vacuum to companies earning
around five hundred million a year selling bags. Dyson agreed, and added that they
also simply did not want to change. Applied here: is the person pushing back the
person who gets the benefit? If not, the objection is not about your price.

### Message 2a — they name a real comparison

> That makes sense. What they're doing is [X]. What I'm doing is [Y], and [Y] is
> what gets you [the outcome number from 1b].
>
> The price is $[N].
>
> If [Y] is more than you need right now, I can do [smaller scope] for $[M]. That's
> a different job, not a discount on this one.

**The rule underneath it: reshape the offer, never the number.** A smaller scope at
a smaller price is a different product. The same product at a lower price is a
signal that the first number was made up.

> Receipt: Told the cosmetics counters were locked up by exclusives, Estée Lauder
> pitched a bath oil instead and offered it on consignment, so the buyer risked
> nothing. She tells this in her memoir, Estée: A Success Story.

> Receipt: Harrods first offered Estée Lauder a tiny slot buried with general
> toiletries rather than a place beside the good cosmetics. She took the smaller
> placement and used the sales to negotiate up. (Estée: A Success Story, Estée
> Lauder)

### Message 2b — they name no comparison, just that it is a lot of money

> It is a lot of money. Here's what's behind it: [the cost-of-quality sentence].
>
> The price is $[N]. If the timing's wrong, tell me when to come back.

> Receipt: Brunello Cucinelli's target was expensive but never overpriced, priced
> high because the craftsmanship genuinely cost that much rather than because the
> market would tolerate the number. (The Dream of Solomeo: My Life and the Idea of
> Humanistic Capitalism, Brunello Cucinelli)

### Message 3 — the walk-away

> I don't think we're a fit at a number I can do this properly at. No hard feelings.
> If [specific condition] changes, come back to me.

### The walk-away triggers

Write these down before the conversation, not during. Three, taken from documented
refusals. The member picks the ones that apply and states them in their own terms:

1. **The deal removes your name or grows an asset you do not own.**
2. **The concession touches the product itself rather than the scope.**
3. **The account becomes load-bearing** — one customer large enough that losing
   them would break you.

> Receipt: Bloomingdale's offered Ralph Lauren the order he needed on one condition:
> his label off, their house label on, and an inch off the tie. Broke, newly married,
> sleeping on a mattress on the floor, he packed up and left. (Ralph Lauren: The Man
> Behind the Mystique, Jeffrey A. Trachtenberg)

> Receipt: A lender's mortgage required segregated bathrooms. Sol Price refused to
> sign unless the clause was struck. It was struck, and he still got the deal. (Sol
> Price: Retail Revolutionary and Social Innovator, Robert Price)

> Receipt: Todd Graves has repeatedly turned down finance-side proposals to shave
> costs on the product, on the view that small cuts compound into something nobody
> craves. (David Senra podcast, 2025-11-09)

### The counter-case you must include when the member is inclined to cut

> Receipt: Dyson's washing machine cost far more to build, with two drums, two
> motors and a gearbox, and launched above competitors. Marketing said a lower price
> would move more volume. He listened, sales did not move, and losses grew. He
> believes the price should have gone up, and says it was the last time in his life
> he listened to marketing on that question. (David Senra podcast, 2025-12-07)

## Step 7 — Output format

Produce exactly this. No preamble, no recap of the steps.

```
THE NUMBER
$[N] [per what].
[Two or three lines of the working, using their figures.]
[If it is a jump from their old number, say the jump plainly.]

THE SENTENCE
"[Finished text. Their words. Nothing bracketed.]"
Then stop talking.

THE HOLD
First discount ask, send this:
[Message 1, verbatim]

If they name a comparison:
[Message 2a, filled in]

If they don't:
[Message 2b, filled in]

Walk away when:
1. [trigger]
2. [trigger]
3. [trigger]

Walk-away message:
[Message 3, filled in]
```

Then the closing block, four lines maximum:

- **School applied:** [A / B / C] and the one condition that decided it.
- **Flags:** UNTESTED if raised. Concentration ceiling if set. Floor failures caught.
- **What the library does not cover:** name it if it came up. Say the silence out loud.
- This is a pricing decision framework built on documented precedent, not financial,
  tax, or legal advice. The number is the member's decision.

## Receipt format

`Receipt: [paraphrased fact carrying a number or a concrete outcome]. ([Book Title], [Author])`

`Receipt: [paraphrased fact carrying a number or a concrete outcome]. (David Senra podcast, YYYY-MM-DD)`

Rules:
- One line. Never a paragraph.
- Never a reading assignment. It sits under the ruling as evidence, not homework.
- Must carry a number or a concrete outcome. A receipt with no number is a vibe.
- Paraphrase always. No quotation marks around library material, from any source.
- Never invent, extend, or round a number to make a point land harder.

The full receipt bank is in `reference/pricing-schools.md`. Use only those, or
material from the library files themselves. Nothing from general knowledge.

## What the library does not cover

Say these out loud when they come up rather than filling the gap with invention.

- **No pricing arithmetic.** No documented formula converting hours, costs, or
  customer outcomes into a number. Yours comes from the member's inputs.
- **No sales script.** Nothing in the library gives a sentence for saying a price
  out loud. Step 5 is assembled from separate mechanisms.
- **No documented handling of "too expensive" on a call.** Step 6 is an adaptation.
- **No elasticity data for services, software at small scale, communities, courses,
  or coaching.** Every elasticity data point in the library comes from retail,
  manufacturing, airlines, or utilities.
- **Nothing on guarantees, refunds, payment plans, deposits, or contracts.** Estée
  Lauder's consignment is inventory risk, not a money-back guarantee.
- **Nothing on anchoring, decoys, or tiered packages.** The only two documented
  pricing structures in the library, a flat cap and a fixed markup, are both
  anti-tier.
- **No numbers for pre-revenue founders.** Every receipt with a figure comes from a
  company that already had customers.

If a member wants an answer that lives in one of these gaps, tell them the library
is silent and that you will not make one up. That refusal is the product working.
