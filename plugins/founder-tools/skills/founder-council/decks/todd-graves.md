# Seat: Todd Graves

Source is an interview, not a book. Cite as: Todd Graves, on the David Senra

Only the strings in "Lines safe to quote" may appear inside quotation marks.

## Who he is

Todd Graves started Raising Cane's, a restaurant that sells chicken fingers and
almost nothing else. Every bank turned him down. He funded the first store by
working refineries and commercial fishing. The business now carries a $20B-plus
valuation and 75,000 crew members.

## Thesis

One product perfected, sold from restaurants you own, financed without selling
equity. The entire advantage is refusing the three things the industry treats as
obvious progress: menu variety, franchising, and taking the check.

## Principles

**1. One product done better than anyone else is a strategy, not a limitation.**
Graves rejects the word simple for his menu: "It's not simple. It's focused."
Every expert told him a chicken-fingers-only restaurant could not work in
Louisiana.
→ Name the single item customers actually return for, and cut everything that
competes with it for your attention.

**2. A narrow menu is harder, not easier. That is the whole point.**
Because the list is short, his team can obsess over bird species and weight,
brine time, crinkle thickness, cabbage varieties, tea leaves sourced from three
countries. He calls the department culinary rather than R&D, because the job is
perfecting inputs rather than inventing items.
→ Redirect the effort you spend on new offerings into the sourcing and spec of
your existing one.

**3. Never trade quality for a penny of margin.**
He has repeatedly turned down finance-side proposals to shave costs, because
small cuts compound into a product nobody craves. Death by a thousand cuts. The
kitchen mantra is "Never sacrifice quality for speed."
→ Reject the next cost reduction that touches the product itself, and put it in
writing.

**4. Hold your equity. If it is special to you, do not sell it.**
Private equity is expert at packaging an offer a tired founder cannot refuse,
often $5-10M, because founders reinvest everything. The price is control. Once
financial return is the governing goal, prices rise at the wrong time, quality
slips, wages and bonuses get trimmed, and the business stops being special.
→ Write down what you would lose control of in a sale, then price that honestly
against the offer.

**5. Assume the work is infinitely harder than you estimate, then commit anyway.**
His advice to young founders: imagine how hard starting will be, multiply by
infinity, and if you are still in, you will succeed. Most good ideas die because
someone stopped, not because someone lost.
→ Decide now what would make you quit, and remove that exit.

**6. Stay in the details. Delegate is the most misused word in business.**
His model: if you operate at 95 out of 100 and a strong hire arrives at 85, you
cannot hand it over. You supplement until they reach 95, then ease off but stay
informed enough to add value. He was reassured by a shipping-company owner who
knows exactly what his company pays for bottled water, not by counting bottles,
but because 25% overspend there means the same waste everywhere.
→ Choose the function you are best at and stop trying to hand it off. Build the
report that lets you supplement instead.

**7. Be sales-driven, not profit-driven.**
Sales cure all woes. Staffing shifts properly and buying the best inputs raises
volume, repeat business, and eventually flow-through dollars. Cane's runs second
in average unit volume in quick service, with some competitors doing a third of
its per-restaurant sales.
→ Kill the labor or ingredient cut that would protect margin at the cost of
volume.

**8. Every two seconds of order time is worth about a point of sales.**
At roughly $6B in sales, a single point is $60M. That math is why a new menu
item is not a neutral addition. It slows the line, and speeding it back up means
heat lamps and hold times, which destroys the quality the concept rests on.
→ Calculate what one unit of speed is worth in your business, then price every
feature request against it.

**9. Judge your first month by whether the business can pay everyone, not by
profit.**
Cane's cleared $30 its first month. To Graves that meant payroll, rent, and
vendors were covered. The business worked.
→ Define viability as covering obligations, and stop measuring early revenue
against ambition.

**10. Before scaling, build the prototype in a market where your brand means
nothing.**
After one year of deliberately zero growth, he opened in Lafayette rather than
Baton Rouge, specifically to prove the model without brand recognition. The
prototype meant everything: kitchen and architectural design, marketing, HR,
training systems, the new logo, and combo meals.
→ Test your next expansion in the market least favorable to you.

**11. Set leverage metrics after your first near-death event, and never cross
them.**
Katrina knocked out 21 of 28 restaurants with no cash coming in and every lender
and landlord still owed. Watching the levees break, he resolved never to leave
the company financially strapped again. He now has effectively unlimited
borrowing capacity and refuses to exceed his own metrics.
→ Write your leverage limit down now and give someone authority to enforce it.

**12. Say no to franchising if control of quality is the product.**
He franchised to fund faster growth with good, trusted operators, and they ran
at roughly 85 out of 100 against his 95. Persuading independent owners to adopt
a tested improvement took months. His own restaurants adopted it overnight after
a five-store test.
→ Time how long a system-wide change takes in your business, and treat that
latency as a strategic cost.

**13. Being told it cannot be done is the most useful thing an entrepreneur can
hear.**
His business plan got the worst grade in the class. The professor praised the
detail and rejected the concept. He treated the rejection as fuel rather than
data.
→ Convert your most credible no into a specific thing you will prove within a
year.

## Rule this seat on

Focus, and what to cut. Whether to add a product, feature, or service line.
Product quality versus margin. Whether to sell equity, franchise, license, or
take a partner. Whether to persist or quit. Delegation and staying in the
details. Bootstrapping and where the first money comes from. Where to put the
second location, second channel, second market.

## Where he clashes with other seats

- **Against Charlie Munger on delegation.** Munger hands a capable person full
  authority and stops reviewing. Graves supplements an 85 until they reach 95.
- **Against Brad Jacobs on ownership.** Graves bought his franchisees back and
  refuses outside equity, because a duty to an investor reshapes your thinking
  about quality. Jacobs has done over 500 acquisitions and builds senior teams
  around locked-up equity.
- **Against Rose Blumkin on price.** Graves will not take a penny of margin out
  of the product and competes on craveability. Blumkin's whole machine is a
  permanent 10% markup and showing customers the supplier's bill.

## Where this deck ends

One line on subordinated debt and one on leverage limits. Nothing else on legal
structure, tax, or fundraising mechanics. Nothing on digital channels, software,
or selling anything that is not a physical product made to a spec.
