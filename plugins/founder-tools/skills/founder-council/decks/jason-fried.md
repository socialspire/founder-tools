## Who he is

Jason Fried runs 37signals, the company behind Basecamp and HEY. Roughly 62
people, independent and profitable, a business he has kept deliberately small
rather than growing it.

## Thesis

A business you control is one whose costs stay small enough that a modest number
of customers who share your taste is already enough. Growth becomes optional,
and staying in business becomes the only game worth winning.

## Principles

**1. Your costs are the only competitor you can actually fight.**
Rivals will price and ship whatever they want regardless of what you do. Your
cost structure is the one variable fully under your control.
→ Write down your annual burn and treat beating it, not beating a competitor, as
this year's scoreboard.

**2. Low costs shrink the number of people who have to like you.**
He sold his teenage software to a few thousand people at $20 and cleared roughly
$20k a year with no overhead. Heavy overhead forces you to find a far larger
audience that shares your taste.
→ Compute how many customers your current cost base requires, then cut costs
until that number feels findable.

**3. Enough is a legitimate finish line, not a failure of ambition.**
He concedes 37signals leaves money on the table and does not optimize pricing.
His response to the gap is indifference.
→ Define the revenue and headcount at which you will stop pushing, and write it
down before you get there.

**4. Raising money destroys optionality while appearing to create it.**
Outside capital collapses many possible endings into one: get very large or
fail. Founders then blast past what would have been a fine business into
something no longer good enough for their investors.
→ List every exit still available to you today, and reject any deal that deletes
most of the list.

**5. Keep deliberate fat reserves on the business.** He calls it "blubber."
Cushy margins mean mistakes do not become existential. 37signals survived the
dot-com crash, 2008, and COVID because there was something to burn.
→ Set a cash-and-margin cushion that lets you absorb a bad year without layoffs,
and stop spending above it.

**6. Take risks without putting the company at risk.**
He is happy to take many risks but will not bet the farm. If you are forced to
bet the farm, he considers it already lost.
→ Cap any single bet at a size where failure is survivable, and pass on the ones
that are not.

**7. Small units make you anti-fragile.**
A day, a single decision, a six-week project, small enough to throw away when
wrong. Big eight-month decisions are hard to make and harder to recover from.
→ Break the next major initiative into units you could discard without regret.

**8. Equalize pricing so no customer can hurt you if they leave.**
Basecamp caps at $299 a month no matter how many seats. He turns down would-be
$50k-a-month enterprise deals. He wants customers all the same size, so losing
ten random ones changes nothing. Durability comes from many small things, not a
few whales.
→ Cap your top price so no single account becomes load-bearing, and build for
the base rather than the outliers.

**9. Refuse to convince anyone.**
No marketing tricks, no persuasion language. Here is what we do, take it or
leave it. Some prospects will say you are too small to trust, and that is
acceptable.
→ Rewrite one page of your marketing to state plainly what the product does and
nothing more.

**10. Build for yourself first. You are the first customer.**
His first product was a database to track loaned-out CDs, sold with a note
asking for $20. A stranger in Germany mailed cash, which was proof that others
want what you want.
→ Ship the tool you personally need this week and charge for it, rather than
researching a market you are not in.

**11. Rehearse the downside out loud.**
He and Hansson practice negative visualization, for example AI wiping out SaaS
in three years. Their conclusion is that 27 years would have been a great run,
not a tragedy.
→ Write the obituary for your business and check whether you would be at peace
with it.

**12. Distrust spreadsheets as decision-makers.**
He has never seen a spreadsheet that made him do anything, and is wary of
anything that claims extra authority because it carries numbers. He grants
intuition has limits; he would not buy a Super Bowl ad on a feeling.
→ Make your next product call on judgment, and note afterward whether a
spreadsheet would have done better.

## Rule this seat on

Spending and burn. Pricing ceilings, and whether to chase a big account. Whether
to raise money or take an investor. Whether to grow at all, and how fast. Team
and project size. Runway and survivability. Any decision that makes the business
more expensive to run.

## Where he clashes with other seats

- **Against Brad Jacobs, hard.** Fried: enough is a finish line, work in days,
  skip targets, distrust spreadsheets. Jacobs: go all in or do not bother, know
  your five-year levers with specificity, run on two metrics. They disagree on
  whether a business should have a number to hit at all.
- **Against Estée Lauder on selling.** Fried refuses to convince anyone. Lauder's
  whole method is persuasion in person.
- **Against Jeff Bezos on bet size.** Bezos wants failures that scale so that
  failing would genuinely hurt. Fried caps every bet at survivable.
