# The Founder Council

You have a decision you have been circling for two weeks. You have asked three
friends and got three different answers, all confident, none of them people who
have run the thing you are running.

This tool seats eight founders on it and gives you one ruling, plus the best
argument against that ruling written down next to it, plus the exact condition
that would make the other side right.

It takes about two minutes.

## The eight

| Who | What they rule on |
|---|---|
| Jason Fried, 37signals | Costs, burn, pricing ceilings, raising money, whether to grow at all |
| Todd Graves, Raising Cane's | Focus, what to cut, quality versus margin, selling equity, whether to quit |
| Estée Lauder | First customers with no budget, samples, getting past a gatekeeper |
| David Ogilvy | What to say, keeping or killing a campaign, charging properly, standards |
| Jeff Bezos | How to decide and how fast, entering a market, how big to bet |
| Charlie Munger | What to avoid, focus, whether you have an edge here, partners, quit rules |
| Brad Jacobs, XPO | Which market to be in, whether to get bigger, partnering, comp |
| Rose Blumkin | Price and margin, competing with no money, surviving a bad month |

They do not agree with each other. That is the point. Two of them are on record
in flat opposition about whether to price high or price low, and the council will
show you that fight rather than blend it into mush.

## What is actually behind it

Each founder has a deck: their documented principles, the evidence under each
one, and a note about where their material runs out. Nothing else gets used. The
council cannot reach outside those decks, cannot search the web, and cannot fill
a gap with something that sounds right.

When the decks do not cover your question, it tells you that instead of guessing.
That line is at the bottom of every verdict.

## Install it

Nothing to do here. This tool installed with the rest of them in one step.
The classroom lesson **Install the tools** covers it once, in about three minutes.

## Use it

Type one of these, then your decision:

```
founder council: should I launch the course at $49 or at $500
council this: do I take the $4,000 client project or finish the product
what would the founders say about hiring my first contractor
```

You do not need to write it neatly. It will sharpen the question for you.

**One thing it will do before it rules:** it will read your situation back to you
in four or five lines and ask if that is right. Answer honestly, including the
parts that make you look bad. Eight founders reasoning perfectly from a wrong
picture of your business produces a confident, useless answer. Thirty seconds
here is the whole difference.

To ask one founder instead of the whole council:

```
/founder-council consult munger should I take on a co-founder
```

That loads only that person's deck and ends by telling you where their documented
material stops.

## What it looks like

**You type:**

> council this: should I launch my course at $49 or $500

**It reads your situation back:**

> Here is what I think is true. You have a finished course on getting a first
> paying client. You have never sold it. Your list is about 40 people, mostly
> from a Facebook group. You have no ad budget and you need this to work inside
> two months. Doing nothing means it sits unsold on your drive.
>
> Is that right?

You say yes, or you fix it. Then:

> **SEATED**
> Rose Blumkin, price and margin. Todd Graves, quality versus margin, and the
> one who disagrees with her. Estée Lauder, first customers with no budget.
> Jason Fried, what your costs mean for how many buyers you need.
>
> **RULINGS**
>
> **Rose Blumkin**
> Call: Launch at $49 and fix it there.
> Stands on: pick a low markup and make it permanent, not promotional. She sold
> at 10% over cost from the start and never let it drift upward.
> Do: Put the price on the page and stop planning discounts.
> Right if: one more buyer costs you almost nothing to serve.
> Wrong if: every buyer costs you hours you cannot get back.
>
> **Todd Graves**
> Call: Charge $500 and make the thing worth it.
> Stands on: never trade quality for a penny of margin. The kitchen mantra is
> "Never sacrifice quality for speed." (Todd Graves, on the David Senra podcast,
> 2025-11-09)
> Do: Cut the course down to the one outcome people came for, and build that
> part to the limit.
> Right if: quality is the thing people are buying.
> Wrong if: nobody has bought yet, so you have no evidence of what they buy for.
>
> **Estée Lauder**
> Call: The number is not your barrier. Get it into hands.
> Stands on: find the reason people will not buy and repackage the same value
> under something they already buy freely.
> Do: Give the first module away and personally message all 40 people before you
> post anywhere.
> Right if: they want it and cannot justify the spend.
> Wrong if: they can justify it and simply do not want it.
>
> **Jason Fried**
> Call: $49, and cap your top price.
> Stands on: low costs shrink the number of people who have to like you. He sold
> his teenage software to a few thousand people at $20 and cleared roughly $20k a
> year with no overhead. (Jason Fried, on the David Senra podcast, 2026-02-15)
> Do: Work out how many $49 sales cover your year, then cut costs until that
> number looks findable.
> Right if: delivery is near free per extra buyer.
> Wrong if: you need this to replace an income by next month.
>
> **WHERE THEY CLASH**
> Blumkin and Graves are in direct opposition. She routes every efficiency gain
> into a lower price and treats being impossible to undercut as the entire
> strategy. He refuses to take a penny of margin out of the product and competes
> on how much people crave it. Both are documented, both worked. The split is
> whether people are buying your price or your quality.
>
> Lauder disagrees with the framing itself. In her reading the number is rarely
> the objection. The objection is that the buyer cannot justify the category at
> all.
>
> **VERDICT**
> The call: Launch at $49.
> Why: You have never sold this. At this stage you are buying evidence, not
> margin, and a low permanent price is the cheapest way to buy it. Rose Blumkin
> sold at 10% over cost from the beginning and never let it drift. Competitors
> sued her over carpet priced at $3.95 against their $7.95. The case was thrown
> out and the judge bought carpet from her the next day.
> (*The Women of Berkshire Hathaway*, Karen Linder)
> Do this week: Put $49 on the page, name the single outcome it delivers, and
> message all 40 people one at a time.
> Strongest dissent: Graves. A cheap price on a thing that is not finished
> teaches you nothing except that cheap things sell. Small cuts compound into
> something nobody craves.
> Flips if: ten of those forty tell you the price is fine and still do not buy.
> Then price was never the barrier, Lauder is right, and you change the framing
> rather than the number.
> Confidence: Medium. The decks are strong on price as a permanent rule and have
> nothing on selling a digital product.
> Decks were silent on: how to sell a course online. Ogilvy is print and
> television. None of the eight sold anything digital.

That last block is the part worth keeping. Write the flip condition somewhere you
will see it.

## What it will not do

- **It will not make things up.** If the decks do not cover your question, it
  says so and stops. No invented quotes, no borrowed statistics, no stories these
  people did not actually leave behind.
- **It will not give you legal, tax, or financial advice.** Nothing in the decks
  covers incorporation, contracts, tax, or how to structure a raise. Ask a
  professional.
- **It will not tell you about ads, funnels, SEO, or algorithms.** These founders
  worked in print, retail, restaurants, and software. It reasons from what
  carries over, promise one specific benefit, use facts instead of adjectives,
  put the product in people's hands, run what works until the numbers fall, and
  it says plainly when a question is past the edge.
- **It will not promise you an outcome.** No money claims, no freedom pitch. It
  rules on a decision. What happens next is on you.
- **It will not give you a menu.** You get one call. You are free to ignore it,
  and the reason to ignore it is printed right there in the dissent.

## If it feels off

Two things go wrong most often. Both are fixable in one message.

**It ruled on the wrong problem.** You confirmed a premise that was not quite
true. Say "the premise was wrong, here is what is actually going on" and run it
again.

**Everyone agreed.** Either the decision was less contested than it felt, which
it will tell you, or it seated four people who think alike. Say "seat someone who
disagrees" and it will.
