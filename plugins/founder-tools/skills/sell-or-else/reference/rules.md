# SELL OR ELSE — the rule set

48 rules in ten graded sections, plus one standing warning that is not graded.

Every rule carries three things: a checkable test, a receipt, and a fix pattern.

- The **test** is something you can actually check on the page. Not a feeling.
- The **receipt** is one line of real evidence about the rule. Paste it exactly as written here. It is a historical fact about the rule, never a prediction about the member's results.
- The **fix pattern** is what you do to the failing line.

The governing standard, which sits above all ten sections: the purpose of the work is to sell, not to be admired. Ogilvy drilled a two-word standard into his agency and awards were beside the point. Judge every piece of marketing by whether it moved a sale, not by whether the team liked it. (*Confessions of an Advertising Man*, David Ogilvy)

# SECTION A — THE PROMISE (14 points)

## R1. Does it promise a benefit at all

**Test:** Read the whole asset and try to write down, in one sentence, what the reader gets. If you cannot, it fails.

**Fails when:** the copy describes what the thing is, what it includes, or who made it, and never says what changes for the reader.

**Receipt:** Advertising that promises the customer no benefit does not sell, and most campaigns promise none. Ogilvy called that the most important sentence in his book. (*Confessions of an Advertising Man*, David Ogilvy)

**Fix:** Write the benefit sentence first. Kill anything that states no customer benefit.

---

## R2. Is the benefit specific enough to be yours

**Test:** Paste a competitor's name over the promise. Is it still true? If yes, it fails.

**Fails when:** the promise is better, faster, easier, all-in-one, the leading solution, built for modern teams.

**Receipt:** Deciding what to say and what benefit to promise outranks deciding how to say it. Ogilvy treated it as the most important decision in the entire campaign. (*Confessions of an Advertising Man*, David Ogilvy)

**Fix:** Write the benefit sentence first and treat everything else as execution.

---

## R3. Sell the improvement, not the product

**Test:** Remove the product name from the sentence. Does a description of the reader's changed life survive? If only a description of the thing survives, it fails.

**Receipt:** Jobs did not describe his work as selling computers. He described it as selling tools that unleash human potential and change how you work and play. His own summary was that a computer is a bicycle for the mind, a tool that takes an unremarkable human capability and puts it off the top of the chart. (*The Presentation Secrets of Steve Jobs*, Carmine Gallo)

**Fix:** Write one sentence describing the reader's life after your product, with your product's name removed from the sentence. Then put that sentence at the top.

---

## R4. Self-interest, not company-interest

**Test:** Count sentences whose subject is you, your company or your product. Count sentences whose subject is the reader or the reader's outcome. If the first number is larger, it fails.

**Receipt:** The founders of modern advertising converged on the same conclusion: talking about your product or your company wastes the customer's time, because they only care what it does for them. (*The Mind of Napoleon*, ed. J. Christopher Herold)

**Fix:** Rewrite the headline so it states the reader's outcome, not your feature set.

---

## R5. Sell the behavior, not the object

**Test:** Does the copy sell a thing and a task list, or the state the reader ends up in? Feelings, status and connection pass. Stuff and tasks fail.

**Receipt:** Michelin's own framing was that they were not selling tires, they were selling movements. A tire company only prospers if people travel more, so they went into the business of making people travel. (*Michelin: A Century of Secrets*, Alain Germain; *The Michelin Men*, Herbert Lottman)

Companion, if the copy is a feature list: people do not want what you make. They want what it will do for them. Market with the emotional state and the change the customer is after. (*This Is Marketing*)

**Fix:** Rewrite the sentence as the human behavior you sponsor, not the object you sell.

---

# SECTION B — THE BIG IDEA (10 points)

## R6. The one-sentence test

**Test:** After one read, can you state the single idea of this asset in one sentence? If you have to list three things, it fails.

**Receipt:** Ogilvy estimated that fewer than one campaign in a hundred contains a big idea, and claimed no more than twenty across his entire career. (*Confessions of an Advertising Man*, David Ogilvy)

**Fix:** Refuse to launch until the single idea fits in one sentence.

---

## R7. One objective, one message

**Test:** How many separate things is this asset trying to accomplish? More than one objective, or more than one message, fails.

**Fails when:** the page sells the product, recruits affiliates, announces a webinar and asks for a newsletter signup.

**Receipt:** Ogilvy's account of bloated campaigns is that they read like meeting minutes and achieve nothing. He pairs it with Dyson's view that a consumer can barely handle one new idea, let alone several. (*Confessions of an Advertising Man*, David Ogilvy)

**Fix:** Cut to one objective and one message.

---

## R8. Say the line the same way every time

**Test:** Is the core claim phrased identically everywhere it appears, or reworded on each mention? Rewording fails.

**Receipt:** For the MacBook Air launch, Apple restated the same claim, the world's thinnest notebook, seven separate ways across the keynote, the press release, the site and the marketing. Repetition works because people forget, and then forget that they forgot. (*The Presentation Secrets of Steve Jobs*, Carmine Gallo)

**Fix:** Choose one headline and force it into the deck, the site, the outreach and the press copy word for word.

---

## R9. The repeat-back test

**Test:** Could a reader repeat this claim to a friend a day later, without the page in front of them?

**Receipt:** Ivar Kreuger's entire scheme fit into five words, so investors could pass it on for him. At the peak, a startling share of American securities traded were his companies'. The word scheme is doing work here: this is a point about spreadability, not an endorsement. (*The Presentation Secrets of Steve Jobs*, Carmine Gallo)

If the Kreuger framing distracts from the point, use R8's receipt instead.

**Fix:** Say the line once to someone and ask them to repeat it back a day later.

---

## R10. Analogy as compression

**Test:** Is there one existing thing the reader already understands that this product is the equivalent of? If the copy explains the mechanism instead, it fails.

**Receipt:** Lulu Cheng Meservey, on the David Senra podcast, 2026-08-12: a founder spent six of seven minutes describing a marketplace connecting home cooks to people bored of delivery apps. She handed him "turning a cook into a chef" and he ran with it. Another founder's data-ingestion pitch became "Ozempic for companies." You get the complexity and the simplicity at once, because the listener supplies the rest.

**Fix:** Find the one existing thing your product is the equivalent of and lead with that instead of the feature list.

---

# SECTION C — FACTS VS ADJECTIVES (10 points)

## R11. The adjective sweep

**Test:** Take each sentence. Does it contain a verifiable fact, or only an adjective? Adjective-only sentences fail. This is the core line-level test of the whole tool.

**Fails on:** innovative, seamless, robust, powerful, easy-to-use, cutting-edge, world-class, best-in-class, game-changing.

**Receipt:** Selling door to door, Ogilvy found that the more information he gave, the more he sold. His Rolls-Royce advertising carried facts and no gracious-living puffery. (*Confessions of an Advertising Man*, David Ogilvy)

**Fix:** Replace every adjective with a verifiable fact.

---

## R12. Thin copy is usually missing research, not discipline

**Test:** Is this asset short because the writer chose brevity, or because nobody gathered any facts? If there is not one checkable fact anywhere in it, it fails.

**Receipt:** Ogilvy's diagnosis of thin copy was that gathering facts is hard work, so writers skip it and write short instead. His instruction was to spend the first half of any copy project on research rather than drafting. (*Confessions of an Advertising Man*, David Ogilvy)

**Fix:** Before rewriting, list the facts the member actually has. If that list is empty, say so plainly. The fix is research, not phrasing, and you must not invent polish to cover it.

---

## R13. Vagueness is a sin

**Test:** Is any descriptor generic where a concrete number or detail exists?

**Receipt:** Pulitzer drilled this into his newsroom: not a tall man but six foot two, not a beautiful woman but auburn hair and hazel eyes. (*Pulitzer: A Life in Politics, Print, and Power*, James McGrath Morris)

**Fix:** Replace every generic adjective with a concrete number or detail.

---

## R14. Anchor the drama to a real fact

**Test:** Is every dramatic or emotional claim tied to at least one verifiable detail? Unanchored drama fails.

**Receipt:** Twain took this from a newspaper colleague and it underwrote his fiction for the rest of his life: get the facts first, then you can distort them as much as you like. Exaggeration only works when it is anchored to something real. (*Lighting Out for the Territory*, Roy Morris Jr.)

**Fix:** Ground the story in one verifiable detail before you dramatise it.

---

## R15. The hedge sweep

**Test:** Search for we believe, it may, arguably, one of the, helps you, could, might, designed to. Any hedge sitting on the core claim fails.

**Receipt:** The paper Twain worked for banned the phrases it is rumoured and we understand, and required things to be stated as fact. Their reasoning was that unassailable certainty is what builds a publication's reputation. (*Lighting Out for the Territory*, Roy Morris Jr.)

**Fix:** Strip every hedge out of the page. If the claim cannot survive without the hedge, the claim is not true yet. Send it to R38 rather than softening it.

---

# SECTION D — SPECIFICITY AND NUMBERS (6 points)

## R16. Every number needs a comparison

**Test:** Does each number carry a comparison a stranger can picture? A bare number fails.

**Receipt:** Asked about Apple's small market share, Jobs reframed it against BMW and Mercedes, neither of which anyone considers disadvantaged. Four million iPhones in two hundred days was restated as twenty thousand a day. (*The Presentation Secrets of Steve Jobs*, Carmine Gallo)

**Fix:** Convert the headline metric into a comparison a stranger can picture.

---

## R17. Quantify the improvement as a ratio

**Test:** Does the copy state the size of the improvement as a multiple or a before-and-after, or does it just name a feature?

**Receipt:** Juan Trip did not sell air freight. He sold three days by dangerous mountain road becoming ninety minutes by air, a forty-eight-fold gain. (*The Presentation Secrets of Steve Jobs*, Carmine Gallo)

**Fix:** Calculate the multiple and lead with that number.

---

## R18. Make the number physical

**Test:** Can the reader see the number? An abstract count fails. A physical image passes.

**Receipt:** Rather than report circulation figures, André Michelin told readers the stacked guides would equal five Eiffel Towers, and later twenty, matching Mount Everest. (*Michelin: A Century of Secrets*, Alain Germain; *The Michelin Men*, Herbert Lottman)

**Fix:** Translate every important metric into a physical image before you publish it.

---

# SECTION E — THE HEADLINE / OPENING LINE (20 points)

This section carries the most weight. R19 is why.

## R19. The headline is where the money goes

**Test:** Two checks. How many headline candidates did the writer produce? Fewer than sixteen is below the documented standard. And does the headline carry the selling promise, or is it a label?

**Receipt:** Five times as many people read the headline as read the body, so writing it spends eighty cents of the advertising dollar. A headline change can swing sales tenfold. Ogilvy wrote no fewer than sixteen headlines per ad. (*Confessions of an Advertising Man*, David Ogilvy)

**Fix:** Write sixteen headlines before choosing one. Generate all sixteen yourself, then hand back the three strongest with the reasoning.

---

## R20. Long enough to state the whole benefit

**Test:** Is the benefit fully stated in the headline, or truncated to sound punchy? A headline under ten words that leaves out part of the promise fails.

**Receipt:** Ogilvy's research showed headlines of ten words or more consistently outsold short ones. His own best ran eighteen words, built on a detail he found on roughly page fifty of an internal engineering document nobody else had bothered to read: at speed, the loudest sound in the car was the clock. (*Confessions of an Advertising Man*, David Ogilvy)

**Never write out that headline. Its wording is not documented. Describe it only as above.**

**Fix:** Lengthen the headline until the benefit is fully stated.

---

## R21. Headline is the lure, body is the hook

**Test:** Does the opening line earn the next line, and does the body earn trust? Or does the headline describe and the body repeat it?

**Receipt:** Pulitzer drilled his staff on simple nouns, bright verbs and short punchy sentences. (*Pulitzer: A Life in Politics, Print, and Power*, James McGrath Morris)

**Fix:** Make the opening line earn the click and the body earn the trust.

---

## R22. The headline comes out of primary material, not out of your head

**Test:** Where did this headline come from? If it was invented at the keyboard rather than found inside the product, the spec sheet, the raw research or a customer's own words, flag it.

**Receipt:** Ogilvy's best headline came from roughly page fifty of an internal engineering document nobody else had bothered to read. (*Confessions of an Advertising Man*, David Ogilvy)

**Fix:** Read the primary source your competitors skim. In session, ask the member for the boring internal document, the support log, the spec sheet, or the sentence a customer actually said, and mine that for the headline.

---

## R23. Delete the first page

**Test:** Does the asset open with setup, company history or scene-setting before the action? If the piece is stronger with the first paragraph removed, it fails.

**Receipt:** Kelly's maxim is that most stories and pitches improve significantly when the runway is cut. (*Excellent Advice for Living*, Kevin Kelly)

Companion, for pitches: investors named this as one of the two most common failures in founder pitches. Founders jump to the product and skip the reason it should exist. Start with the problem. Never open with the product. (*The Presentation Secrets of Steve Jobs*, Carmine Gallo)

**Fix:** Delete the setup paragraph. Replace it with the problem.

---

# SECTION F — CLARITY AND PLAIN LANGUAGE (8 points)

## R24. The jargon sweep

**Test:** Count industry acronyms and category jargon. Any word a smart friend outside the industry would not understand fails.

**Receipt:** Jobs's presentations and Buffett's shareholder letters sit in the same category: enormously complex things explained plainly, reading like conversation rather than business writing. (*The Presentation Secrets of Steve Jobs*, Carmine Gallo)

**Fix:** Delete every acronym and say it the way you would say it to a smart friend.

---

## R25. The outsider read-back test

**Test:** Hand the copy to someone outside the industry. Can they repeat the claim back?

**Receipt:** Write it so simply anyone can read it and so colorfully no one forgets it. That was the entire Pulitzer formula. (*Pulitzer: A Life in Politics, Print, and Power*, James McGrath Morris)

**Fix:** Test the copy on someone outside your industry. If they cannot repeat it back, rewrite it.

---

## R26. Simple but uncommon words

**Test:** Are the descriptive words both plain and unusual, or are they generic business adjectives?

**Receipt:** Jobs described competing machines as crummy and said the iMac screams. Neither word is complicated. Neither belongs in a normal business presentation. (*The Presentation Secrets of Steve Jobs*, Carmine Gallo)

**Fix:** Replace three generic adjectives with words you would actually use out loud.

---

## R27. The eleven-word opening

**Test:** Count the words in the opening paragraph. Materially longer than eleven fails. Then check every other paragraph for length.

**Receipt:** Ogilvy capped the first paragraph at around eleven words, on the grounds that a long opening scares readers off and long paragraphs fatigue. (*Confessions of an Advertising Man*, David Ogilvy)

**Fix:** Cut the opening paragraph in half.

---

## R28. The intelligent-reader test

**Test:** Would this line embarrass you if a smart customer read it back to you? If yes, it fails.

**Receipt:** Ogilvy's formulation was that the consumer is your wife, not an abstraction, and you should not insult her intelligence. (*Confessions of an Advertising Man*, David Ogilvy)

Companion: people scan instead of study. (*This Is Marketing*)

**Fix:** Cut any line that would embarrass you if a smart customer read it back to you.

---

# SECTION G — THE READER'S SELF-INTEREST (10 points)

## R29. Who is this for

**Test:** From the copy alone, can you name the specific person it was written for? If it reads as written for everyone, it fails.

**Receipt:** The magic question is who is this for. Mass means average. Specific means accountable. It shows respect to say I did not make this for you, and to say I made this for you to the right people. (*This Is Marketing*)

**The template, use it verbatim:**

> My product is for people who believe ______
> I will focus on people who want ______
> I promise that engaging with what I make will help you get ______

**Fix:** Run the three lines. Rebuild the promise from the third line.

---

## R30. Segment by shared values, not demographics

**Test:** Is the copy addressed to a demographic, or to a shared belief or interest? Demographic targeting fails.

**Receipt:** Steve Stoute, on the David Senra podcast, 2026-06-21: the ad industry of 1999 divided the world into black, white and Hispanic, but nobody chooses a product because of their ethnicity. An eighteen-year-old in Compton and an eighteen-year-old in Greenwich who both skate share a culture. Speak to skateboarding and you reach both. Same episode, on what the data showed: DMX sold in Iowa where radio would not play him, and Eminem was being bought in Harlem.

**Fix:** Replace every demographic segment with a shared-interest segment and rewrite the creative to that interest.

---

## R31. The customer is the hero, the product is ambient

**Test:** In each sentence, who is the subject? If the product is the subject of most sentences, it fails.

**Receipt:** Lulu Cheng Meservey, on the David Senra podcast, 2026-08-12: dystopian stories put the technology in the foreground and it works, like Terminator or Blade Runner. Utopian stories that do the same thing get written off as PR. The Jetsons worked because it was a family sitcom with a robot and a flying car in the background. Her Shopify example is Skims: Kim and Jens did not have to solve web portal, payments and design separately before they could exist. Shopify is ambient in the story and not the hero, and it works better that way.

**Fix:** Rewrite so a person is the subject of every sentence and the product is the instrument. Publish one customer's before-and-after and keep the product out of the subject position.

---

## R32. Marketing is empathy

**Test:** Does every element speak to what the reader feels in the minute before they buy? Anything that does not, cut.

**Receipt:** Jimmy Iovine, on the David Senra podcast, 2026-02-01: his definition of marketing is understanding who you are communicating with and where they click from. He extends it to say the product itself is the marketing, if the product is great.

**Fix:** Write down what the reader actually feels the moment before they buy. Cut any element that does not speak to that feeling.

---

## R33. Loss beats gain

**Test:** Is the promise framed as something gained, or as something the reader stops losing? Gain-only framing on a strong pain point is a miss.

**Receipt:** Both books converge on this. Focus on the avoidance of loss rather than the desire for gain (*This Is Marketing*). People are more motivated by the thought of losing something than gaining something, and they want a solution consistent with the severity of their problem (*Influence: The Psychology of Persuasion*).

**Fix:** Rewrite the promise as what the reader stops losing. Then check that the size of the offer matches the size of the problem you named.

---

# SECTION H — PROOF AND CREDIBILITY (10 points)

## R34. Third-party proof beats your own claims

**Test:** Is any claim in this copy made only by the seller? If a real customer's words could carry it instead, it fails.

**Receipt:** Ogilvy's finding was that readers believe a fellow customer's endorsement more readily than an anonymous copywriter's puffery. (*Confessions of an Advertising Man*, David Ogilvy)

Companions: reading favorable reviews aloud on stage was a standing Jobs technique (*The Presentation Secrets of Steve Jobs*, Carmine Gallo). People are more likely to make a decision if they see people similar to them make the same decision (*Influence: The Psychology of Persuasion*).

**Fix:** Put a real customer's words in place of your own description of your customers. Never write the testimonial for them.

---

## R35. Replace the superlative with a verifiable proof point

**Test:** Every superlative in the copy. Best, leading, trusted, world-class, number one. Is there an external, checkable fact behind it? If not, it fails.

**Receipt:** Wilsdorf pushed his movement supplier to build a wristwatch worthy of an observatory rating. In July 1914 Rolex won the first Class A chronometer certificate ever awarded to a wristwatch, and fifty years later he still called it a day he would never forget. The certificate made every subsequent ad more persuasive because it converted a claim into a verified fact. (*Rolex Jubilee Vade Mecum*, Vol. 1, Hans Wilsdorf, 1946)

**Fix:** Replace one superlative with an externally verifiable proof point.

---

## R36. Own the narrowest true first or only

**Test:** Does the copy claim a category leadership it cannot defend?

**Receipt:** Rolex advertising led on narrow, defensible firsts. The world's first waterproof wristwatch. The world's first self-winding watch. The only wristwatch to earn observatory certificates. (*Rolex Jubilee Vade Mecum*, Vol. 1, Hans Wilsdorf, 1946)

Companion: do not try to be the best, be the only. Competing on a scale everyone else uses puts you in a ranked field. Being uncategorizable removes the field. (*Excellent Advice for Living*, Kevin Kelly)

**Fix:** Define the narrowest true category in which you are genuinely first or only, and lead every message with it.

---

## R37. Demonstrate, do not describe

**Test:** Is the core claim something the reader is told, or something they are shown and can feel in under a minute? Told-only fails.

**Receipts. Pick the one that fits the member's channel.**

- Edwin Land sold polarized lenses to American Optical by renting a sun-facing hotel room, setting a goldfish bowl on the sill so glare washed out the view, apologising that his guests probably could not see the fish, then handing each of them a filter. He closed on the spot. (*The Presentation Secrets of Steve Jobs*, Carmine Gallo)
- At the 1895 Paris exposition Michelin built a carousel with two seats, one on an air-filled tire and one on a bare wooden wheel. Volunteers felt the difference instantly, and the president of France tried it. To prove their tire took minutes rather than half a day to change, they staged their own race, required Michelin tires to enter, and seeded the entire course with nails. (*Michelin: A Century of Secrets*, Alain Germain; *The Michelin Men*, Herbert Lottman)
- To prove the Silver Ghost's lack of vibration, a full glass of water was set on the running car at 1,600 rpm and not a drop spilled. The idea was still being copied in car commercials a century later. (*Rolls-Royce: The Magic of a Name*, Peter Pugh)
- Wilsdorf put a Rolex Oyster around the neck of a swimmer attempting the English Channel in freezing November water. She failed the crossing. The watch ran perfectly for ten hours. He then bought the entire front page of the Daily Mail on 27 November 1927 to announce it, and retail windows featured an aquarium with plants and goldfish and an Oyster submerged inside. (*Rolex Jubilee Vade Mecum*, Vol. 1, Hans Wilsdorf, 1946)

**Fix:** Convert the best claim into something a stranger can physically experience in under a minute.

---

## R38. Reputation is built on evidence, not claims

**This is the honesty rail. Use it whenever the copy fails because the claim is not true yet.**

**Test:** Is the strongest claim in this copy one the member has actually earned? If not, the fix is not a rewrite. The fix is to go earn it.

**Receipt:** A Rolls-Royce came back in 1923 in perfect running order after 100,000 miles over Scottish hills, a level of reliability no competitor could approach. (*Rolls-Royce: The Magic of a Name*, Peter Pugh)

**Fix:** Name the single hardest verifiable claim the product could survive, then go earn the right to make it. In the report card this is the honest "I cannot fix this line for you" verdict. Say it plainly. Do not polish an unearned claim into a better-sounding one.

# SECTION I — THE CLOSE AND THE ASK (6 points)

## R39. Reduce brain strain

**Test:** How many decisions does the reader have to make before acting? More than one is friction.

**Receipt:** Reduce the brain strain in the customer's decision-making process and make it easier for them to say yes. The goal is not to trick people, it is to use higher-leverage technique. (*Influence: The Psychology of Persuasion*) Smart marketers make it easy for people to work with them. (*This Is Marketing*)

Companion: removing friction was a Jobs preoccupation. Find the one step your customer has to do that you could do for them, and do it. (*The Presentation Secrets of Steve Jobs*, Carmine Gallo)

**Fix:** Reduce the ask to one decision, and do for the reader any step you can do on their behalf.

---

## R40. Give a reason

**Test:** Does the ask include a reason, or is it a bare instruction? Sign up. Book a call. Learn more. Those fail.

**Receipt:** Adding a reason dramatically increased the likelihood of people saying yes. (*Influence: The Psychology of Persuasion*)

**Fix:** Rewrite every call to action as instruction plus a because clause.

---

## R41. Free removes the buyer's risk

**Test:** Does the offer require the reader to take on risk or spend money before they experience anything?

**Receipts:**

- Ogilvy learned selling stoves door to door that offering free cooking lessons with the purchase transformed conversion. Glock bundled a trainer into every procurement deal. (*Confessions of an Advertising Man*, David Ogilvy; *Glock: The Rise of America's Gun*, Paul Barrett)
- Glock shipped free samples by the thousand with an invitation to send a check at a steep discount if the buyer wanted to keep them. Nine in ten sent the check. (*Glock: The Rise of America's Gun*, Paul Barrett)
- Estée Lauder had no ad department and no copywriter. She took the money earmarked for advertising and bought raw material to give away in volume, shaving off bits of lipstick and folding powder into wax envelopes for women who bought nothing. Trying it at home in her own mirror is what converted the customer. (*Estée: A Success Story*, Estée Lauder)
- Told the cosmetics category was locked up by exclusives, Lauder pitched bath oil instead and offered it on consignment. If it does not sell, you have lost nothing. (*Estée: A Success Story*, Estée Lauder)

**Fix:** Bundle free education into the offer. Take the inventory or trial risk yourself when the buyer's objection is risk rather than interest.

---

## R42. Do the deciding for the reader

**Test:** Does the copy hand the reader a conclusion, or a set of specs to evaluate? Specs-only fails.

**Receipt:** Rolex's proof removed the buyer's deliberation. With only one brand holding proven accuracy, the accuracy-seeking buyer had one obviously correct choice. (*Rolex Jubilee Vade Mecum*, Vol. 1, Hans Wilsdorf, 1946)

**Fix:** Remove one research step the buyer currently has to perform and hand them the conclusion instead.

---

## R43. If you cannot track it, it is not direct response

**Test:** Is there a single trackable action, and does a funnel exist behind it?

**Receipt:** Direct marketing is action marketing. If you cannot track it, it is useless. It requires a funnel, and if you do not have the funnel, do not buy the ads. Put the effort into improving the funnel, not buying more clicks. (*This Is Marketing*)

Companion: Adam Foroughi, on the David Senra podcast, 2026-05-03. His stated core operating idea is that a customer puts in a thousand dollars and knows with certainty they made more than a thousand back, on the schedule their own business model requires. Once that is measurable and reliable, spending scales until the arbitrage breaks down.

**Fix:** Name the single trackable action. If the member has no funnel behind the asset, say plainly that copy is not the constraint.

---

# SECTION J — LENGTH AND BOREDOM (6 points)

## R44. The boredom test

**The strongest single line in the rule set. Use it as the default verdict on filler.**

**Test:** Is this asset interesting, or merely inoffensive? If its only defense is that nobody could object to it, it fails.

**Receipt:** You cannot bore a customer into buying. You can only interest them. (*Confessions of an Advertising Man*, David Ogilvy)

**Fix:** Kill the line rather than soften it.

---

## R45. Bandwidth

**Test:** How many claims does this asset make? More than three fails.

**Receipt:** The common failure is cramming megabytes of data through a channel that carries kilobytes. Build the full list of what you want remembered, compress it to three, then fill each one with a story, an easy fact, a metaphor or social proof. Jobs's Stanford address is an opening, three stories and a close. (*The Presentation Secrets of Steve Jobs*, Carmine Gallo) People remember three points from a speech, maximum. Anything beyond three is decoration. (*Excellent Advice for Living*, Kevin Kelly)

**Fix:** Cut to three claims.

---

## R46. The dinner-table test

**Test:** Would anyone repeat any line of this out loud to another person?

**Receipt:** Pulitzer's explicit standing goal for the newsroom was to publish one thing a day worth talking about at dinner. Intriguing, unusual, provocative. He also noticed many readers read the advertisements the way others read articles, and set out to make the ad columns no less varied and interesting than the news. (*Pulitzer: A Life in Politics, Print, and Power*, James McGrath Morris)

**Fix:** Set the bar at whether anyone would repeat this out loud.

---

## R47. You cannot stand out by looking the same

**Test:** Does this asset follow every convention of its category? If a competitor's version is interchangeable with it, it fails.

**Receipt:** Every newspaper on the newsstand was gray, unbroken text. Pulitzer hired a cartoonist and paid him more than twice a reporter's salary. That pay gap shows how much he valued visual differentiation. (*Pulitzer: A Life in Politics, Print, and Power*, James McGrath Morris)

Companion: Les Schwab lifted the shape of a Lucky Strike slogan from outside his own category and rebuilt it around his name and his tires. He also watched a plant blend retread rubber, saw they added either walnut shells or sawdust, asked for one shovel of each, and marketed walnut for ice and sawdust for snow. Customers brought old tires in specifically to get it, for years. (*Les Schwab: Pride In Performance — Keep It Going!*, Les Schwab)

**Fix:** Find the one convention your entire category follows and break it. Or take a format from an unrelated industry and rebuild it around your product.

---

## R48. The family test

**This is the tool's safety rail.**

**Test:** Two checks. Would you want your own family to see this? And is the copy built on a claim about what you are for, or on an attack on a competitor?

**Receipt:** Ogilvy's standing rule was never to run an ad he would not want his own family to see. (*Confessions of an Advertising Man*, David Ogilvy) Promote what you love instead of bashing what you hate. The world has enough critics. Evangelists get further. (*Excellent Advice for Living*, Kevin Kelly)

**Fix:** Apply the family test to the whole funnel. Rewrite competitor-negative copy as a claim about what you are for.

**If the copy is manipulative or dishonest, do not rewrite it into something more effective.** Say you will not, and cite this rule. It is the source material's own basis for the refusal.

# SECTION K — TESTING, AND WHEN TO RETIRE AN ASSET

**Not graded. This is a standing warning that goes on every report, and it goes at the top when the member's copy is already producing sales.**

## K1. The moving parade

You are advertising to a moving parade, not a standing army. The audience continuously refreshes. Millions marry every year, so the ad that sold a fridge to last year's newlyweds sells to next year's too. Stop retiring assets because your team is bored of them. Retire them when the numbers fall. (*Confessions of an Advertising Man*, David Ogilvy)

## K2. If it works, run it until it stops working

The Hathaway eye-patch campaign ran twenty-one years. The Dove campaign ran thirty-one and was still leading. Ogilvy's account is that scores of good ads get discarded long before they lose potency. Set a performance threshold, not a calendar date, as the kill criterion. (*Confessions of an Advertising Man*, David Ogilvy)

## K3. Novelty pressure is the enemy

Resisting the pressure to refresh takes real nerve. Humans are addicted to novelty, so it is tragically easy to be stampeded into change every six months. Require evidence of decay before approving a rebrand or a campaign reset. (*Confessions of an Advertising Man*, David Ogilvy)

## K4. You get bored before your customer does

Keep saying the same message even when you are bored of it. Frequency is how people remember you, and frequency builds trust. Rarely change your strategy. Constantly change your tactics. (*This Is Marketing*)

## K5. Repetition is persuasive

Steve Stoute, on the David Senra podcast, 2026-06-21: radio and MTV controlled the system, so songs he had no interest in were drilled into his memory anyway. Awareness alone changes the odds. A coin flip on something you have already heard beats a shot at something you never have.

Corroboration: Pulitzer ran an issue day after day when he wanted it to stick, as many as 112 consecutive stories (*Pulitzer*, James McGrath Morris). Ogilvy noted consumers see over ten thousand ads a year, which is why the product name gets repeated relentlessly (*Confessions of an Advertising Man*, David Ogilvy).

## K6. Test big swings, not button colors

Execution quality varies enormously, which is why learning the craft pays. Measured in sales, one ad can outperform another by as much as nineteen to one. Test two genuinely different creative concepts, not two button colors. (*Confessions of an Advertising Man*, David Ogilvy)

## K7. Scale the winner instead of hunting for a new one

Michelin's 1922 push for more automobiles meant thousands of posters in major cities and a single brochure printed 600,000 times in one month. Pick the best-performing action and run it at ten times the volume rather than testing a new one. (*Michelin: A Century of Secrets*, Alain Germain; *The Michelin Men*, Herbert Lottman)

## K8. Port the winner sideways

What works in one market usually works in others. Port the winning campaign to an adjacent market before inventing a new one. (*Confessions of an Advertising Man*, David Ogilvy)

## K9. Always be testing

Always be testing. Always be wondering. If something is not working it is arrogant to insist on it. Focus on what works. Do not spend and pray. (*This Is Marketing*)

## K10. Set the review date up front

Wilsdorf's path ran through experiment, not insight. The terrible early brand names, the abandoned every-price-point strategy, the false starts. Ship deliberate experiments to generate information, and set a review date to cut what the data kills. (*Rolex Jubilee Vade Mecum*, Vol. 1, Hans Wilsdorf, 1946)

## K11. An experiment framework, not release marketing

Adam Foroughi, on the David Senra podcast, 2026-05-03: iterations are judged internally by advertiser spend and reach at the ROAS goal, tested in an experiment framework. Replace release marketing with an experiment framework tied to customer economics.

# Extra receipts you may use when a rule needs a harder edge

All of these are usable under the same attribution rules. Do not stretch them past what is written here.

**Category reframe, with numbers.** Women would not buy perfume for themselves, because it was a gift men gave for occasions. So Estée Lauder sold Youth Dew as a bath oil, which a woman could buy guiltlessly like a lipstick. That reframe went from about $50,000 in 1953 to over $150 million by 1984. Find the social or psychological reason customers will not buy, and repackage the same value under a category they already buy freely. (*Estée: A Success Story*, Estée Lauder)

**Selling before you have distribution.** Rejected by Harrods, Lauder got a magazine to write that her cosmetics were coming soon to London before she had any London distribution. Customers then asked for her by name and the buyer moved her to a prestigious counter. (*Estée: A Success Story*, Estée Lauder)

**Mailing your existing list before a launch.** Before her Saks debut she mailed her existing clientele and Saks charge-account holders an elegant card announcing the line. Everyone she had ever sampled showed up and they sold out in two days. She broke into Saks with four products and an $800 order, after being told no repeatedly. (*Estée: A Success Story*, Estée Lauder)

**The vendor matters more than the item.** Lauder's framing was that you are selling yourself, not the product. Excellent products die in the marketplace when the vendor lacks passion and cleverness. Stop blaming the product for slow sales and fix the selling. (*Estée: A Success Story*, Estée Lauder)

**Press as leverage.** One article about Glock was valued internally at five million dollars of free advertising. The distributor's advice was to give an influential publication the scoop before capacity existed, so demand was waiting. (*Glock: The Rise of America's Gun*, Paul Barrett)

**Free as a twenty-year strategy.** The Michelin Guide was free for its first twenty years. Road signs across France were free. Trip planning at their Paris office was free. Each one produced longer trips. A reporter noted that people assume Michelin tires are good because the maps and guides are so good, not the other way around. Twelve thousand readers mailed corrections back each year. (*Michelin: A Century of Secrets*, Alain Germain; *The Michelin Men*, Herbert Lottman)

**Make the ad itself a sample of the product.** Twain spent $150 on newspaper ads for his first lecture, using giant headlines to promise a splendid orchestra, ferocious wild beasts and magnificent fireworks, then admitted in small print that he had arranged none of them. The joke was the demonstration. He also reused the same closing line across shows: doors open at seven, the trouble begins at eight. (*Lighting Out for the Territory*, Roy Morris Jr.)

**Copy that filters for the person you want.** The Pony Express ad asked for young, skinny, wiry fellows willing to risk death daily, and added that orphans were preferred. Hundreds applied. (*Lighting Out for the Territory*, Roy Morris Jr.)

**Why a founder should bother with copy at all.** If your product genuinely improves someone's life, getting good at marketing is an obligation. It is how people become aware the thing exists at all. A great product nobody knows about is not a great product. (*The Mind of Napoleon*, ed. J. Christopher Herold; *Michelin: A Century of Secrets*, Alain Germain)

**Lower confidence. Use only if nothing above fits, and never as the only receipt in a report.** Ralph Lauren's ties started at $7.50 wholesale and retailed at $15 when the category sold at $3 or $4. His argument was that a better product does not split a fixed market, it expands it. Add one concrete quality upgrade, then raise the price above the category ceiling and defend it with the story. (*Ralph Lauren: The Man Behind the Mystique*, Jeffrey A. Trachtenberg)
