# Precedent Bank: The Five-Second Proof

Thirty-five cards, every one traced to a named file in
`socialspire/skool/insights/`. This replaces the seven-principle deck of v1.

**Why a bank and not a deck.** Seven principles are knowledge the model already
holds. Thirty-five specific, verified demonstrations, most of them ones no model
volunteers, are information. Read the index, route to the sections the run
needs, and read only those.

**Attribution rule.** Every card is paraphrase, including the ones marked
PODCAST. Not one phrase is quoted and none may be quoted downstream. Book
cards name the book and author. PODCAST cards name the founder, the show and
the date. Neither kind ever goes inside quotation marks, because the card text
is this bank's rendering, not the founder's words. If a member asks for a
quotable line, the bank holds none.

# A. The unfakeable object

**A1. Royce's glass of water.** Situation: a six-cylinder car whose central
claim, no vibration, was invisible in a showroom and easy for any rival to
assert. Did: set a full glass of water on the running Silver Ghost at 1,600
rpm and let people watch the surface. Happened: not a drop spilled. The device
was still being copied in car commercials a century later. Then vs now: the
failure mode is instant and the prop is universal. A software or service
founder has no engine block and must first find the physical analogue. Move:
find the single quantity your claim reduces to, put an everyday object on it
that a stranger reads at a glance, and that would visibly betray you if the
claim were false.
Source: *Rolls-Royce: The Magic of a Name*, Peter Pugh.
`founders/2024-02-07-the-biography-of-the-founder-of-rolls-royce.md`

**A2. Wilsdorf's aquarium.** Situation: the first waterproof wristwatch, and
waterproof is a word on a card at the counter that nobody can check. Did: put
an Oyster in shop windows submerged in an aquarium with plants and goldfish,
and ran an advertisement of a leading British actress with her arm in a fish
tank, watch visible. Happened: customers walked into shops asking for a Rolex
by name, which gave him control of wholesale pricing. Then vs now: this is the
library's goldfish bowl. A tank in a window runs without a salesperson, and its
equivalent, the product left visibly running in its failure condition where
the buyer decides, costs almost nothing. Move: put the product in its failure
condition in public and leave it there, on the buyer's path, with no presenter,
so any passing minute could be the one it fails.
Source: *Rolex Jubilee Vade Mecum*, Vol. 1, Hans Wilsdorf.
`founders/2024-07-08-the-founder-of-rolex-hans-wilsdorf.md`

**A3. Blumkin's supplier invoice.** Situation: carpet at $3.95 a yard against
competitors' $7.95, on a permanent 10 percent over cost that buyers had every
reason to disbelieve. Did: produced the supplier's bill and let the customer
read the cost themselves. When competitors sued, she told the judge she sold
at 10 percent over cost and asked what was wrong with that. Happened: the case
was thrown out and the judge bought carpet from her the next day. Then vs now:
a service business has fewer third-party documents than a carpet retailer and
has to build the equivalent, a raw log, a live timer, a supplier's quote. Move:
if your claim is a number, show the document that would convict you if the
number were false, and let the buyer check it against your price themselves.
Source: *The Women of Berkshire Hathaway*, Karen Linder.
`founders/2024-03-20-rose-blumkin-warren-buffetts-favorite-founder.md`

**A4. Turner's postmark piles.** Situation: a national cable audience that
Nielsen refused to measure, so advertisers had nothing to buy against. Did:
sorted incoming direct-response cheques by postmark into an Atlanta pile and a
non-Atlanta pile, building a measurement from evidence the buyers themselves
had generated. Happened: the outside pile dwarfed the local one. The big
advertisers still would not buy; small direct-response companies did, and that
revenue carried the business until the market caught up. Then vs now: a
founder has server logs and can show them live. Buyer-generated evidence is
only unfakeable if the prospect can see it was not curated. Move: when the
official scoreboard will not show your advantage, count the buyers' own
evidence in front of the prospect, the whole pile and not the good pile.
Source: *Call Me Ted*, Ted Turner.
`founders/2024-04-14-the-autobiography-of-ted-turner.md`

# B. The buyer supplies the input

**B1. Groq: your own question.** PODCAST. Situation: years of telling
customers and his own staff that fast inference mattered, with no reaction. A
strong demo months before the ChatGPT moment drew nothing. Did: diagnosed that
watching someone else's question get answered is not magical, while your own
question answered is. Stopped explaining speed and shipped it publicly so
anyone could type their own prompt. Happened: on a customer tour in Norway he
noticed his own queries lagging, checked, and found usage had exploded after a
viral post. Then vs now: public self-serve at internet scale is a software
luxury. A service business can still let the buyer supply the input in a live
session. The limit is stated in the case: it only works when the advantage is
large enough to be felt without instruments. Move: replace the demo where you
supply the input with one where the prospect supplies it. A demo you drive
with your own prompt is a claim.
Source: Jonathan Ross, on the David Senra podcast, 2026-07-05.
`david-senra/2026-07-05-why-asking-the-right-questions-is-the-most-important-skill-in-the-ai-age.md`

**B2. Musk hands Daimler the keys.** Situation: Tesla near death. Daimler
asked for a proposal for an electric drivetrain. Did: instead of a proposal,
built a working electric Smart car and handed Daimler's executives the keys.
Happened: zero to sixty in four seconds, driven by the buyer. Daimler invested
$50M, which Musk says saved the company. Then vs now: a bespoke product per
prospect is beyond a small business. The transferable rule is answering a
request for a document with a thing the buyer can operate. Musk is a household
name; the Smart car episode is not. Move: when the buyer asks for a proposal,
answer with the crudest working version they can drive, and put the keys in
their hand rather than yours.
Source: *Elon Musk*, Walter Isaacson.
`founders/2025-08-26-how-elon-works.md`

**B3. Lauder's five minutes on the buyer's own face.** Situation: a salon
owner told her to leave the products behind. Did: refused, and insisted on
five minutes to apply them herself, on the owner's own face, in the chair
where the owner had just seen the problem in the mirror. Happened: that one
application won her first retail space. The same method later broke her into
Saks with four products and an $800 order after repeated refusals. Then vs
now: a cosmetic shows its result on skin in minutes. Most products do not, so
the founder has to isolate the five-minute slice of the outcome that does.
Move: run the fix on the buyer's own problem, in the place they already feel
it, and refuse the exit where you leave material behind for them to evaluate
alone.
Source: *Estée: A Success Story*, Estée Lauder.
`founders/2024-09-04-estee-lauder.md`

**B4. Glock's keep-it-or-send-a-cheque.** Situation: a fifty-year-old maker of
curtain rods with no firearms experience, whose reliability claims
institutional buyers would not take on a stranger's word. Did: when agencies
requested free samples by the thousand in a single year, shipped them with an
invitation to simply send a cheque at a steep discount if they wanted to keep
the unit, otherwise return it. Where rivals made buyers travel to an academy,
he sent trainers to the customer. Happened: nine in ten agencies sent the
cheque. Gross margins ran above 65 percent, later about 70, against
incumbents at 5 to 20. Then vs now: a thousand free units was a rounding error
against contracts worth tens of millions. A small business can do this with
one or two reference buyers, and only if the sample is cheap enough that a one
in ten non-return does not hurt. Move: ship the real unit to the buyer the rest
of your market watches, on keep-or-return terms, so their own use in their own
venue is the test and a returned unit is the visible failure.
Source: *Glock: The Rise of America's Gun*, Paul Barrett.
`founders/2024-03-26-the-biography-of-the-founder-of-glock.md`

**B5. Glock lets the buyer write the spec.** Situation: about to design a
pistol for buyers who did not trust him. Did: before building anything,
convened the experts and the procurement chief, asked them to define what an
ideal future product had to do, and had every guest sign and date the sheet.
Happened: the buyer wrote the specification he then had to beat rivals on.
Sold more than twice his entire first-year break-even volume on the first day
of one trade show. Then vs now: transfers exactly at any size. Move: before
you demonstrate, get the buyer to state what the demonstration has to show,
in their words, and hold them to it.
Source: *Glock: The Rise of America's Gun*, Paul Barrett.
`founders/2024-03-26-the-biography-of-the-founder-of-glock.md`

**B6. Duveen's year in the buyer's house.** Situation: John D. Rockefeller Jr.
said a million and a half dollars for a set of pieces was too high. A discount
would have told him the price was never real. Did: refused to move the price
and negotiated time instead, leaving the pieces in Rockefeller's own house for
a year on option at full price, with a hard expiry. Happened: Rockefeller paid
the full price the day before the option expired. Ninety-five of the 115
paintings in Andrew Mellon's collection came through Duveen. Then vs now: a
year is the opposite of a minute, and a small business cannot leave inventory
in a prospect's office for a year. The structure transfers: the buyer's own
environment, the buyer's own use, no discount, a fixed expiry that is held.
Move: put the product in the buyer's own space at full price with a real end
date, so their use runs the test and the deadline converts possession into
purchase.
Source: *The Days of Duveen*, S.N. Behrman, New Yorker, 1951, with material
from *The 48 Laws of Power*, Robert Greene.
`founders/2024-04-26-the-robber-barons-art-dealer-joseph-duveen.md`

# C. It can visibly fail

**C1. Wilsdorf's swimmer failed. The watch did not.** Situation: a waterproof
claim that needed a public test with a real chance of failure. Did: put an
Oyster around the neck of a swimmer attempting the English Channel in freezing
November water, and only after the result was in bought the entire front page
of the Daily Mail on 27 November 1927. Happened: the swimmer failed the
crossing. The watch ran perfectly for ten hours. He ran the front page anyway.
Then vs now: no founder gets a Channel swimmer or a national front page. The
sequence transfers: proof first, amplification second, and a claim narrow
enough to survive the event around it failing. Move: attach the product to an
event that already has an audience and a real chance of failure, separate the
product's success from the event's success, and amplify only after the result
is in.
Source: *Rolex Jubilee Vade Mecum*, Vol. 1, Hans Wilsdorf.
`founders/2024-07-08-the-founder-of-rolex-hans-wilsdorf.md`

**C2. Bugatti sold the car that raced.** Situation: hand-built cars priced
above every competitor of equal horsepower, from a founder who trusted his own
eye over industry consensus. Did: made the racetrack the only judge. Racing was
both his test bench and his entire channel, entered every cycle. Every annual
catalogue opened with the list of races won, and every racing model was a
catalogue model any customer could buy. Happened: win the race and buyers
lined up. Two years after founding, the public and press understood he knew
more about cars than the giant firms. The venue also punished him: he
dismissed superchargers as vulgar and lost races for years before adopting
them. Then vs now: most small businesses have no scored public contest and no
press watching. What transfers: pick a test the buyer already believes in,
enter it repeatedly so a single loss cannot be explained away, and never
demonstrate a special unit the buyer cannot buy. Move: enter the one public
contest your buyer already trusts, and sell the exact unit that competed.
Source: *The Bugatti Story*, L'Ebe Bugatti.
`founders/2024-04-03-the-biography-of-bugatti.md`

**C3. Jobs let the builder run it live.** Situation: Apple choosing an
operating system between NeXT and Be. Did: brought his head of software
engineering and a live, running demo, and turned the floor over to the
engineer rather than performing himself. The demo could have crashed in the
room. The competing founder brought no team, no laptop and no presentation.
Happened: Be lost a company-defining deal and Apple acquired NeXT. Then vs
now: the buyer's own engineers could evaluate what they saw; a non-expert
buyer needs a demo they can judge without expertise. The asymmetry transfers:
a live demo that might fail beat a rival who eliminated all risk by bringing
nothing, because bringing nothing is also visible. Move: replace the deck with
a working thing, and let the person who built it run it live, standing beside
the buyer.
Source: *Steve Jobs in Exile*, Geoffrey Cain.
`founders/2026-06-03-steve-jobs-in-exile.md`

**C4. Dana White never edits a fight.** PODCAST. Situation: a product whose
outcome is decided in front of the audience and can be boring or ugly. A
rival's expensive format cut the dull stretches out; earlier promoters told
fans they had just seen something great when they plainly had not. Did: the
rule was never edit a fight, and when a night was bad he said so rather than
insult the audience's own eyes. Happened: Senra identifies telling fans when
the product was bad as the source of White's following and of the trust that
makes his praise credible. Then vs now: a founder's demonstration has an
audience of one and every temptation to rehearse. The honesty rule transfers:
the buyer's eyes outrank your narration. Move: run the demonstration unedited,
and say out loud when it went badly.
Source: Dana White, on the David Senra podcast, 2026-05-10.
`david-senra/2026-05-10-dana-white-the-man-behind-the-ufc.md`

**C5. Carnegie inspected harder than the buyer's test.** Situation: iron and
steel bridges sold to railroads in an era when competing bridges fell and
caused some of the worst railway disasters. A buyer could not tell a sound
bridge from an unsound one until a train was on it. Did: used only the best
materials and enough of them, and made the company its own harshest inspector,
so the failure rivals discovered under a customer's train was discovered
before delivery. Happened: nothing ever happened to a Keystone bridge while
competitors' structures fell. Then vs now: a small business does not get a
multi-decade reliability record before its first sale. Compress it: run the
destructive test yourself, then show the buyer the survivor and the wreckage
side by side. Move: find the breaking point in your own shop, so the buyer's
demonstration has nothing left to find.
Source: *The Autobiography of Andrew Carnegie*, Andrew Carnegie.
`founders/2024-01-23-the-autobiography-of-andrew-carnegie.md`

**C6. Cognition shipped the failure rate next to the demo.** PODCAST.
Situation: a weak early AI coding agent with a claim most of the audience
would not believe from a description. Did: launched in March 2024 with a demo
of what was possible and published a benchmark beside it, 13 percent on
SWE-bench when the best known result was 3 to 4. Happened: reactions hit both
extremes, the coolest thing ever and outright scam. Wu says 13 percent means
failing 87 percent of the time and still calls releasing that early
unambiguously worth it. First real customer was Nubank; the whole team flew to
Brazil and sat with their engineers until it worked. Then vs now: a small
business has no SWE-bench. The equivalent is a count the buyer can check
themselves. This is the only demo in the library accused of being staged, and
the accusation came from the public, not from the buyer who signed. Move: ship
the honest demo with its failure rate stated next to it, against a reference
the buyer can check. A demo that admits how often it fails survives the
accusation a polished one invites.
Source: Scott Wu, on the David Senra podcast, 2026-06-28.
`david-senra/2026-06-28-the-future-of-software-ai-cognitions-scott-wu.md`

# D. The venue forces the advantage to show

**D1. Michelin seeded the course with nails.** Situation: the only detachable
air-filled tyre in the world, whose real edge, a puncture fixed in minutes
rather than half a day, only existed at the moment of failure, and failure
does not happen on demand. Did: staged their own race, made Michelin tyres a
condition of entry, and seeded the entire course with nails so every rider
punctured and the repair-time gap had to appear in front of the crowd and the
press. Happened: every race and stunt bought newspaper coverage they never
paid for. Honest gap: the library gives no finishing result for the
nail-seeded race itself. Then vs now: a small business cannot rig a public
road and would be exposed if it tried. What transfers is the nail, not the
racecourse: put the breakage into a demo the buyer watches. And the ordering
rule from the same file: none of the spectacle started until they had the only
detachable tyre in the world. Nails under a marginal advantage just show the
margin. Move: seed the failure condition your product handles best into the
demonstration itself, so the failure is guaranteed to occur on a clock and the
recovery is what they watch.
Source: *The Michelin Men: Driving an Empire*, Herbert Lottman, with
*Michelin: A Century of Secrets*, Alain Germain.
`founders/2025-07-09-the-greatest-marketer-of-all-time-and-the-cofounder-of-the-michelin-family-dynas.md`

**D2. Michelin's three-hour lunch.** Situation: France's largest newspaper
announced a 1,200 km Paris to Brest to Paris race fifteen days out, and the
favourite was locked to a competitor. Did: coaxed a retired champion out of
retirement over a three-hour lunch to ride on the Michelin tyre. Édouard said
they could never be ready; André's answer was that their advantage was
temporary, so figure it out. Happened: the retired champion finished more than
seven hours ahead of the favourite, and the company's own history credited
that lunch as a turning point. Then vs now: universal. Move: when the obvious
demonstrator is unavailable, find the overlooked one and give them a reason to
come back, at the deadline the opportunity actually has.
Source: *The Michelin Men: Driving an Empire*, Herbert Lottman.
`founders/2025-07-09-the-greatest-marketer-of-all-time-and-the-cofounder-of-the-michelin-family-dynas.md`

**D3. Wilsdorf entered the observatory trial.** Situation: industry veterans
said a wristwatch movement was too small to keep accurate time. His own
assertion was worthless. Did: pushed his movement supplier to build a
wristwatch worthy of an observatory rating, then submitted it to the
chronometer trial, the harshest third-party test the category already
respected, and one he could publicly fail. Happened: July 1914, the first
Class A chronometer certificate ever awarded to a wristwatch. Every ad from
then on led with it, and fifty years later he still called it a day he would
never forget. Then vs now: most categories have no observatory. The substitute
is a trial the buyer runs under conditions you publish in advance. Move: enter
the most credible trial your category already runs, one you could fail in
public, and lead every message with the result.
Source: *Rolex Jubilee Vade Mecum*, Vol. 1, Hans Wilsdorf.
`founders/2024-07-08-the-founder-of-rolex-hans-wilsdorf.md`

**D4. Dana White paid for the production himself.** PODCAST. Situation: barred
from pay-per-view, and networks passed on the reality show. The one property
of the product, a live fight nobody leaves early, could not be shown because
nobody would carry it. Did: built the show as taped contests gatekeepers could
cut if needed, targeted a network that had just rebranded as a men's channel
and had recently wasted money on failed content, and when it passed, offered
to fund the entire production and let them simply air it. The last investment
his backers would make. Happened: the network aired it as pure distribution
with no downside. His verdict: paying that bill was the greatest thing that
ever happened to them. Then vs now: funding a whole production is beyond most
founders; targeting the distributor that just repositioned is not. Move:
repackage into a format the venue already accepts, target the one that just
changed strategy and needs a win, and carry the cost in exchange for the one
live moment.
Source: Dana White, on the David Senra podcast, 2026-05-10.
`david-senra/2026-05-10-dana-white-the-man-behind-the-ufc.md`

**D5. Glock brought the trial to the buyer's range.** Situation: rivals
required buyers to travel to an academy at headquarters. Did: sent trainers to
the customer. Happened: those sessions drew neighbouring agencies and became
regional open-house seminars, and professional adoption then set the taste of
the larger civilian market. Then vs now: transfers at any size. Move: run the
demonstration in the buyer's venue, on their equipment, and let the neighbours
watch.
Source: *Glock: The Rise of America's Gun*, Paul Barrett.
`founders/2024-03-26-the-biography-of-the-founder-of-glock.md`

# E. Problem first, then the fix

**E1. Land's goldfish bowl.** Situation: selling glare reduction to a large
optical company whose executives could not perceive glare as a problem from a
meeting room. Did: rented a hotel room facing the sun, placed a goldfish bowl
on the windowsill so the glare washed out the view, apologised that his guests
probably could not see the fish, and handed each of them a polarising filter.
Happened: he closed on the spot. Then vs now: Land controlled the sun, the room
and the fish. What transfers is the sequence, problem first, fix second, and a
filter the buyer holds. The library carries this second-hand through a book
about Jobs's presentations, with no number beyond the close. Move: engineer
the room so the buyer personally experiences the failure your product removes,
then put the removing object in their own hand.
Source: *The Presentation Secrets of Steve Jobs*, Carmine Gallo. The founder
is Land; the source is Gallo's book.
`founders/2024-07-03-how-to-sell-like-steve-jobs.md`

**E2. Michelin's two-seat carousel.** Situation: a comfort claim for an
air-filled tyre that was unarguable in words and unfelt in practice, in a city
that rode on solid wheels and had no reference point. Did: at the 1895 Paris
exposition mounted a carousel with two seats, one on an air-filled tyre, one on
a bare wooden wheel, and let volunteers ride both. The control was built into
the same object as the product. Happened: volunteers felt the difference
instantly. The president of France tried it. Then vs now: a founder has a
booth, a screen-share or a sales call, and the mechanism scales down intact:
buyer supplies the input, the old way sits beside the new way, no narrator.
Move: build the comparison into one object the stranger's own body supplies
the input to, with the status quo sitting right next to the product.
Source: *The Michelin Men: Driving an Empire*, Herbert Lottman.
`founders/2025-07-09-the-greatest-marketer-of-all-time-and-the-cofounder-of-the-michelin-family-dynas.md`

**E3. Lauder's mirror.** Situation: a rented counter inside a beauty salon,
where women were sent home with great hair and a tired face. Did: nothing to
induce the problem. The salon chair and the mirror had already done it before
she said a word. Happened: the five-minute application landed on a buyer who
was already looking at the problem. Then vs now: universal. Move: find the
place where the buyer already feels the problem and demonstrate there, rather
than describing the problem somewhere they do not.
Source: *Estée: A Success Story*, Estée Lauder.
`founders/2024-09-04-estee-lauder.md`

# F. What they walk out holding

**F1. Lauder's lipstick shavings.** Situation: no advertising department and a
customer who had just watched the demo and still bought nothing. Did: shaved
slivers off lipsticks and folded powder into wax envelopes so nobody left her
presence empty-handed, even non-buyers. Took the money earmarked for
advertising and bought raw material to give away in volume. Handed samples to
the hat and dress salespeople in every department store. Happened: the
non-buyer converted later, at home, in her own mirror. Before the Saks debut
she mailed everyone she had ever sampled; they all showed up and the line sold
out in two days. Competitors laughed at the giveaway budget. Then vs now:
cosmetics have near-zero sample cost and a self-evident result. A service
founder has to invent the sample: a slice of the buyer's own material with the
work already done on it. Move: send every prospect away holding a unit small
enough to cost you nothing, sized so they use it alone and the verdict is
theirs.
Source: *Estée: A Success Story*, Estée Lauder.
`founders/2024-09-04-estee-lauder.md`

**F2. Glock's thousand samples.** See B4. The unit that leaves with the buyer
is the real unit, not a demo unit, and the cheque is the measurement.
Source: *Glock: The Rise of America's Gun*, Paul Barrett.
`founders/2024-03-26-the-biography-of-the-founder-of-glock.md`

**F3. Musk's keys.** See B2. What Daimler's executives held was the thing
itself, operating.
Source: *Elon Musk*, Walter Isaacson.
`founders/2025-08-26-how-elon-works.md`

# G. Close while the proof is in the room

**G1. The napkin in the alleyway.** PODCAST. Situation: the reality show's
live finale was the moment the network decided whether to renew. Did: let the
live fight be the proof. The finale bout had the crowd demanding another round
in front of the network's executives, and White did not wait for a follow-up
meeting. Happened: executives pulled them into an arena alleyway and agreed a
renewal on a napkin, bullet points and a handshake, no contract, in the hour
after the proof. The library carries no dollar figure for the deal. Then vs
now: a crowd of thousands is not available to a founder. The timing rule holds
at any scale: the buyer who just watched it work is asked right then, not in a
follow-up email a week later, which is the interest-then-silence failure this
skill exists to fix. Move: the demonstration is not finished until the close is
attempted before the buyer leaves.
Source: Dana White, on the David Senra podcast, 2026-05-10.
`david-senra/2026-05-10-dana-white-the-man-behind-the-ufc.md`

# H. The crude version, and the service or software demonstration

**H1. Dyson's cereal packets.** Situation: convinced a cyclone could replace
the vacuum bag, not a qualified engineer, no money. Did: built the first
cyclone from cereal packets and masking tape, in his kitchen. Happened: the
first crude prototype worked before he understood why it worked. It then took
5,127 prototypes to reach production. Then vs now: Dyson demonstrated to
himself, not to a buyer, and the trade resisted for years afterward. The
cardboard version answers whether the claim holds, in a day. The library
records no case of the cardboard version itself being shown to a buyer. Move:
build the crude version from what is already in the house before you
understand the mechanism.
Source: *Against the Odds: An Autobiography*, James Dyson.
`founders/2024-03-17-james-dyson-against-the-odds.md`

**H2. DoorDash in 43 minutes.** PODCAST. Situation: four founders wanting to
know whether restaurants and customers actually wanted delivery. A service
claim with no dataset and no product. Did: put a service demo live in 43
minutes. A $9 domain, a static page with eight scanned restaurant menus, and a
Google Voice number that rang all four founders' phones. They took orders by
voice, drove the food themselves, and collected payment with a Square dongle
in an iPhone headphone jack. Happened: real orders came in. The founders
personally ran deliveries for the first six months, and Xu says every
non-obvious fact came from that. The demo could visibly fail, late food, wrong
order, in front of the customer. Then vs now: a solo founder can copy the
mechanics exactly. The demo only counts when the buyer supplies the input, an
order, rather than when the founder shows a page. Move: demonstrate a service
by delivering one unit of it to one real buyer this week, with the buyer
supplying the order and you doing the work by hand.

# I. What is not on record

Every one of these was searched for and not found. State the relevant one in
every run. It is a required output line.

**How to state it.** As an absence, never as a verdict. Holding no case of
a demonstration that failed in front of a buyer does not mean demonstrations do not fail.
It means nothing is on record either way, so the member should price the
attempt as untested.

**The words the member reads.** Open with "I have no documented case of",
name their own situation in plain words, then name the closest case and how
it differs. The member never reads the words library, bank, card, corpus or
record of this file, and never a flourish about being the first: an absence
is information, not a warning against the ruling. Never "the library says
no."

1. **No demonstration that failed in front of a buyer.** Two hundred files
   searched, claim lines and evidence lines. Not one. The nearest are Wilsdorf's
   swimmer failing while the watch worked, Lütke's demo failing on the buyer's
   phone, and Cognition being called a scam by the public while the buyer
   signed. The library is written by winners. The demonstration that broke on
   stage was never recorded, and the skill cannot tell you what to do when
   yours does.
2. **No demonstration exposed as staged.** The one staged prop in the library,
   Zip2's server rack, was never caught. There is no precedent for the cost of
   being caught.
3. **No head-to-head against a named rival's product in front of buyers.**
   Michelin's carousel compared against a bare wheel, not a rival tyre. The
   nail-seeded race required Michelin tyres to enter, so no rival was on the
   course. If the member wants a you-versus-them demo, the library holds no
   precedent for it.
4. **One explicit precedent for the buyer supplying the input.** Groq. Lauder's
   own face, Musk's keys and Glock's keep-or-return are the physical cousins.
   No case anywhere of a skeptic handing the founder their own data, product
   or worst case to test. The skill's central rule rests on thin precedent and
   says so.
5. **No sub-minute, buyer-supplied-input demonstration with a measured
   outcome.** Royce's glass and Dyson's transparent bin are the only
   demonstrations in the library that genuinely run under a minute, and neither
   carries a number. Every card with a number ran longer: a court case, a year
   in a house, a race season, a fight night. The skill's promise of a proof in
   under a minute is the skill's, assembled from the material, and not a thing
   any founder in the library is recorded doing with a result attached.
6. **No agency deliverable ever demonstrated to a client.** No sample ad, no
   spec campaign, no free audit, anywhere in the corpus. Cameron's calling-card
   film and DoorDash's hand-delivered orders are the nearest precedents, from
   adjacent crafts. A marketing or consulting founder is at the edge of what
   this bank can support.
7. **Six software or service demonstrations, and no more.** NeXT, Cognition,
   Shopify, DoorDash, Groq, Cameron. The v1 deck claimed the library held none.
   It holds six, and this bank carries all of them. There is nothing on screen
   recordings, sandboxes or trial environments as such.
8. **No money-back guarantee or risk-free trial used as the demonstration.**
   Lauder's consignment offer to a distributor is the only trace, and it is
   distributor-level.
9. **No blind test, taste test or before-and-after as a claim line.** The
   carousel is the only side-by-side, and it lives in an evidence line.
10. **No count of demonstrations run, demos-to-close ratio, or conversion
    figure for any demonstration.** Glock's nine in ten is agency conversion on
    shipped units, the only ratio in the bank.
