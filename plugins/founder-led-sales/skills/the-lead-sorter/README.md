# The Lead Sorter

Part of **Founder Led Sales**. Version 4.2.0

You have open deals that will not close. People took the meeting, said it looked
interesting, and went quiet. You are following up with all of them because you
cannot tell which ones are still alive.

Some of them were never able to say yes. Not because you pitched badly, and not
because they did not understand. Because your product removes a line of revenue
they depend on. No amount of follow-up changes arithmetic.

This skill separates those groups. Then it asks the one question that can move
a dead deal.

## What you get back

A sheet, Who Can Say Yes. Every open deal in exactly one group:

- **Already excited.** They described the use themselves, in their own words,
  without being taught it. Or they paid before delivery. Or they ordered past
  your plan.
- **Still explaining.** The logical buyers who should want it. Not impossible.
  Expensive. Sorted by what they argue about, and given a decision deadline.
- **Cannot say yes.** Their own revenue forbids it, and the sheet names which
  line of theirs your product removes.

Every row also names who can actually sign, and whether that is the person you
have been talking to. A franchise location, a branch, an enthusiastic contact
with no budget: none of them is the buyer, and the sheet says so. A deal whose
only contact cannot sign gets its own section, the wrong room, whatever column
it would otherwise sit in, because a deal in the wrong room is not proven
anything yet.

Every row carries a next move. The deals being dropped get the closing line to
send. The ones still being explained to get the single task that advances them.
The excited ones get a start date. Nobody leaves the sheet with a verdict and
nothing to do about it.

Then, on every deal in that last group, the question of whether a version
exists they could buy after all. Then the meetings you have spent on
each column this quarter, and one deal to drop this week as the priority.

There is a second procedure, **the target list**, for when the pipeline is clear
and you need targets.

## The Gould test

Western Union refused Edison's phonograph. The same company bought Jay Gould's
quadruplex, a device that multiplied the messages a single existing telegraph
line could carry. One product cut what their customers spent. The other raised
what their own wires could do.

So for every buyer in the third column, the skill asks whether a version of
your product exists that raises what they produce from what they already own,
rather than cutting what their customers pay them. If it does, that is the one
pitch that could still work, and the sheet names it. If it does not, the sheet
says none exists. It will not invent one to rescue a deal you like.

That test rests on one precedent in 194 episodes, and the skill tells you so.

## Every reply ends with questions

Whatever it gives you back, the last thing on the page is a short numbered
list: everything it needed and did not have, asked in one go, each answerable
in a line. Answer them and re-run it, and the next version is built on facts
instead of gaps. It never asks one question at a time.

## What it refuses to do

It will not sort a pipeline where nothing has been contacted more than once.
One contact is evidence of one contact, not of a no.

It will not build a target list from accounts already well served by somebody
else, because filling an empty seat and displacing a working provider are
different jobs with different odds.

It will not cite Kodak, Blockbuster or Britannica. None of them is in its
sources. If a famous case comes to mind, that is the signal it has left them.

And it will not tell you your pipeline looks healthy. There is no praise line.

## Where it comes from

Forty-four verified cases, most of them ones nobody volunteers.

Sam Walton offered the Ben Franklin executives his discounting idea and himself
as guinea pig. It required cutting their 25 percent wholesale margin to roughly
12. They blew up at him. Large industrial buyers dismissed Jim Clark's geometry
engine as a useless toy while Hollywood understood immediately and Lucas and
Spielberg asked to be first. Michael Ovitz called one unreachable agent every
Thursday at 10am for a full year. Ted Turner proved a national audience Nielsen
refused to count by sorting his incoming cheques by postmark. Ingvar Kamprad's
own trade association organised a boycott to cut off his suppliers, and he
never spent a day persuading it.

Every one is traced to the book it came from. The skill will not tell you
anything about these people that is not in its own files, and it does not
search the web.

It also carries a list of what those files hold no case of. Among them: no
buyer whose revenue a product removed later said yes to the same founder on the
same offer, and no case at all in a services or software business. When your
situation touches one of those, the skill says so. A library that has been
searched can tell you what it does not contain. Nothing reasoning from memory
can.

## Install

You need the marketplace added once. In Claude Code:

```bash
/plugin marketplace add socialspire/founder-tools
```

Then install the course:

```bash
/plugin install founder-led-sales@socialspire
```

Start a new conversation and paste what you have:

> here's everything open, nothing has moved in six weeks, and what each of
> them sells

You do not need to name the skill. If it does not fire, say `sort my pipeline`.

## What to bring

Every open deal, and for each one: who they are, **what they sell**, how many
times you have contacted them, and the exact words of their last reply.

What they sell is the field people skip and it is the one that matters. Without
it the skill cannot see whose revenue your product removes, and cannot run the
Gould test, which between them are the whole point.

## If it gets something wrong

Tell us what you typed and what came back. A deal put in the wrong column, an
invented number, a famous case that should not be there, or a verdict that went
soft when it should have held.
