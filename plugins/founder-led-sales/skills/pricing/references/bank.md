# Precedent Bank: Pricing

Thirty-four cards, every one traced to a named file in
`socialspire/skool/insights/`.

**Why a bank and not a deck.** A list of principles about holding a price is
knowledge the model already holds, so it adds nothing a strong model would not
reach alone. A bank of thirty-four specific, verified cases, most of them ones
no model volunteers, is information. Read the index, route to the sections the
run needs, and read only those. Do not read the whole file on every run.

**Attribution rule.** Every card is paraphrase, including the ones marked
PODCAST. Not one phrase is quoted and none may be quoted downstream. Book
cards name the book and author. PODCAST cards name the founder, the host and
the date. Neither kind ever goes inside quotation marks, because the card text
is this bank's rendering, not the founder's words. If a member asks for a
quotable line, the bank holds none.

**Obscurity.** Each card is marked household name, known to business readers,
or genuinely obscure. Obscurity is a tiebreak for STANDS ON, never the reason
a card is cited. The card that moved the ruling is the one that is cited.

**One honesty note before the cards.** The one-counter rule in this skill has
no founder precedent on the selling side. It is what Tom Murphy did as a buyer, mirrored to the selling side.
Section F says so, and the skill says so when a member asks.

# A. The counter and the walk

**A1. Tom Murphy asked the seller first.** Situation: buying television and radio stations,
newspapers and eventually ABC from owners who each had their own idea of what
the property was worth, with bankers and auctions as the industry default.
Did: asked the seller what they thought it was worth. If the figure was fair,
accepted it outright. If it was high, gave exactly one best price. If that was
refused, walked. Never used investment bankers. Happened: he never won an
auction, by design, and built a company one-sixteenth the size of CBS into the
one that outran it. ABC, at roughly $3.5 billion, was the largest deal.
Station margins went from the low thirties to over fifty percent within two
years, and buybacks under the same discipline returned 22.4 percent a year
over nineteen years. Then vs now: Murphy was the buyer. The founder is the
seller, and the loop mirrors cleanly: ask their number first, accept a fair
one, one counter, then walk. The cost of the rule is never winning a contested
deal, which is the point of it. Move: ask what they think it is worth before
countering; accept outright if it clears the walk-away; otherwise one counter,
then the end. Known to business readers, and the only procedure of its kind
in the library.
Source: *The Outsiders*, William Thorndike.
`founders/2024-04-15-tom-murphy-warren-buffetts-favorite-manager.md`

**A2. Zell and the number he let them hear.** Situation: deciding whether to
sell the company built from managing one student apartment building for a free
flat. Did: ran one test: would he buy his own buildings at the number being
quoted? The answer was no, so he would sell. Set an explicit walk-away number
in advance, his standing line being that it had to be an offer too good to
refuse, and had his team repeat that number to the buyer. Happened: the buyer
raised the bid. The company sold for tens of billions. Then vs now: a trophy
asset in a bidding market, so publishing the floor pulled bids up. A founder
with one prospect cannot expect the lift; what transfers is that a number
decided before the conversation does not get renegotiated inside it. Move:
write the walk-away before the call and let the buyer know one exists, so the
conversation is about whether they clear it, not how far you will drop. Known
to business readers.
Source: *Am I Being Too Subtle?*, Sam Zell.
`founders/2023-10-19-sam-zells-autobiography.md`

**A3. Duveen, Mellon, and the next day.** Situation: Andrew Mellon, his most
important client, hesitated and passed on a painting. Did: sold it the next
day to Collis Huntington at the same price, instead of chasing Mellon or
reopening the number. Happened: the painting stayed in the Huntington
collection for decades, and Mellon never hesitated on a Duveen offer again.
Ninety-five of the 115 paintings in Mellon's collection came through Duveen.
Then vs now: one object that could only go to one buyer; a founder's version
is a delivery slot that genuinely goes to someone else, and it works only if
the scarcity is real. Move: when the counter is refused, the walk is to the
next buyer at the same price. Never back to the first buyer with a lower one.
Genuinely obscure.
Source: *The Days of Duveen*, S.N. Behrman.
`founders/2024-04-26-the-robber-barons-art-dealer-joseph-duveen.md`

**A4. Lauren walks out of Bloomingdale's.** Situation: the order that would
have changed his life, on one condition: his label off, the store's house
label on, and an inch off the width of the tie. He was broke, newly married,
sleeping on a mattress on the floor. His ties were priced at $7.50 wholesale
and $15 retail against a market at $3 to $4. Did: packed up and walked out.
Happened: he later forced Bloomingdale's to give him the first designer
boutique inside a menswear department, arguing that with no ad budget the
store was his only way to tell the story, and the store's president later
advanced him money on fall merchandise when he could not make payroll. Then vs
now: the modern version is a client asking for white-label work or a stripped
version that damages the result. Move: separate concessions on terms from
concessions on the product itself. When the ask changes what the thing is or
whose name is on it, walk instead of countering. Known to business readers.
Source: *Ralph Lauren: The Man Behind the Mystique*, Jeffrey A. Trachtenberg.
`founders/2024-02-10-the-biography-of-ralph-lauren.md`

**A5. Sol Price and the clause.** Situation: a lender's mortgage carried a
clause requiring segregated bathrooms. Did: refused to sign unless the clause
was struck, stating it as a non-negotiable rather than bargaining around it.
The same file records him telling a hostile inspector with off-mandate
questions to leave and take his best shot. Happened: the clause was struck and
he still got the deal. The inspector never returned. Then vs now: the same
posture applies to unlimited revisions, ninety-day payment terms, or IP
assignment. Move: write down the terms you will not sign before the
conversation and state them early. A counterparty who wants the deal usually
strikes the clause rather than losing the deal over it. Genuinely obscure.
Source: *Sol Price: Retail Revolutionary and Social Innovator*, Robert Price.
`founders/2024-03-22-sol-price-the-founder-who-taught-jim-sinegal-sam-walton-jeff-bezos-and-bernie-ma.md`

**A6. Sinegal and the hot dog.** Situation: told internally to raise the price
of the hot dog and soda combination. Did: refused outright and sent the person
back to figure out how to keep the price on the cost side. Happened: the
combination is still $1.50, a price inherited from Price Club and held for
decades. Then vs now: a loss leader at a warehouse chain where membership fees
paid for the hold; it transfers only as the reflex, not the number. Move: when
pressure comes to move the price, send the work to the cost side instead of
touching the number. Household name, and the weakest card here against a
strong model; it is included because the reflex is the whole procedure.
Source: *Sol Price: Retail Revolutionary and Social Innovator*, Robert Price.
`founders/2024-03-22-sol-price-the-founder-who-taught-jim-sinegal-sam-walton-jeff-bezos-and-bernie-ma.md`

**A7. Zeckendorf stopped once the price was fair.** Situation: the buyer's side of the
table, as a developer who dealt constantly. Did: held that once a price is
fair, you stop negotiating and pay it, because sharp operators lose more
business and poison more relationships refining a good bargain than they ever
gain. Happened: the file records the rule beside the Astor fee in C3 rather
than a single deal. Then vs now: unchanged. Move: when the buyer's number is
at or above the walk-away, accept it and stop. Grinding past a fair number is
the dance the rule refuses. Genuinely obscure.
Source: *Zeckendorf: The Autobiography*, William Zeckendorf.
`founders/2024-03-11-what-i-learned-from-having-lunch-with-sam-zell.md`

**A8. Buffett's single price.** Situation: buying Helzberg Diamonds from an
owner who wanted him specifically. Did: gave an immediate answer and a single
non-negotiable price, repeated against every counter, with no non-compete
requested and compressed due diligence. Happened: the seller took it, and the
file records the promise to keep the business as treated as binding. It gives
no dollar figure. Then vs now: the buyer here is the one holding the number,
and the seller was not price-motivated; the mechanism is the same from either
seat. Move: one number, stated as the only one, repeated without variation
when the other side counters. Genuinely obscure.
Source: *What I Learned Before I Sold to Warren Buffett*, Barnett C. Helzberg
Jr.
`founders/2024-01-09-what-i-learned-before-i-sold-to-warren-buffett.md`

# B. The cost to deliver

**B1. Carnegie knew the cost per ton.** Situation: bidding steel rails into a
market where rivals charged $70 a ton and none of them ran unit-level cost
accounting, so every rival quote was a guess about its own margin. Did:
imported freight-hauling cost accounting into manufacturing and became the
only firm in the field that knew its cost per ton. Charged $65 because he
could produce for under $50. Asked managers what things cost, never what they
earned. Happened: the company became the most profitable on earth and sold to
Morgan for $480 million, because Morgan concluded a price fight would end with
Carnegie running him into the ground. Then vs now: a commodity with a hard
per-ton cost; a service founder's cost is mostly their own hours, which is why
this skill forces an hourly number. The discipline transfers; the structural
advantage usually does not, so the founder's cost floor is protection rather
than a weapon. Move: write down the true cost to deliver this specific deal
before the call, so any number you say, including a counter, is a known
margin and not a guess. Household name.
Source: *Meet You in Hell*, Les Standiford.
`founders/2024-01-29-andrew-carnegie-and-henry-clay-fricks-bitter-partnership.md`

**B2. Blumkin's supplier bill.** Situation: competitors sued her for unfair
trade practices over carpet at $3.95 against their $7.95. Did: held a fixed
rule of ten percent over cost from the beginning, never drifting, never run as
a promotion. Her standing practice was to produce the supplier's bill to show
a customer her actual cost. Told the judge she sold at ten percent over cost
and asked what was wrong with that. She had taken over pricing at 43 from a
husband who sold clothing at exactly what he paid for it. Happened: the case
was thrown out, the judge bought carpet from her the next day, and the press
from the trial was worth more than she could have bought. Buffett later paid
$60 million on a handshake and a one-page agreement. Then vs now: a retailer's
cost is an invoice she could put on the table; a service founder has no
invoice, and ten percent would starve a low-volume business. What transfers is
the fixed rule and the willingness to show what the price is built from. Move:
set your markup over real cost as a standing rule before the conversation, so
the number is a policy you state rather than a position you defend. Known to
business readers.
Source: *The Women of Berkshire Hathaway*, Karen Linder.
`founders/2024-03-20-rose-blumkin-warren-buffetts-favorite-founder.md`

**B3. Del Vecchio's sheet of paper.** Situation: bidding for Ray-Ban, which
was losing about $50 million a year and selling in gas stations across roughly
13,000 outlets, against rival bidders pricing off each other. Did: priced the
deal from three numbers on a single sheet of paper, units per year, cost per
unit, achievable sale price, and bid far above every rival because his number
came from what he knew he could make per unit. Then pulled the brand out of
the 13,000 outlets, raised prices, and multiplied the lacquer layers on the
frames so the higher price had a visible cost of quality behind it. Happened:
Ray-Ban went from a $50 million annual loss to the most valuable optical brand
in the world. His original stake of roughly 6,000 euros became about 25
billion sixty years later. Then vs now: he was the buyer; from the founder's
seat, the same three-line sheet is what makes a buyer's claim that a
competitor charges less answerable. Move: build the number from units, cost
per unit and sale price on one sheet before looking at what anyone else
charges, and let that sheet, not the buyer's comparison, decide whether the
price moves. Genuinely obscure.
Source: *Leonardo Del Vecchio*, Thomas Ebhardt.
`founders/2025-07-13-this-orphan-built-a-116-billion-empire-without-you-noticing.md`

**B4. Glock designed the factory with the product.** Situation: a
fifty-year-old with no experience in the industry, entering a market of
centuries-old incumbents. Did: designed the product and the factory from a
blank sheet at the same time, so he knew his cost structure before setting a
price. Set wholesale and retail prices below comparable brands while running
gross margins above 65 percent, roughly 70 later, against competitors at 5 to
20. Calculated the modest first-year volume at which he would break even and
drove that number down. Refused a government demand that manufacturing rights
for the winning design be opened to competitive bidding, keeping the
production profit rather than trading it for one large contract. Happened:
sold more than twice the entire first-year break-even target on the first day
of one trade show. Then vs now: he could be the cheapest and the most
profitable at once; most founders cannot. What transfers is knowing the
survival volume and the per-unit margin cold before anyone asks for a
discount, and treating the big contract that wants your economics as the deal
to refuse. Move: know your break-even volume and your gross margin before the
call, and walk from any term that trades away your economics for a single big
order. Known to business readers.
Source: *Glock: The Rise of America's Gun*, Paul Barrett.
`founders/2024-03-26-the-biography-of-the-founder-of-glock.md`

**B5. Sol Price's twelve percent.** Situation: retail rivals ran 36 to 50
percent markups; the State of California sued him over a 12 percent liquor
markup. Did: fixed the markup at 12 percent, enough to cover costs only, and
made the profit on membership fees so the margin sat on the customer's side of
the table. Treated the lawsuits and industry backlash as the expected cost of
the strategy. Happened: the 12 percent markup plus membership fee became the
profit engine of a company worth hundreds of billions. Then vs now: a 12
percent floor was survivable because a second revenue line carried the profit
and 4,500 SKUs kept cost structurally low; a founder without a second revenue
line cannot run it. Move: fix the markup over cost as a structural rule, and
when pressure comes to move the price, attack cost instead of margin. Known
to business readers.
Source: *Sol Price: Retail Revolutionary and Social Innovator*, Robert Price.
`founders/2024-03-22-sol-price-the-founder-who-taught-jim-sinegal-sam-walton-jeff-bezos-and-bernie-ma.md`

# C. Terms and time instead of price

**C1. Duveen and the year on option.** Situation: John D. Rockefeller Jr. told
him a million and a half for a set of pieces was too high. His whole business
was eight to ten buyers, so losing the account was not an option either. Did:
did not move the number. Left the pieces installed in Rockefeller's house for
a year on option, with a hard expiry, so the objects became part of the home
while the price stayed where it was. Happened: Rockefeller paid the full price
the day before the option expired. Then vs now: a one-of-a-kind object and a
buyer with effectively unlimited funds; possession worked because the object
was irreplaceable and already on his wall. A service founder cannot leave a
painting in the house, but a full-price pilot with a hard end date is the
same mechanism, and it weakens when the product is replaceable, so the
deadline has to be real. Move: answer a too-high objection with a longer
trial or an option at the original price and a dated expiry you will enforce.
Never with a lower number. Known to business readers, and the anchor for this
procedure.
Source: *The Days of Duveen*, S.N. Behrman.
`founders/2024-04-26-the-robber-barons-art-dealer-joseph-duveen.md`

**C2. Lauder on consignment.** Situation: the Harrods buyer said the cosmetics
category was locked up by exclusives. The objection was shelf risk and
existing commitments, not interest in the product. Did: pitched bath oil
instead of cosmetics, a category the exclusives did not cover, and offered it
on consignment: if it did not sell, the store had lost nothing. Accepted a
tiny slot buried among general toiletries, then shipped creams the buyer had
not ordered with a note that he would not want to tell a customer he lacked
the rest of the line. Happened: after three trips and repeated press rounds,
customers were asking for her by name and the buyer moved her to a
prestigious counter. The bath oil was Youth Dew, and the file records that
reframe growing from about $50K in 1953 to over $150M by 1984. Then
vs now: consignment cost her inventory carry, affordable because the product
was cheap to make; a service founder's version is deferred billing tied to a
usage measure, and only once the cost to deliver is known. This is inventory
risk, not a money-back guarantee. Move: when the buyer's no is about their
risk rather than your price, take the risk yourself and leave the price alone.
Known to business readers.
Source: *Estée: A Success Story*, Estée Lauder.
`founders/2024-09-04-estee-lauder.md`

**C3. Zeckendorf's fee for the Astor estate.** Situation: hired to reorganise
the Astor estate's holdings for a client who had no way to know in advance
what the work was worth. A flat fee would have been argued down; a high one
refused. Did: proposed being paid what he turned out to be worth, or nothing.
Took the whole fee risk onto himself instead of negotiating the rate in either
direction. Happened: he roughly tripled the estate's earnings, then billed
$350,000. Vincent Astor told his people to pay it and send flowers. Then vs
now: he had the standing to bill after the fact; a founder needs the measure
written down before the work starts, and should cap the downside at the cost
to deliver. Move: when the objection is that the buyer cannot see the value
yet, keep the rate and move the risk: a result-contingent fee with a defined
measure, or a small fixed base plus a share of the measured gain. Never a
lower flat fee. Genuinely obscure.
Source: *Zeckendorf: The Autobiography*, William Zeckendorf.
`founders/2024-03-11-what-i-learned-from-having-lunch-with-sam-zell.md`

**C4. Zeckendorf's one-year option.** Situation: he wanted land priced at
$6.5 million, about $17 a square foot, beside slaughterhouses, and did not
have the money. The seller wanted the full price. Did: put up a $1 million
one-year option against the full price, raised from a syndicate, with twelve
months to find the rest and a secrecy clause so surrounding prices stayed
flat. The seller kept the full price; what moved was timing and who carried
the risk of the year. Happened: under the secrecy clause he quietly assembled
roughly 75 surrounding parcels at about $9 a square foot, having bought the
core at $17 on the expectation it would be worth $50 to $100 once the smell was
gone. The file records the option and the assembly, not a resale figure. Then
vs now: he was the buyer; from the founder's seat this is accepting a dated
deposit instead of a discount. The deposit has to be real money, his was a
million against six and a half, and the window has to expire. Move: if the buyer says
the price is too high because they cannot pay it now, hold the price and sell
them time: a non-refundable deposit at a fraction of the price and a dated
window to pay the rest. Genuinely obscure.
Source: *Zeckendorf: The Autobiography*, William Zeckendorf.
`founders/2024-03-11-what-i-learned-from-having-lunch-with-sam-zell.md`

**C5. Cucinelli's fifty-three sweaters.** Situation: no bank would finance
production or inventory, so the yarn supplier, the dyer and the first customer
each had a reason to say no. The first order was 53 sweaters. Did: ran the
business on payment timing instead of capital. The yarn supplier agreed to be
paid after Cucinelli got paid, and a customer paid upfront on the strength of
belief in him. He did not cut the price to win the order; he moved when money
changed hands on both sides of it. Happened: the business financed its own
growth on those terms, held to women's coloured cashmere for roughly two
decades, and became a company listed on the Milan exchange. Then vs now: his
suppliers extended credit on character in a village economy; a founder's
equivalent is asking one customer for prepayment and one vendor for
pay-when-paid terms in the same week. Move: price what a prepayment is worth
to you before cutting anything. Full price paid upfront can be worth more
than a discounted price paid late. Known to business readers.
Source: *The Dream of Solomeo*, Brunello Cucinelli.
`founders/2024-02-13-the-biography-of-brunello-cucinelli.md`

**C6. Disney and the host's concession.** Situation: building a park nobody
would fund, having hocked his life insurance and sold his vacation home, and
needing a broadcaster to host the live opening. A star's fee was cash he did
not have. Did: paid the television host union-minimum scale and gave a
ten-year concession on photo sales inside the park. The host accepted the
concession in place of the fee. Same file, same playbook: he sold his weekly
show to the weakest network on condition it help pay for the park, and was
paid roughly $50,000 to $70,000 an episode for a show that advertised the park
for eight months before it opened. Happened: by his own account the host
became the highest-paid broadcaster for a single show in television history.
Then vs now: Disney had a park to grant concessions in; what transfers is the
seller's side, the host pricing the concession instead of refusing on the fee.
Move: when the buyer genuinely cannot pay the price, name a currency you have
that they value more than cash, price it before you take it, and put a term on
it. Genuinely obscure.
Source: *Disney's Land*, Richard Snow.
`founders/2024-05-14-how-walt-disney-built-his-greatest-creation-disneyland.md`

**C7. Foroughi's flat guarantees.** Situation: an advertising platform shipped
inside other people's apps, with no users of its own, in 2012. App developers
were desperate for revenue and had no reason to trust an unproven network's
revenue-share promise. Did: offered flat guaranteed payments to the earliest
distribution partners, on the order of ten thousand dollars a day to a
calendar app with a million users, and took on the job of making the ads pay.
Kept the monetisation risk rather than lowering what it offered. Happened:
eight months from launch to a million dollars a month of run rate, barely
profitable, which made the November 2012 round easy: $4 million at a $25
million pre-money note, mostly from people he already knew. Then vs now: he
could fund the guarantees because he had built two ad businesses before; a
founder's scaled-down version is a specific, measurable floor on the first
engagement so the buyer's risk is capped while the price stands. Move: when
the objection is that your promised upside is unproven, guarantee a floor and
keep the upside risk yourself rather than dropping the rate. The guarantee is
a term; it costs you only if you cannot deliver. Genuinely obscure. PODCAST.
Source: Adam Foroughi in conversation with David Senra, 2026-05-03.
`david-senra/2026-05-03-bootstrapping-a-business-to-5-billion-in-free-cash-flow-applovins-adam-foroughi.md`

**C8. Dyson's back-loaded licence.** Situation: licensing the cyclonic vacuum
to a Japanese firm that understood the product and closed in three weeks. The
trade on the table was a modest guaranteed minimum against royalties on sales
reported from another country. Did: accepted it, already knowing big
companies guaranteed fat back-end royalties and then bled lone inventors dry.
Happened: the licensee's product reached $12 million a year in sales while he
collected only the $60,000 guaranteed minimum, because reported sales were
unverifiable from abroad. His rule afterward: take the money up front,
negotiating the upfront fee and audit rights before the royalty rate. Then vs
now: reporting and audit terms can be written into a contract in an
afternoon; the temptation is identical. Cautionary card: which side of a
terms swap to insist on. Move: when you trade price for a share of the
upside, make the term you take front-loaded and verifiable. A back-end promise
you cannot audit is a discount wearing a disguise. Known to business readers.
Source: *Against the Odds: An Autobiography*, James Dyson.
`founders/2024-03-17-james-dyson-against-the-odds.md`

**C9. Plank covered the downside first.** Situation: selling oil-drilling
investments knowing nothing technical about oil, only tax and finance. Did:
structured the offer so the buyer's downside was covered before the upside was
discussed. What he sold was a tax-efficient vehicle: deductions cut the
investor's real exposure to a fraction of the cheque, and any discovery was
upside on top. When later bets stalled on group consensus, he funded them from
his own pocket. Happened: the structure solved his own financing problem at
the same time as the buyer's fear. Apache grew from wells the majors had
abandoned into a company that at one point held fifty-eight subsidiaries. The
file carries no per-deal figure. Then vs now: tax law did the heavy lifting;
the modern equivalents are a guarantee tied to a metric the buyer already
reports, or founder-carried risk on the first deal. Move: when the objection
is the payoff rather than the product, cover the worst case with a rule the
buyer already trusts, and leave the price alone. Genuinely obscure.
Source: *A Small Difference*, Raymond Plank.
`founders/2026-08-01-how-raymond-plank-made-a-fortune-in-big-oils-scraps.md`

# D. Price as a signal, discounting as damage

**D1. The moderate price that read as a defect.** Situation: selling
one-of-a-kind objects to buyers with money and no way to judge quality except
by the number attached. Did: a rival dealer offered a bust at $25,000.
Duveen's practice for the same class of object was to ask a quarter of a
million. A room of furnishings was offered at $50,000 and spurned. Happened:
the $25,000 bust was refused as suspect. The $50,000 room was turned down,
then later sold for $200,000, with gratitude. The lower price had cost the
sale. Then vs now: a founder's buyer usually has comparables, so this carries
only when the buyer cannot assess the work before paying and reads price as
the proxy, which describes most expertise. Move: before cutting, check
whether the buyer's objection is really that the number is too low for the
claim. Dropping the price after a doubt about quality confirms the doubt.
Genuinely obscure.
Source: *The Days of Duveen*, S.N. Behrman.
`founders/2024-04-26-the-robber-barons-art-dealer-joseph-duveen.md`

**D2. Ellison and the last day of the quarter.** Situation: Oracle's sales
force discounted aggressively at quarter-end to pull revenue forward, and
separate pricing teams re-set prices country by country. Did: let the
discounting run as a revenue tool for years, then reversed it: standardised
pricing so reps quantified business benefit instead of improvising on price,
and consolidated the pricing function into one system. Happened: the
discounting had taught buyers to wait and negotiate on the last day of the
quarter, and undoing that took years. Consolidating the country-by-country
pricing teams cut roughly 200 people's work to fewer than ten. Letting
salespeople write their own contracts and book their own revenue nearly ended
the company in 1991. Then vs now: a solo founder is both the rep and the comp
plan, and the lesson holds at one: the first concession under pressure sets
the pattern for the whole early base, because early customers talk to each
other and to the next prospects. Move: treat the first discount as training
data for every future buyer. Decide the floor before the quarter starts and
hold it through the last day, because the buyer remembers the day you moved.
Known to business readers.
Source: *Softwar*, Matthew Symonds.
`founders/2025-11-04-how-larry-ellison-thinks.md`

**D3. Arnault kills the licences.** Situation: after the frenzy around what
became the Lady Dior bag, third-party licensees were producing discounted
Dior-labelled goods that were widely available. Later, Tiffany was acquired
with a large but low-priced American ticket base. Did: at Dior, killed the
third-party licences producing the discounted goods, making the product less
obtainable and bringing quality back under control. At Tiffany, treated
untapped pricing power as a free lunch and raised the level of what was sold.
Happened: Dior became less obtainable, more desirable and higher margin.
Tiffany's average US ticket went from roughly $500 to roughly $2,000 after the
acquisition. Then vs now: he was removing discounted versions of a brand
people already wanted; a founder's version is any cheap version of the
service that exists, a friend rate, a beta price, a discounted first client,
because it becomes the number the next buyer anchors to. Move: audit every
channel or offer that sells your work at a discount and cut the one that
trades long-term desirability for short-term volume, before the next buyer
uses it as a comparable. Household name.
Source: *The House of Arnault*, Brad Stone and Angelina Rascouet, Bloomberg
Businessweek.
`founders/2024-07-17-rare-bernard-arnault-interview.md`

**D4. Zeckendorf's reputation runs backwards.** Situation: early on, the Astor
association meant buyers paid a premium for anything his firm touched. A
decade later Webb & Knapp had dozens of projects all needing cash and none
yet profitable, and the market could see it. Did: kept dealing while visibly
needing money. Happened: buyers held back, bid low, and waited for the fire
sale instead of paying. The identical reputation mechanism that had earned a
premium ran backwards, and the firm went bankrupt from too many commitments.
Then vs now: his distress was public because his projects were; a founder's
leaks through smaller tells: a fast unprompted discount, an eagerness to close
this week, a reply within minutes to a lowball. Move: keep any sign that you
need this specific deal out of the conversation, because a buyer who smells
necessity stops evaluating your price and starts waiting for you to cut it.
Genuinely obscure.
Source: *Zeckendorf: The Autobiography*, William Zeckendorf.
`founders/2024-03-11-what-i-learned-from-having-lunch-with-sam-zell.md`

**D5. Duveen, Kress, and the same collection.** Situation: Henry Goldman
offered his collection directly to Samuel Kress at Goldman's own price, and
Kress declined. Did: Duveen bought the identical collection, presented it under
his own name, and offered it back to Kress at a much higher price. When
Kress's adviser said he was overpaying, Duveen let the buyer's own wanting do
the arguing rather than debating the figure. Happened: Kress bought the
unchanged collection for millions more than Goldman had asked, and got
irritated at his adviser, not at Duveen. Then vs now: his name was the
wrapper; a pre-revenue founder has no such name yet. What transfers is the
split between the objector and the buyer. Move: separate who is objecting
from who is buying. When the too-high comes from a gatekeeper rather than the
decision-maker, restate what the decision-maker wanted instead of re-arguing
the number with the gatekeeper. Known to business readers.
Source: *The Days of Duveen*, S.N. Behrman.

# E. Classifying the objection

Five classes. Only the first is about price.

- **No money.** The buyer believes the product and wants it now, and the
  number is the block at any date. The counter.
- **Cannot pay now.** The buyer believes it, wants it, and the block is when
  the money exists, not how much. Time swap: a dated deposit against the full
  price (C4), payment timing (E2). Not the counter.
- **Does not believe the claim.** The buyer doubts the product does what it
  says. Not a price problem. The skill that builds the demonstration. Terms
  swapped for a doubter are free inventory and a delay, and the library
  holds no case of it either way (F15).
- **No urgency.** The buyer believes it and can afford it and has no reason
  to decide now. Time swap: possession before payment with a real expiry, or
  a longer window at the same price.
- **Too much risk.** The buyer believes the product but doubts the payoff, or
  cannot survive it not working. Risk swap: consignment, a floor, a
  guarantee, a result-contingent fee. The price does not move; the risk does.

**When the buyer's words name two classes**, classify by the clause that
carries the condition on saying yes. A number qualified by proof, time or
risk takes that class, not money.

**E1. Lauder's objection was shelf risk.** The Harrods buyer's words were
about exclusives and existing commitments, not the product and not the price.
She classified it as risk and answered with consignment; see C2. Move: read
the buyer's exact words for which of the four they name before touching the
number. Known to business readers.
Source: *Estée: A Success Story*, Estée Lauder.
`founders/2024-09-04-estee-lauder.md`

**E2. Rockefeller's Depression was a timing problem.** Situation: Rockefeller
Jr. tried to use the Depression as leverage for a lower number. Did: Duveen
answered that he was not in the stock market and unaffected, then implied he
could help if Rockefeller was short of cash. Happened: financing was on offer;
a discount never was. Then vs now: unchanged. Move: when the buyer cites a
downturn or a cash squeeze, classify it as timing and offer payment timing,
never a lower price. Genuinely obscure.
Source: *The Days of Duveen*, S.N. Behrman.
`founders/2024-04-26-the-robber-barons-art-dealer-joseph-duveen.md`

**E3. Foroughi's developers doubted the payoff, not the product.** App
developers had no reason to trust an unproven network's revenue-share
promise. That is risk about the outcome, not disbelief in the mechanism, and
he answered it with a guaranteed floor; see C7. Move: when the buyer accepts
that the product works but doubts it will pay, that is too much risk, and the
answer is a floor. Genuinely obscure. PODCAST.
Source: Adam Foroughi in conversation with David Senra, 2026-05-03.
`david-senra/2026-05-03-bootstrapping-a-business-to-5-billion-in-free-cash-flow-applovins-adam-foroughi.md`

**E4. Plank's investors feared the downside.** The buyer's doubt was about
what happened to the money if the well produced nothing, not about whether
drilling works. He covered the downside with the tax code before discussing
upside; see C9. Move: a doubt about the worst case is risk, and the term is a
rule the buyer already trusts. Genuinely obscure.
Source: *A Small Difference*, Raymond Plank.
`founders/2026-08-01-how-raymond-plank-made-a-fortune-in-big-oils-scraps.md`

**E5. Morita's tape recorder was in the wrong room.** Situation: Sony's first
tape recorder failed with the Japanese public. The doubt was unanimous: too
expensive, and it read as a toy. Did: stopped arguing and re-aimed the
identical machine at the Japanese Supreme Court, which had a stenographer
shortage. Happened: it sold at once. Then vs now: unchanged. Move: when every
prospect in a segment says too expensive, that is not a price objection to
counter. It is the wrong buyer, and the deal goes to the skill that sorts your
pipeline. Known to business readers.
Source: *Made in Japan*, Akio Morita.
`founders/2025-05-05-the-life-story-of-akio-morita-founder-of-sony.md`

**E6. Southwest's price was a design, not a concession.** Situation: rivals
charged $62 on a route where Southwest charged $15. Did: set the fare from
the decision that the competition was cars and buses rather than airlines.
Happened: cutting fares into one region took annual passengers from 123,000
to 325,000, and the increase alone exceeded the entire prior market. Then vs
now: unchanged. Move: a low price set from a cost structure before any buyer
conversation is strategy. A low price given because a buyer pushed back is a
discount. The two are not the same decision, and this skill only rules on the
second. Known to business readers.
Source: *Nuts!*, Kevin Freiberg and Jackie Freiberg.
`founders/2024-04-09-herb-kelleher-founder-of-southwest-airlines.md`

**E7. Ford cut the price before anyone asked.** Situation: two models at
$2,000 and $1,000. Did: cut them to $750 and $600. Happened: sales went from
about 1,500 cars to about 8,500. Then vs now: a manufacturer whose cost fell
with volume; the cut was arithmetic on his own cost curve, made before any
buyer objected. Move: the same as E6. If the member's price is low by design,
the number on the card is the designed one, and it still does not move in the
room. Household name.
Source: *My Life and Work*, Henry Ford.
`founders/2023-10-06-henry-fords-autobiography.md`

# F. What is not on record

Every one of these was searched for and not found. State the relevant one in
every run. It is a required output line, because a corpus that has been
searched can say what it does not contain, and nothing reasoning from memory
can.

**How to state it.** As an absence, never as a verdict. Holding no case of
a founder holding a price with a single counter does not mean the counter fails.
It means nothing is on record either way, so the member should price the
attempt as untested.

**The words the member reads.** Open with "I have no documented case of",
name their own situation in plain words, then name the closest case and how
it differs. The member never reads the words library, bank, card, corpus or
record of this file, and never a flourish about being the first: an absence
is information, not a warning against the ruling. Never "the library says
no."

**Scope.** Items marked claim-index were checked against the 7,718 bolded
claims. Items marked corpus-wide were also checked against every evidence line
in every file. Where only the index was checked, an evidence line in one of
the roughly 190 files not opened in full could still hold a case.

1. **No one-to-one sales conversation in which a prospect says the price is
   too high and the founder answers with a single counter and holds.** The
   closest is Duveen, who traded time and made no counter at all. The
   one-number rule mirrors what Murphy did as a buyer; no seller doing it is
   on record. Claim-index, plus the Murphy and Duveen files in full.
2. **No founder computed the cost to deliver a specific deal and walked at
   that floor.** Blumkin's ten percent is a standing rule, not a per-deal
   floor. Every documented walk-away is over something other than price:
   Lauren's label, Price's clause, Glock's manufacturing rights. Corpus-wide.
3. **No founder in the library won by lowering a price because a prospect
   pushed back.** Every low-price winner, Ford, Southwest, Blumkin, Price,
   Glock, Carnegie, set the price by design from a cost structure before any
   buyer conversation. The one case of discounting under pressure, Ellison's,
   is recorded as damage. Claim-index, plus five files in full.
4. **No money-back or performance guarantee offered in reply to a price
   objection with a recorded outcome.** Foroughi's guarantee answered risk
   before a price was ever contested. Claim-index.
5. **No seller-side deposit, pre-sale or letter of intent used as a counter
   to too-expensive.** Zeckendorf's option was the buyer's. Claim-index.
6. **No payment-plan or instalment case at all.** Claim-index.
7. **No exclusivity granted in exchange for holding a price.** Every
   exclusivity case is about refusing distribution. Claim-index.
8. **Consignment appears exactly once, Lauder.** The phrase instead of a
   discount appears nowhere. Corpus-wide.
9. **Ogilvy's rule that clients should not haggle the fee has no outcome
   attached.** Nor does a gallerist telling a painter her prices were too low.
   Corpus-wide for both files.
10. **Murphy's rule has an aggregate outcome and no single named deal in which
    the counter was refused and he walked.** Corpus-wide for that file.
11. **The Ellison file documents the cost of discounting, never an instance of
    Oracle holding a floor and what it produced.** Corpus-wide for that file.
12. **No pricing formula anywhere.** No rule for converting hours, costs or
    customer outcomes into a figure. The fixed-markup cases, Blumkin and
    Price, are standing policies over a known cost, not formulas for setting
    a price from scratch. Claim-index.
13. **No services or software business holding a price against a buyer's
    objection to the price.** Every hold against too-expensive is physical
    goods, art, real estate or media. Foroughi's network held its rate
    against a trust objection and answered with a floor, which is a risk swap;
    Zeckendorf's Astor fee pre-empted the objection; Wu's budget reframe is a
    restatement, not a hold. Claim-index.
14. **No interview-era card on holding a number with an outcome.** The
    walk-away lines from recent guests carry no number. Claim-index for the
    interview folder.
15. **No case of a doubter given consignment, an option or a pilot, and what
    happened next.** The rule that terms swapped for a doubter are free
    inventory is this skill's classification, not a founder's recorded
    outcome. Claim-index.
