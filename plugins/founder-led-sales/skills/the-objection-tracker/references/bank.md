# Precedent Bank: The Objection Tracker

Thirty-six cards, every one traced to a named file in
`socialspire/skool/insights/`.

**Why a bank and not a deck.** A list of principles about handling objections
is knowledge the model already holds, so it adds nothing a strong model would
not reach alone. A bank of thirty-six specific, verified cases, most of them
ones no model volunteers, is information. Read the index, route to the
sections the run needs, and read only those. Do not read the whole file on
every run.

**Attribution rule.** Every card is paraphrase, including the ones marked
PODCAST. Not one phrase is quoted and none may be quoted downstream. Book
cards name the book and author. PODCAST cards name the founder, the host and
the date. Neither kind ever goes inside quotation marks, because the card text
is this bank's rendering, not the founder's words. If a member asks for a
quotable line, the bank holds none.

**Obscurity.** Each card is marked household name, known to business readers,
or genuinely obscure. Obscurity is a tiebreak for STANDS ON, never the reason
a card is cited. The card that moved the verdict is the one that is cited.

# A. Who is saying no

**A1. James Dyson and the bag makers.** Situation: a working bagless vacuum,
pitched for a licence to the established makers for about a decade. Did: heard
the same line from every one of them, that if a better vacuum were possible
Hoover would already have made it, and checked whether the objection contained
a technical claim. It did not. Happened: no incumbent ever signed. A company
earning about $500M a year selling bags cannot say yes to a machine with no
bag, whatever it thinks of the engineering. He stopped answering it and went
to buyers who decided fast. Then vs now: he had a patented product a licensee
could ship, so walking had somewhere to go. What transfers is the test, not
the exit. Move: ask the objector to name the specific technical or operational
flaw. If none comes, log the doubt as structural, stop building answers to it,
and hand the deal to the skill that sorts your pipeline. Known to business
readers.
Source: *Against the Odds: An Autobiography*, James Dyson.
`founders/2024-03-17-james-dyson-against-the-odds.md`

**A2. Dyson, the trade, and the public.** Situation: the ballbarrow, before the
vacuum. Did: offered it to builders and retailers, the professional trade, who
rejected it outright. Then offered it straight to the public through a tiny
newspaper drawing. Happened: the public mailed cheques to an unknown company
off that drawing. A single Sunday Times gardening piece sent sales climbing,
and earned media became his publicity model for decades. Then vs now:
newspaper mail-order; the direct channel is far cheaper now. The diagnostic
is unchanged. Move: before concluding the product is wrong, run the identical
offer past end users. A no from the trade and a no from the user are two
different findings, and only the second one counts against the product. Known
to business readers.
Source: *Against the Odds: An Autobiography*, James Dyson.
`founders/2024-03-17-james-dyson-against-the-odds.md`

**A3. Morita and the first tape recorder.** Situation: Sony's first tape
recorder failed with the Japanese public. The doubt was unanimous: too
expensive, and it read as a toy. Did: stopped arguing with the doubt. Re-aimed
the identical machine at a buyer with an urgent, expensive problem, the
Japanese Supreme Court, which had a stenographer shortage. Happened: it sold
at once. Same machine, different buyer. Then vs now: post-war Japan and one
physical product; the modern version is a horizontal tool that only closes
once the department with the shortage is named. Move: when every prospect in
a segment raises the same doubt, that doubt is fatal for the segment, not
answerable inside it. Stop rehearsing the answer and find the buyer for whom
the price is trivially smaller than the problem. Known to business readers.
Source: *Made in Japan*, Akio Morita.
`founders/2025-05-05-the-life-story-of-akio-morita-founder-of-sony.md`

**A4. Duveen, Kress, and the adviser.** Situation: Henry Goldman had offered
his collection directly to Samuel Kress at Goldman's own price, and Kress
declined. Duveen bought the identical collection, unchanged, and offered it
back to Kress under his own name at a much higher price. Kress's professional
adviser told Kress he was overpaying. Did: did not argue the figure with the
adviser. Answered the buyer's own wanting. Happened: Kress bought the same
collection for millions more than he had refused it at, and got irritated at
the adviser, not at Duveen. Then vs now: Kress's adviser counselled; he did
not sign. A modern finance lead or procurement head can veto, so this holds
only when the objector advises rather than approves. Move: before answering,
write down whether the person raising the doubt can sign. A doubt from someone
who cannot is a doubt about the room, and the answer goes to the person who
can. Known to business readers.
Source: *The Days of Duveen*, S.N. Behrman.
`founders/2024-04-26-the-robber-barons-art-dealer-joseph-duveen.md`

**A5. The moderate price that read as a defect.** Situation: selling
one-of-a-kind objects to buyers who had money but no way to judge quality
except by the number attached. Did: a rival dealer offered a bust at $25,000.
Duveen's practice for the same class of object was to ask a quarter of a
million. A room of furnishings was offered at $50,000 and spurned. Happened:
the $25,000 bust was refused as suspect. The $50,000 room was turned down,
then later sold for $200,000, with gratitude. The lower price had cost the
sale. Then vs now: a founder's buyer usually has comparables, so this carries
only when the buyer cannot assess the work before paying and reads price as
the proxy. Move: when the doubt is about the work's quality, check whether the
number is too low for the claim before assuming it is too high. A doubt about
quality answered with a discount confirms the doubt. Genuinely obscure.
Source: *The Days of Duveen*, S.N. Behrman.
`founders/2024-04-26-the-robber-barons-art-dealer-joseph-duveen.md`

# B. The sequence

**B1. The doubts arrived in sequence at Apple.** Situation: after the return, every answered doubt
produced a new one. Did: mapped the whole sequence in advance instead of
resenting it. Each time scepticism about survival was answered, the question
became stability, then strategy, then growth. Happened: the file records the
ladder and the decision to get ahead of it, and attaches no figure. Then vs
now: the sequence is about a company; a buyer runs the same ladder on a
vendor, and the rungs are the same. Move: for the top live doubt, write the
doubt that arrives the moment it is satisfied, and the one after that, and
name the proof for each now. Household name, and the only ranked sequence of
doubts in the library.
Source: *Make Something Wonderful*, the Steve Jobs Archive.
`founders/2024-03-14-steve-jobs-make-something-wonderful.md`

**B2. Three objections to the wristwatch, in order.** Situation: industry
veterans gave three doubts about a wristwatch for men: the movement was too
small and delicate, dust and water would ruin it, and it could never keep
accurate time. The trade treated the wristwatch as a woman's accessory. Did:
answered them in sequence with proofs rather than arguments, each proof from a
standard the trade already respected. Happened: the accuracy proof came first,
in 1914, and the water proof in 1927; each removed a rung and the next rung
was waiting. Rolex has been the top US watch brand since the 1950s. Then vs
now: the proofs took years each; today's equivalents arrive in weeks and are
easier for a rival to match. Move: write the doubts in the order the buyer
will raise them, and build the proof for the first before the second is
asked. Known to business readers.
Source: *Rolex Jubilee Vade Mecum*, Hans Wilsdorf.
`founders/2024-07-08-the-founder-of-rolex-hans-wilsdorf.md`

**B3. Turner and the number that did not exist.** Situation: national
advertisers would not buy the Atlanta superstation because Nielsen refused to
measure its audience. The doubt was not about the product; it was that the
pitch had no number the buyer's world accepted. Did: sorted incoming
direct-response cheques by postmark into Atlanta and non-Atlanta piles. The
outside pile dwarfed the local one. Sold that evidence to small
direct-response advertisers who needed him more than he needed them.
Happened: direct-response revenue carried the station through the gap. It took
nearly two years to win meaningful national buys, and once a few large players
validated the concept the rest followed. Then vs now: the accepted metric was
controlled by one agency; today the founder can usually produce the buyer's
metric from their own data. Move: when the doubt is that there is no number,
the next doubt is whether your number is real. Build it in the buyer's own
currency and sell first to whoever needs you most. Household name.
Source: *Call Me Ted*, Ted Turner.
`founders/2024-04-14-the-autobiography-of-ted-turner.md`

# C. The precedent the buyer already accepts

**C1. Morita and the car stereo.** Situation: nobody inside Sony wanted the
Walkman. Engineers, marketers and executives all said the same thing: a
recorder that cannot record will not sell. Did: did not argue the merits.
Pointed at the millions of car stereos already installed and paid for that
also record nothing, took personal responsibility for the decision, and
shipped it. Happened: the product shipped over the objection of the whole
room and created its category. The file carries no unit figure. Then vs now:
this was an internal objection and Morita had the authority to overrule it. A
founder facing a buyer cannot, so the precedent has to carry the whole load;
pick one the buyer already pays for, not one they merely know about. Move:
name the market the buyer already spends money in that has the exact same
supposed flaw, and say it in their world's terms. Known to business readers,
and the anchor for this procedure.
Source: *Made in Japan*, Akio Morita.
`founders/2025-05-05-the-life-story-of-akio-morita-founder-of-sony.md`

**C2. Graves and In-N-Out.** Situation: every bank turned him down. His
business-school professor gave the plan the worst grade in the class. The
experts agreed a chicken-fingers-only restaurant could not work in Louisiana.
Did: while working refineries in Los Angeles to fund the gap himself, he ate
at In-N-Out, saw a single menu unchanged since 1948 while dozens of burger
chains opened and died around it, and put In-N-Out into the loan pitch as
living proof of single-product focus. Happened: he credits the In-N-Out
precedent with finally landing the SBA loan. The first month cleared thirty
dollars, meaning payroll, rent and vendors were covered. Then vs now: the
precedent had to be seen in person and carried to a local banker; now the
buyer checks it on their phone during the call, so it has to survive a
search. Move: find the company already succeeding for decades on the exact
principle the buyer doubts, and name it before they raise the doubt. Known to
business readers. PODCAST.
Source: Todd Graves in conversation with David Senra, 2025-11-09.
`david-senra/2025-11-09-how-todd-graves-built-raising-canes.md`

**C3. Wu and the incumbent that already has it.** Situation: the objection
heard constantly from investors and buyers was that Microsoft already had
GitHub Copilot, plus GitHub, the OpenAI partnership and VS Code. Did: did not
deny the incumbent's position. Answered that the objection assumes a single
known end state where the most resourced player wins, and specified the niche
that assumption misses. On the show the host supplied the precedent the
objection ignores: Datadog, Snowflake and Databricks all exist beside cloud
providers who care about the same problems. The precedent list is the host's
framing; the niche answer is Wu's. Happened: Microsoft became a partner rather
than the company that killed them. The file carries no revenue figure. Then vs
now: the precedent list is now long and familiar, so the founder's job is to
pick the two the buyer already uses, not to explain the pattern. Move: when
told the incumbent covers this, name two companies the buyer already respects
that grew in an incumbent's shadow, then name the niche the incumbent's end
state misses. Known to business readers. PODCAST.
Source: Scott Wu in conversation with David Senra, 2026-06-28.
`david-senra/2026-06-28-the-future-of-software-ai-cognitions-scott-wu.md`

**C4. Stoute and the translation.** Situation: corporate clients could not see
why a commercial pairing of Jay-Z and 50 Cent made sense. They had no
reference point for that audience. Did: translated the idea into a precedent
from the client's own world: for that audience, the pairing was the
equivalent of Oprah and Martha Stewart. He calls it the only translation
available. He built the wider Reebok platform on the same move. Happened: the
clients bought the translation, and the platform built on it changed
Reebok's trajectory. The file carries no dollar figure. Then vs now: the
analogy bridged a gap the buyer could not research; now the buyer can look
anything up, so the analogy has to be exact rather than flattering. Move:
prepare one analogy from the buyer's own world for every idea they have no
reference point for, and say it before you explain the mechanism. Known to
business readers. PODCAST.
Source: Steve Stoute in conversation with David Senra, 2026-06-21.
`david-senra/2026-06-21-the-marketing-genius-of-steve-stoute.md`

**C5. Noyce and the one buyer who ended the argument.** Situation: the
integrated circuit was a new component with no track record, and a rival had
announced his version a month after Noyce's first notes. Buyers had no reason
to trust it. Did: rather than run a campaign, Fairchild won the one customer
whose choice would settle it for everyone else: NASA selected Noyce's circuits
for the first spacecraft computers. Happened: orders poured in after the NASA
selection. Fairchild went from a few thousand dollars in revenue to about
$130 million in ten years. Then vs now: the credibility customer was a
government programme with a public budget for failure; today it is more
likely a respected operator in the buyer's own peer group, possibly a small
one. Move: identify the one buyer whose name would end the argument for the
rest, win them first, and lead every conversation with that name. Known to
business readers.
Source: *The Tinkerings of Robert Noyce*, Tom Wolfe.
`founders/2024-07-18-how-the-sun-rose-on-silicon-valley-bob-noyce-founder-of-intel.md`

**C6. Wilsdorf and the observatory.** Situation: the accuracy objection, from
a trade that trusted marine chronometers and nothing on a wrist. Did: pushed
his supplier to build a wristwatch worthy of an observatory rating and in
1914 won the first Class A chronometer certificate ever awarded to a
wristwatch. For the water objection, bought the waterproof seal patent about
sixty days after it was granted, put the watch on a swimmer attempting the
English Channel in 1927, bought the Daily Mail front page to announce it had
run perfectly for ten hours, and had retailers show the watch submerged in
aquariums in their windows. Happened: the certificate converted a claim into
a verified fact; a buyer who wanted accuracy had one obviously correct choice.
Then vs now: the equivalent today is a security audit, a compliance
certification or a benchmark the buyer's industry already publishes. Move:
submit the product to the third-party test the buyer already respects in an
adjacent category, and put the result where the objection usually surfaces.
Known to business readers.
Source: *Rolex Jubilee Vade Mecum*, Hans Wilsdorf.
`founders/2024-07-08-the-founder-of-rolex-hans-wilsdorf.md`

**C7. Plank leaned on something the buyer already trusted.** Situation: selling
oil-drilling investments knowing nothing technical about oil, only tax and
finance. The buyer's live doubt was the obvious one about the downside. Did:
structured the offer so the downside was covered before any upside was
discussed. What he sold was a tax-efficient vehicle: deductions the investor
already accepted from the tax code cut their real exposure to a fraction of
the cheque, and any discovery was upside on top. When later bets stalled on
group consensus, he funded them from his own pocket until they took root.
Happened: the structure solved his own financing problem at the same time as
the buyer's fear. Apache grew from wells the majors had abandoned into a
company that at one point held fifty-eight subsidiaries. The file carries no
per-deal figure. Then vs now: tax law did the heavy lifting; the modern
equivalents are a guarantee tied to a metric the buyer already reports, or
founder-carried risk on the first deal. Move: cover the buyer's worst case
with a rule they already trust before discussing upside. When the doubt is
about the payoff rather than the product, this is the answer, and the skill
that holds your price has the term. Genuinely obscure.
Source: *A Small Difference*, Raymond Plank.
`founders/2026-08-01-how-raymond-plank-made-a-fortune-in-big-oils-scraps.md`

# D. Restating the objection

**D1. Jobs at NeXT, selling to Apple.** Situation: competing for a
company-defining deal against a rival whose buyer's team remembered the
younger Jobs dismissing their concerns. Earlier in the same file, the
unprepared version had killed a $30M IBM deal by refusing to fly to Dallas
over a slide problem. Did: acknowledged each of Apple's concerns instead of
dismissing them, restating each as a solvable engineering problem before
answering it. Brought his head of software engineering and a live demo and
handed the floor to the builder rather than performing himself. When the sale
price came up, conceded it in a five-minute kitchen conversation. Happened:
Apple bought NeXT. The rival brought no team, no laptop and no presentation,
and lost. The file credits the restating with making the mature version
credible where the younger one was not, and carries no sale price. Then vs
now: he had a finished operating system to point at, so every restated
objection came with an existing answer; a founder restating still owes the
evidence. Move: restate each objection out loud before answering it, then let
the person who built the thing show it working. Household name, and the only
statement of the restate-then-answer pattern in the library.
Source: *Steve Jobs in Exile*, Geoffrey Cain.
`founders/2026-06-03-steve-jobs-in-exile.md`

**D2. Peterffy and the screen thirty feet away.** Situation: the exchange
banned him from using any computing device in the pit. The only thing allowed
was a screen thirty feet away, which made his algorithmic pricing useless to
his traders in real time. Did: did not appeal the rule or argue it was
arbitrary. Treated the constraint as a specification and rewrote his code so
each digit of the price flashed as a colour his traders could read across the
room. Happened: his traders kept trading on machine-generated prices inside a
pit that had banned machines. Then vs now: he was routing around an exchange
rule with engineering he controlled end to end; a buyer's compliance or
procurement constraint usually needs the buyer's cooperation, so the reframe
has to be offered as a joint spec. Move: write the buyer's policy objection
down as a spec line with the exact limit they named, and design the version of
the offer that fits inside it before the next call. Known to business readers.
Source: David Senra reading a Colossus profile of Thomas Peterffy, 2025-10-05. PODCAST.
`founders/2025-10-05-the-billionaire-who-automates-everything-thomas-peterffy.md`

**D3. Lauder at Harrods.** Situation: the buyer said the cosmetics category was
locked up by exclusives. The objection was shelf risk and existing
commitments, not interest in the product, and she had already been rejected
once. Did: restated the objection as the buyer held it, a category rule and a
risk, and answered each. Pitched a bath oil instead of a cosmetic, a category
the exclusives did not cover, and offered it on consignment so that if it did
not sell the buyer had lost nothing. Took a tiny slot buried among general
toiletries. Shipped creams the buyer had not ordered with a note that he would
not want to tell a customer he lacked the rest of the line. Got a magazine to
announce the line was coming to London before any distribution existed.
Happened: after three trips and repeated press rounds, customers were asking
for her by name and the buyer moved her to a prestigious counter. The bath oil
was Youth Dew, and the file records that reframe growing from about $50K in
1953 to over $150M by 1984. Then vs now: consignment cost her inventory
carry she could afford; a service founder's version is deferred billing, and
only once the cost to deliver is known. Move: say the objection back as a risk
or as a category rule, whichever the buyer actually said, then remove the
risk yourself or move the same product into a category they already buy
freely. Known to business readers.
Source: *Estée: A Success Story*, Estée Lauder.
`founders/2024-09-04-estee-lauder.md`

**D4. Wu and the buyer's own budget.** Situation: selling an AI engineering
agent into large banks, where a vendor typically takes twelve to eighteen
months to clear security review. Buyers also raised cost. Did: treated the
procurement timeline as an engineering problem with an owner and a deadline.
Restated the cost objection into the buyer's own budgeted project: a job
scoped at eighteen months and $15 million with an outsourced contractor,
restated as done internally in three months for $1 million, tied to actual
tickets. Volunteered the projects the product cannot speed up so the rest of
the claims were credible. Happened: by his account the company lands in large
organisations in about three months when the customer treats it as a
priority. Then vs now: the three-month figure is the founder's own claim in an
interview, from a well-funded company selling to buyers who can prioritise a
vendor. What transfers is naming both numbers and owning the deadline for the
piece within reach. Move: restate a timeline or budget objection as a project
with both numbers named, assign an owner and a date to your side of it, and
volunteer the one thing your product cannot do. Known to business readers.
PODCAST.
Source: Scott Wu in conversation with David Senra, 2026-06-28.
`david-senra/2026-06-28-the-future-of-software-ai-cognitions-scott-wu.md`

# E. Facts over adjectives, and the third-party voice

**E1. Ogilvy, door to door and Rolls-Royce.** Situation: selling door to door
before he had an agency, then writing for clients in a trade that ran on
adjectives. Did: found door to door that the more information he gave, the
more he sold. For Rolls-Royce, pulled the headline from roughly page fifty of
an internal engineering document nobody else had read, and ran ads carrying
facts with no puffery. Held that a fellow customer's endorsement is believed
more readily than an anonymous copywriter's claim. Happened: the file records
the direction rather than a sales figure: more facts sold more, and Claude
Hopkins had reached the same finding fifty years earlier. His measured range
was that one ad can outsell another by as much as nineteen to one, and the
fact-led Hathaway campaign ran twenty-one years. Then vs now: a fact can now
be verified instantly, which makes a checkable fact worth more and an
uncheckable adjective worth less. Move: replace every adjective in the answer
with a fact the buyer can check, and let an existing customer say the thing
you most want to claim. Known to business readers.
Source: *Confessions of an Advertising Man*, David Ogilvy.
`founders/2024-03-24-david-ogilvy-confessions-of-an-advertising-man.md`

**E2. The same number, restated.** Situation: numbers that could be heard as
weaknesses. Did: asked about Apple's five percent share, Jobs compared it to
BMW and Mercedes, neither of which anyone considers disadvantaged. Four
million iPhones in two hundred days became twenty thousand a day. For the
MacBook Air, one line, the thinnest notebook, was restated seven separate ways
across the keynote, the press release, the site and the marketing. Happened:
the file records the technique, not a sales result attached to it. Then vs
now: a keynote is uninterrupted; in a sales conversation the restatement has
to survive the buyer's follow-up question. Move: for every number in the
answer, write the version the buyer will feel, and check it is still true.
Household name.
Source: *The Presentation Secrets of Steve Jobs*, Carmine Gallo.
`founders/2024-07-03-how-to-sell-like-steve-jobs.md`

**E3. Kreuger's seven words.** Situation: raising very large sums from American
investors for a structure they had never seen. Did: compressed the whole offer
to seven words, government loans exchanged for match monopolies, so investors
could repeat it. Practised his lines for hours, before parties and casual
meetings as well as presentations, so that people described him as speaking
in fully formed paragraphs. Happened: the structure raised capital for seven
years. He then signed a $125M loan he could not fund over the same weekend as
the largest two-day market decline in history, and the enterprise was a fraud
by the time markets closed in 1929. Then vs now: cautionary. The compression
worked exactly as intended, which is the warning: polished delivery moved
investors while the substance was rotten. Move: cut the answer to under ten
words so the buyer can carry it to the next decision-maker without you, and
use the technique only on a claim you would hand to due diligence. Genuinely
obscure.
Source: *The Match King*, Frank Partnoy.
`founders/2024-05-15-the-financial-genius-behind-a-century-of-wall-street-scandals-ivar-kreuger.md`

**E4. Blumkin and the supplier's bill.** Situation: a customer, or a court,
calling her price wrong. Did: her standing practice was to produce the
supplier's bill to show her actual cost. When competitors sued her over carpet
at $3.95 against their $7.95, she told the judge she sold at ten percent over
cost and asked what was wrong with that. Happened: the case was thrown out and
the judge bought carpet from her the next day. Then vs now: a retailer's cost
is an invoice; a service founder's cost is hours, and harder to show. What
transfers is answering a doubt about the number with the fact behind it. Move:
when the doubt is about price, the answer is the cost basis, not an adjective
about value. Hand the deal to the skill that holds your price with this
deal's cost to deliver as the first thing to do. Known to business readers.
Source: *The Women of Berkshire Hathaway*, Karen Linder.
`founders/2024-03-20-rose-blumkin-warren-buffetts-favorite-founder.md`

# F. Rehearsal, preparation, conviction

**F1. Duveen and the secretary.** Situation: selling single objects at record
prices to eight to ten of the richest men alive, each with a professional
adviser in the room. Did: the day before a meeting, acted out the likely
conversation with his secretary, mapping the branches the buyer might take, so
the real conversation looked spontaneous. Kept a written model of each client
and repeated the same few lines for decades. Happened: ninety-five of the 115
paintings in Andrew Mellon's collection came through him. Then vs now: his
buyers had effectively infinite money, so a price objection was about status;
what transfers is the stand-in rehearsal and the fixed answer to a known
objection. The file also records intelligence-gathering that was a breach of
trust; take the rehearsal, not the espionage. Move: run the conversation out
loud the day before with someone playing the buyer, script the branches for
the two or three objections you already know are coming, and answer each with
the same line you used last time. Genuinely obscure, and the only documented
case in the library of rehearsing a sale with a stand-in playing the buyer.
Source: *The Days of Duveen*, S.N. Behrman.
`founders/2024-04-26-the-robber-barons-art-dealer-joseph-duveen.md`

**F2. Ninety to one.** Situation: product launches where the keynote was the
sale. Did: rehearsed at the book's benchmark for elite speakers, roughly
ninety hours of practice per hour on stage. Once arrived four hours late to an
interview because he was still practising. Began stepping through a keynote
weeks out and built it up until he knew it cold. Happened: the file records
the ratio and the habit, not a figure attached to a single keynote. Then vs
now: a keynote is one-to-many with no interruption; a sales conversation is
interrupted by the objection at the moment the founder least wants it. The
ratio transfers, the format does not. Move: rehearse the branch where the
buyer pushes back, not the monologue, at a ratio you would currently find
embarrassing. Household name, and the only preparation-to-delivery ratio in
the library.
Source: *The Presentation Secrets of Steve Jobs*, Carmine Gallo; timeline from
*Creative Selection*, Ken Kocienda.
`founders/2024-07-03-how-to-sell-like-steve-jobs.md`
`founders/2024-01-16-working-with-steve-jobs.md`

**F3. Lauder before Saks.** Situation: told no repeatedly by department-store
buyers, carrying four products and no distribution. Did: mentally rehearsed
the sale to a large department store step by step before it happened. Before
the Saks debut, mailed her existing clientele and Saks charge-account holders
an elegant card announcing the line, so the store's own customers walked in
asking for her by name. Happened: broke into Saks with four products and an
$800 order after repeated refusals. Everyone she had ever sampled showed up
and the launch sold out in two days. Then vs now: her rehearsal was solitary
walk-through, not role-play. Her buyer's objection was demand risk, which she
removed before the room rather than arguing in it. Move: walk the entire sale
step by step before the meeting, including the exact moment the buyer says
no, and pre-load the room with customers who already want the thing. Known to
business readers.
Source: *Estée: A Success Story*, Estée Lauder.
`founders/2024-09-04-estee-lauder.md`

**F4. Helzberg and the thirty seconds.** Situation: wanted one specific
acquirer for reasons that were not price, and had no introduction. Did: bought
Berkshire shares purely to attend annual meetings for years and absorb the
buyer's published criteria. Meeting Buffett on a New York street, made a
pitch that lasted under thirty seconds: a match against Buffett's stated
criteria plus a name. Then, told the numbers would be confidential, sent
nothing for a while and had to scold himself in the mirror for procrastinating.
Happened: Buffett bought the company. The file gives no figure. Then vs now:
most buyers do not publish their criteria, so the founder has to extract them
in discovery before rehearsing the match. The hesitation transfers exactly.
Move: learn the buyer's own stated criteria before the room, rehearse a
two-sentence match you can say in thirty seconds, and send the information
the same day. Genuinely obscure.
Source: *What I Learned Before I Sold to Warren Buffett*, Barnett C. Helzberg
Jr.
`founders/2024-01-09-what-i-learned-before-i-sold-to-warren-buffett.md`

**F5. Knight, flat and then not.** Situation: a failed salesman twice over,
before the shoes. Did: failed selling encyclopedias and felt dead inside
selling mutual funds. The pitch would not come out because he did not believe
in the product. Selling Japanese running shoes he actually ran in, he was not
selling at all; belief did the work and he could not write orders fast enough.
Presented the thesis to a business-school classroom and got zero questions,
pure boredom. Happened: the classroom silence meant nothing. The company
passed $1B and the IPO morning left him worth $178M. Then vs now: the same man
was a bad encyclopedia salesman and an unstoppable shoe salesman, so the
flatness was product-side, not skill-side. A flat room of evaluators who will
never buy is not an objection. Move: say the pitch out loud and notice whether
you can get excited. If you cannot, the problem is the offer, and no rehearsal
fixes it. Household name.
Source: *Shoe Dog*, Phil Knight.
`founders/2026-05-07-phil-knight-founder-of-nike.md`

**F6. The two failures investors named.** Situation: founder pitches, as
described by the investors in Gallo's book. Did: named the two most common
failures. Opening with the product instead of the problem. Pushing more
information than the listener can receive. Happened: the file records the
diagnosis and the rule that follows: if you cannot get excited saying your own
pitch out loud, fix the product or the pitch before you fix the slides. Then
vs now: unchanged. Move: in rehearsal, cut every point where you reached for
a slide instead of a proof, and every point where you explained instead of
showed. Household name.
Source: *The Presentation Secrets of Steve Jobs*, Carmine Gallo.
`founders/2024-07-03-how-to-sell-like-steve-jobs.md`

**F7. Ovitz and the 150 presidents.** Situation: pitching 150 university
presidents on a college football playoff, alongside Phil Knight. A committee
room with no single decider. Did: built a full presentation and ran a
rehearsed dual pitch. Mid-presentation, crossed the stage to Knight, said it
was not going well, and they started improvising. Happened: they were
ignored. The NCAA later commissioned a roughly 500-page report that reached
exactly their conclusion and adopted it about thirty years later. His lesson:
being right is not enough; plan how the recommendation gets adopted, not just
how it gets presented. Then vs now: a committee of 150 is the worst case for a
rehearsed set-piece. Counterweight card. Move: rehearse the pitch and also
rehearse abandoning it: decide in advance the signal that means the room has
stopped listening, and plan how a yes gets adopted by the people not in the
room. Known to business readers. PODCAST.
Source: Michael Ovitz in conversation with David Senra, 2026-08-09.
`david-senra/2026-08-09-lessons-from-hollywood-super-agent-legendary-dealmaker-michael-ovitz.md`

**F8. The deal that died in Dallas.** Situation: a $30M IBM deal in the NeXT
years. Did: refused to fly to Dallas over a slide-duplication problem.
Happened: the deal died. The file reads the no-show as lack of commitment, and
sets it against the later Apple sale, where he showed up prepared and
personally. Then vs now: unchanged. Move: the rehearsal is worth nothing if
the founder does not turn up. Put the meeting, the travel and the demo on the
calendar before rehearsing a word. Household name.
Source: *Steve Jobs in Exile*, Geoffrey Cain.

# G. What the words mean

**G1. Dyson and the eight-month handle.** Situation: several potential
licensing partners in play. One stalled him for eight months with endless
handle redesigns. Lawyers elsewhere argued single clauses for months. A
Japanese firm understood the product immediately. Did: read slowness itself as
the signal and put his effort on the party that decided fast. Happened: the
Japanese firm closed in three weeks; the eight-month staller never intended to
produce anything. The cautionary half: the licensee's product reached $12M a
year in sales while Dyson collected only the $60,000 guaranteed minimum,
because reported sales were unverifiable from abroad. Then vs now: the
cross-border trap is smaller now; the prospect asking for one more handle
redesign is unchanged and is the most common false positive in a pipeline.
Move: a stream of minor objections after the big one is answered is a no that
has not been said. Mark it as one, and put a decision date on the buyer.
Known to business readers.
Source: *Against the Odds: An Autobiography*, James Dyson.
`founders/2024-03-17-james-dyson-against-the-odds.md`

**G2. Knight's silent classroom.** Situation: presenting the Japan-imports
thesis to a business-school classroom. Did: got zero questions. Happened:
pure boredom, and it meant nothing; see F5. Then vs now: unchanged. Move: a
flat room of people who will never buy is not an objection and not a yes. Log
only the words of people who could sign. Household name.
Source: *Shoe Dog*, Phil Knight.
`founders/2026-05-07-phil-knight-founder-of-nike.md`

**G3. Helzberg's week after.** Situation: a pitch that went well, and a buyer
who had said in person and in writing that the numbers would be confidential.
Did: sent nothing for a while, and had to scold himself in the mirror for
procrastinating. Happened: the deal nearly died in the week after a good
pitch, not in it. Then vs now: unchanged. Move: after a meeting that went
well, the founder's own delay is the live objection. Send the information the
same day. Genuinely obscure.
Source: *What I Learned Before I Sold to Warren Buffett*, Barnett C. Helzberg
Jr.
`founders/2024-01-09-what-i-learned-before-i-sold-to-warren-buffett.md`

**G4. Foroughi and the objection nobody stated.** Situation: a $1B investment
at a $1.4B valuation from a China-based fund for 70% control, reviewed by
CFIUS and agencies including the NSA for more than a year. Nobody in the room
ever stated the actual objection; they asked about unrelated things while
taking notes. Did: inferred the real concern from what the reviewers asked
about: a large mobile data platform passing under foreign control. Treated it
as a structural problem with a threshold rather than a rejection, and
converted the equity deal into a $1B convertible note that converts to under
10%, beneath the control line. Happened: the deal survived. The billion was
paid out to shareholders as a pre-IPO liquidity round, the leftover
convertible debt brought KKR in, and the business doubled to well over $100
million of EBITDA during the freeze. Then vs now: a government review has a
published threshold; a buyer's unstated objection rarely does, so the founder
has to name the line in the buyer's words and get it confirmed. Move: when
the buyer circles something they will not say, restate it in the terms their
questions reveal and ask whether a version below their threshold would clear
it. Known to business readers. PODCAST.
Source: Adam Foroughi in conversation with David Senra, 2026-05-03.
`david-senra/2026-05-03-bootstrapping-a-business-to-5-billion-in-free-cash-flow-applovins-adam-foroughi.md`

**G5. Duveen, Mellon, and the next day.** Situation: Andrew Mellon, his most
important client, hesitated and passed on a painting. Did: sold it the next
day to Collis Huntington at the same price instead of chasing Mellon or
reopening the number. Happened: the painting stayed in the Huntington
collection for decades, and Mellon never hesitated on a Duveen offer again.
Then vs now: a single object that could only go to one buyer; the founder's
version is a delivery slot that genuinely goes to someone else. It only works
if the scarcity is real. Move: a hesitation is not an objection. Do not build
proof against it. Let the hesitating buyer lose the thing once, at the same
price. Genuinely obscure.
Source: *The Days of Duveen*, S.N. Behrman.
`founders/2024-04-26-the-robber-barons-art-dealer-joseph-duveen.md`

# H. What is not on record

Every one of these was searched for and not found. State the relevant one in
every run. It is a required output line, because a corpus that has been
searched can say what it does not contain, and nothing reasoning from memory
can.

**How to state it.** As an absence, never as a verdict. Holding no case of
a founder repeating an objection back word for word does not mean restating fails.
It means nothing is on record either way, so the member should price the
attempt as untested.

**The words the member reads.** Open with "I have no documented case of",
name their own situation in plain words, then name the closest case and how
it differs. The member never reads the words library, bank, card, corpus or
record of this file, and never a flourish about being the first: an absence
is information, not a warning against the ruling. Never "the library says
no."

**Scope.** Items marked claim-index were checked against the 7,718 bolded
claims. Items marked corpus-wide were also checked against every evidence
line in every file. Where only the index was checked, an evidence line in one
of the roughly 190 files not opened in full could still hold a case.

1. **No case where a buyer's objection is recorded as fatal and the founder
   later named which doubt killed it.** The nearest are Duveen's rival losing a
   sale because a $25,000 bust read as suspect, and Jobs's IBM deal dying from
   his own no-show. Claim-index. The stack's whole method, counting doubts
   against outcomes, has no founder precedent; it is this skill's rule.
2. **One ranked sequence of doubts in the library, and it carries no figure.**
   Jobs's ladder: survival, stability, strategy, growth. The coming-next rule
   rests on it alone. Claim-index.
3. **No founder is recorded repeating a buyer's objection back in the buyer's
   own words before answering.** The Jobs in Exile claim characterises his
   mature style and does not say which concerns he restated. Claim-index.
4. **No founder closed a live sale by pointing at a named peer of the buyer
   who already uses the product.** The nearest are institutional validators:
   NASA for Noyce, the first national advertisers for Turner. The phrase
   reference customer appears in zero files. Corpus-wide.
5. **Testimonial appears in one claim, Ogilvy's. Case study appears in one,

6. **The anchor for the precedent answer cannot be numbered.** The Morita file
   carries no Walkman sales figure. The Jobs in Exile file carries no NeXT sale

7. **No documented case of flat or unconvinced delivery costing a named deal
   with a number.** Knight's flatness is product-side. Claim-index.
8. **One preparation-to-delivery ratio in the library, Gallo's ninety to one,
   for a stage, with no second source.** Nothing for a sales call.
   Corpus-wide.
9. **One documented case of rehearsing a sale with a stand-in playing the

   choreography, or speech practice with no sales outcome. Corpus-wide.
10. **No close rate, objection frequency or conversion figure anywhere.** No

11. **No price objection resolved by discounting successfully.** Every

12. **Almost every objection in the library is internal, from investors, or

13. **No founder converted a buyer's product objection into a documented

14. **Two counterweights to rehearsal exist and neither carries an outcome.**
