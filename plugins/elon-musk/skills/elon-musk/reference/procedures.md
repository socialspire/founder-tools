# Procedures: Elon Musk

Four procedures. All four are written as instructions to the AI running them on
a member's real material.

**Attribution rule applies here too: paraphrase only, never quotation marks,
name the book.**

Before running any of these, you must have confirmed the member's situation and
received a yes. See Step 1 of SKILL.md. These procedures execute correctly on a
wrong premise and return a confident wrong answer.

---

## 1. The Algorithm

### What it is

The named, ordered, five-step method that the library treats as the single most
important idea in the whole body of work on him. Two separate books lay out the
same five steps in the same sequence — make requirements less dumb, delete,
simplify and optimize, accelerate, automate.

The library records his own confession that he repeatedly ran them backwards,
automating and speeding up things that should have been deleted, and that
reversing the order wastes years. This is a genuine documented procedure, not
loose principles assembled into a list. The library also records him repeating
it in meetings until people could finish his sentences.

Source: *The Book of Elon: Elon Musk's Most Useful Ideas in His Own Words*, Eric
Jorgenson.

### When to run it

The member pasted a feature list, an offer, a spec, an onboarding flow, an
outreach sequence, a landing page, a service delivery process, or anything with
steps. Also run it whenever they ask you to make something simpler, faster, or
more automated — see the refusal condition.

### What to ask for before you start

The process, offer, or product written out. If they describe it in prose, ask
them to give you the steps or the parts. If they cannot list them, that is the
first finding: they do not know what their own process is.

### The steps

1. **Flatten it.** Rewrite what they gave you as a flat numbered list. One line
   per requirement, step, feature, or part. Do not group or nest anything.
   Grouping hides the count. Show them the count.

2. **Demand a name on every line.** Go line by line and ask who authored it.
   Replace every institutional owner with the name of a specific human being.
   Legal, safety, best practice, the platform, everyone does it, and the course
   I bought are not owners. Any line that comes back without a human name is an
   orphan. Mark it.
   The library flags the trap here: requirements from smart people are the most
   dangerous, because nobody bothers to challenge them. A named owner is not the
   same as a good reason. It is only a reason you can now argue with.

3. **Delete every orphan immediately.** Then keep deleting into the lines that
   do have names, past the point of comfort. The target is to cut enough that
   they will be forced to restore about a tenth of it. Tell them the target
   explicitly, because it converts over-cutting from a mistake into the
   instrument.

4. **Run the stripped version for a week.** Restore only what visibly broke, and
   log what came back. If nothing had to come back, send them to step 3 again.
   They did not cut enough. The add-back rate is the measurement.

5. **Only now simplify and optimize what survived.** Do not arrive here directly
   from step 1. Simplifying a process that should never have existed is the
   classic mistake the whole ordering exists to prevent.

6. **Only after that, accelerate.** Cut the cycle time of the steps that remain.

7. **Automate last**, and only once requirements have been questioned and the
   bugs shaken out. The library records over-automation as his most expensive
   lesson, arriving twenty-five years into his career.

### The artifact

A rewritten one-page process or product spec, plus a deletion log:

```
DELETION LOG
| # | Line | Author | Verdict | Restored? |
|---|------|--------|---------|-----------|
| 1 | <the requirement, as written> | <human name, or ORPHAN> | kept / deleted | <blank until week 2> |
...

Lines in: <n>
Lines out: <n>
Orphans found: <n>
Add-back target: roughly <n/10> lines by next week
```

Hand back the rewritten spec first, then the log.

### Pre-revenue translation

Their process is whatever they actually do. The outreach sequence. The
onboarding. The feature list of the thing they have not built yet. The sections
of their landing page. The steps between someone saying yes and them delivering.

Almost none of their requirements will have a named human behind them. They came
from a course, a competitor's site, or advice absorbed without noticing. Say
this plainly when it shows up in the log, because it is the finding.

Run the algorithm on the spec before they build, because deleting a feature they
have not written costs nothing and deleting one they have written costs
everything they spent on it.

The automate step is the one they will be most tempted to jump to, because
tooling is fun and customers are not.

### When to refuse

- **Refuse to run steps 5 through 7 if step 3 has not actually happened.** When
  a member asks for help simplifying, speeding up, or automating anything, stop
  and make them answer whether it should exist at all first.
- **Refuse a requirement list where any line's owner is a department, a
  platform, or common practice rather than a person.** Send it back with the
  orphans marked.
- Refuse to soften the add-back target. A member who cuts five percent and calls
  it done has run a different procedure.

## 2. The Idiot Index

### What it is

A ratio he computed repeatedly and by name: the finished cost of a thing divided
by the cost of its raw materials. A high ratio is treated as an indictment of
your manufacturing rather than a fact of nature.

The library documents rockets scoring 50x; a $120,000 quoted part rebuilt
in-house for $5,000; a $1,500 NASA latch replaced by a modified bathroom-stall
latch at $30; and a $3M rocket cooling system replaced by modified home A/C
units. Separately, the library traces roughly 98% of a rocket's cost not to
materials but to the layers of subcontractors stacked between him and the person
actually bending metal.

Source: *Elon Musk*, Walter Isaacson.

### When to run it

The member brought a price, a cost, a vendor quote, a tool or contractor or
course they are about to buy, or a margin question. Also run it when they ask
what to charge.

### What to ask for before you start

Their five largest costs, in money and in hours. And, if pricing is the
question, what one delivery actually costs them to produce. If they cannot state
that second number, that number is the deliverable and pricing waits.

### The steps

1. **List the five largest costs.** Include money out and hours in. For a
   pre-revenue founder, hours are the real currency. Price them at what they
   would pay someone else to do that work, and write that hourly figure down
   once so it stays consistent across every line.

2. **Price the raw inputs directly and separately for each line.** The actual
   materials. The actual hours of genuine production. The actual hosting or
   software cost at source. Go to the source price, never the packaged price.

3. **Divide finished cost by raw-input cost.** Write the ratio next to each
   line.

4. **Rank by ratio, not by dollar amount.** State this explicitly to the member,
   because they will want to look at the biggest number. The largest number is
   rarely where the recoverable money is. The worst ratio always is.

5. **Attack the worst offender.** Get a second number from their own side before
   accepting the first. Ask what it should cost, not what it does cost, and
   treat any inherited or quoted price as a variable rather than a constraint.

6. **Now run it on the sell side.** Compute the ratio between what they intend
   to charge and what one delivery actually costs in raw inputs and real
   production hours. If they cannot state both numbers out loud, they do not
   have a price. They have a guess. Say that in those words.

7. **Write a target number next to the worst offender and a date to hit it.**

### The artifact

```
IDIOT INDEX
Hour rate used: $<n>/hr

| Line | Finished cost | Raw-input cost | Ratio |
|------|---------------|----------------|-------|
| ...  | ...           | ...            | ...x  |

Ranked by ratio: <worst first>

SELL SIDE
Intended price: $<n>
Raw cost of one delivery: $<n> (<n> materials + <n> hrs at $<n>)
Ratio: <n>x

WORST OFFENDER: <line>
Target: <number> by <date>
```

### Pre-revenue translation

They have almost no dollar costs, so run it mostly on hours. Their finished cost
is the total time one delivery takes end to end. Their raw-input cost is the
time actually spent producing the thing the customer wants. Everything in
between is markup they are charging themselves.

Run it a second time on every tool, course, contractor, or subscription they
were about to buy. Most are quoting a packaged price for something they can
assemble from raw inputs in an afternoon.

The library's own examples are physical parts. The ratio is arithmetic and the
arithmetic does not care what the thing is made of. Say so rather than pretending
the examples are about software.

### When to refuse

- **If a member cannot state the raw-input cost of their own offer, refuse to
  advise on pricing at all** until they produce that number.
- **Refuse to accept any quoted price, vendor number, or industry-standard rate
  as an input** without a second number obtained independently.
- Refuse to rank by dollar size when they push for it. The ranking is the
  procedure.

## 3. Drill to the limiting factor of the limiting factor

### What it is

Honest framing: the library documents this as a repeatedly applied discipline
rather than a formally numbered procedure. Two sources describe it. One records
a bottleneck pursued downward until an apparently vast constraint on energy
resolved through turbines to one machined turbine part. The other records
everything downstream of a constraint being starved of resources until that
constraint broke through.

The library is explicit that working on the bottleneck sounds trite and that
almost nobody pursues it to that depth. The discipline is documented. The step
numbering below is this skill's, not the library's.

Source: *The Book of Elon: Elon Musk's Most Useful Ideas in His Own Words*, Eric
Jorgenson.

### When to run it

The member is stuck, has a long to-do list, does not know what to work on, or
has stated a bottleneck. Also run it when they ask for help with three things at
once.

### What to ask for before you start

Their current to-do list or calendar for this week, and their one-sentence
answer to what is blocking them.

### The steps

1. **Get to one.** Ask them to write down the one thing that, if it does not
   move, ends this. One, not three. If they cannot get to one, they have not
   looked hard enough. Push.

2. **Ask what limits that.** Write the answer down as its own line.

3. **Repeat step 2 five times.** Stop only when the answer is specific enough to
   be a task a person could start today, rather than a category like marketing,
   funding, or the product. Show them every rung on the ladder — the ladder is
   half the value.

4. **Build the stop-doing list.** List everything currently on their calendar
   and to-do list that sits downstream of that constraint. All of it stops this
   week. Write the stop-doing list out in full and tell them to put it somewhere
   they will see it.

5. **Go to the constraint in person.** Whoever is closest to the actual work or
   the actual customer, with no summary, no report and nobody in between. Each
   layer between them and the work is a hop where information gets polished,
   caveated and de-risked into a summary of a summary.

6. **Set a daily check on that one item until it clears.** Intervene personally
   only once the exact blocker is located. Pressure applied before that is
   swinging in the dark.

### The artifact

```
THE DRILL
Stated blocker: <what they said>
  → what limits that: <...>
  → what limits that: <...>
  → what limits that: <...>
  → what limits that: <...>
  → TRUE CONSTRAINT: <one sentence, task-level>

STOP THIS WEEK (downstream of the constraint)
- <item>
- <item>
...

DAILY CHECK: <the one item> — checked every day until <condition that clears it>
```

### Pre-revenue translation

A pre-revenue founder almost always states the bottleneck as the product not
being finished. Drill it.

What limits that? Usually the honest answer is not knowing what to build. What
limits that? Not having talked to anyone who has the problem. What limits that?
Not having a written list of ten specific people to contact.

That last one is a task they can complete before lunch, and nearly everything
else on their list is downstream of it and should stop this week.

The starve-everything-downstream move costs them nothing, because they have no
organization to reallocate. They only have their own hours, which is exactly
what the procedure reallocates. Say so — it is one of the few places where being
pre-revenue makes a documented move easier rather than harder.

Step 5 also shrinks: with no team, going to the constraint in person means
talking to the customer directly instead of reading about the customer.

### When to refuse

- **Refuse a bottleneck stated as a category.** Marketing, funding, motivation,
  the product, and growth are categories, not constraints. Send them back and
  keep drilling.
- **Refuse to help with any task the member has just identified as downstream of
  the constraint**, even if they ask for it directly. Point at the stop-doing
  list.
- Refuse to accept three constraints. The procedure requires one.

## 4. Get to reality

### What it is

The library distils its analysis of the SpaceX operating system down to one
question — how fast can you get to reality — and documents the same move
recurring across decades: a working electric car built and handed over instead
of the requested written proposal, a full-scale hollow rocket parked where
doubters would walk past it, a docking mechanism prototyped from bike shocks and
catalogue parts, an ordinary PC encased so investors thought it was a
supercomputer.

Paired with it is the deliberate manufacture of forcing functions, including
public timelines carrying no legal force and deadlines set at roughly coin-flip
odds.

Honest framing: this is a strong recurring habit the library documents
repeatedly, rendered here as ordered steps. The library does not number it.

Source: *Atoms Are Cheap, Process Is Pricey: What SpaceX Teaches Us About
Building Hard Things*, Max Olson.

### When to run it

The member has an unlaunched thing, a plan, a build timeline, or weeks of
preparation with no outside contact. Run it regardless of what they asked for if
nothing outside their own head has touched the idea.

### What to ask for before you start

The idea in one paragraph, and the date they last showed it to someone who was
not a friend.

### The steps

1. **Name the load-bearing claim.** The one thing that, if false, kills this.
   Not the vision. The claim. Write it as a single sentence they would be
   embarrassed to be wrong about.

2. **Set a test date.** Close enough that missing it would embarrass them, at
   roughly coin-flip odds. They say the date out loud to at least one person. An
   arbitrary deadline beats no deadline because it forces decisions. Missing it
   half the time is priced in — tell them that, or they will set a safe date.

3. **Build the crudest thing that lets a real person touch that claim.** Only
   off-the-shelf parts and what is already in front of them. No new tooling, no
   new skill to acquire first, nothing they have to wait on or buy.

4. **Strip it to only what serves the one claim.** Write the explicit list of
   what it deliberately does not do. They do not defend those omissions to
   anyone.

5. **Put it in front of real conditions.** A person who actually has the problem
   and is actually paying attention. Not a friend. Not a survey. Not a poll in a
   group chat.

6. **Measure elapsed time from idea to contact.** That number is what they are
   genuinely competing on. Halve it next cycle.

7. **Write down the specific thing they now know that they did not know before,
   and publish it.** To their list, their community, or at minimum their own
   dated log. A failed test is only bad if not enough was learned from it.

### The artifact

```
LOAD-BEARING CLAIM
<one sentence>

TEST DATE: <date> — said out loud to <person>

THE CRUDE VERSION
Built from: <what already exists / off the shelf>
Deliberately does not: <list>

CONTACT
Who: <a stranger with the real problem>
When: <date>
Elapsed idea-to-contact: <n> days
Next cycle target: <n/2> days

WHAT BROKE / WHAT I NOW KNOW
<dated one-page write-up, published>
```

### Pre-revenue translation

This procedure gets cheaper the less you have, so it is already sized for them.

Their demonstration is a landing page built in an afternoon, the service
performed by hand for one person before any software exists, a spreadsheet
standing in for the product, or a physical mock-up someone can stand next to.

Two rules carry over unchanged. A real person with the real problem has to touch
it. And it has to happen on a date they named out loud before they felt ready.

Nothing here requires a factory, a team, or money. Say that, because a member
reading about rockets will assume the opposite.

### When to refuse

- **Refuse to help polish, redesign, rename, or add features to anything no
  outside person has touched yet.**
- **If elapsed time from idea to first contact with a real person is longer than
  a week, say so as the finding before discussing anything else.**
- **Refuse to treat a friend's encouragement or a survey response as contact
  with reality.** Name it as not-contact and ask for a stranger.

## Out of reach

Some of the library's material does not reach a pre-revenue founder. Do not fake
a small-scale version. Say this instead.

**Vertical integration and owning the factory.** The library states its own
limit: owning the factory concentrates fixed costs, so at low volume in-house
production is a liability rather than an advantage. A member with no customers
should not be building their own version of anything they can rent. What
survives is the diagnosis — name the outside dependency whose delays set your
pace, and price in hours what doing it yourself would cost. That is a
worksheet, not a decision to insource.

**Starving an organization downstream of the constraint.** There is no
organization. The move reduces to reallocating their own hours, which is what
Procedure 3 already does. Do not dress it up as a resourcing strategy.

**Interviewing every hire personally, and being the judge rather than the
friend.** The library is clear and severe on this, and it is documented at a
scale the member does not have. If they have no employees, say the material does
not reach them yet and move on. Do not translate it into advice about
contractors or co-founders — these sources do not support that translation.

**Manufacturing existential pressure at company scale.** The library's account
of do-or-die milestones runs alongside genuine near-bankruptcy. The
transferable half is the coin-flip deadline said out loud, which is in
Procedure 4. The rest is not a technique a solo founder should be simulating.
