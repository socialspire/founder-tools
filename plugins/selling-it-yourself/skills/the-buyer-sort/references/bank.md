# Precedent Bank: The Buyer Sort

Forty-four cards, every one traced to a named file in
`socialspire/skool/insights/`. This replaces the seven-principle deck of v1.

**Why a bank and not a deck.** A deck of seven principles is knowledge the model
already holds, so it adds nothing a strong model would not reach alone. A bank
of forty-four specific, verified cases, most of them ones no model volunteers,
is information. Read the index, route to the sections the pipeline needs, and
read only those. Do not read the whole file on every run.

**Attribution rule.** Every card is paraphrase, including the ones marked
PODCAST. Not one phrase is quoted and none may be quoted downstream. Book
cards name the book and author. PODCAST cards name the founder, the show and
the date. Neither kind ever goes inside quotation marks, because the card text
is this bank's rendering, not the founder's words. If a member asks for a
quotable line, the bank holds none.

# A. Cannot say yes: the refusal is arithmetic

**A1. James Dyson and the bag makers.** Situation: a working bagless vacuum,
pitched for a licence to the established makers. Did: spent roughly a decade
pitching them and heard the same line throughout, that if a better vacuum were
possible Hoover would already have made it. Happened: no bag-trade incumbent
ever signed. A company earning about $500M a year selling bags cannot say yes to
a machine with no bag. Then vs now: the escape route was a foreign licence and
later his own factory; a service business has no Japan to sell into. The
diagnostic transfers exactly. Move: file any buyer whose existing revenue line is
the thing you replace in this column on day one, and stop counting their
meetings as pipeline.
Source: *Against the Odds: An Autobiography*, James Dyson.
`founders/2024-03-17-james-dyson-against-the-odds.md`

**A2. Edison and Western Union.** Situation: the phonograph, offered to the
telegraph monopoly. Did: pitched it. Happened: Western Union saw no use at all
in a machine that recorded and reproduced the human voice. The library sets this
beside Dyson and draws the rule: they cannot see what contradicts their own
economics. Then vs now: the refusal is produced by revenue, not by slowness, so
it does not respond to argument. Move: go direct to customers when your product
invalidates the revenue of the obvious acquirer.
Source: *Edison: A Biography*, Matthew Josephson.
`founders/2023-10-10-the-biography-of-thomas-edison.md`

**A3. Sam Walton at Ben Franklin.** Situation: a franchisee with a discounting
idea, pitching the franchisor's executives. Did: offered them the concept and
himself as guinea pig. It required cutting their 25 percent wholesale margin to
roughly 12. Happened: they blew up at him. The library pairs it with Dyson as
the identical mistake. Then vs now: this is the tightest numeric analogue in the
corpus to a deep cut in what a customer spends, and nobody tells it as an incentive story. Move:
before any pitch, write down what the counterparty loses if you succeed.
Source: *Sam Walton: The Inside Story of America's Richest Man*, Vance Trimble.
`founders/2024-07-11-the-inside-story-of-americas-richest-man-sam-walton.md`

**A4. Rose Blumkin and the wholesalers.** Situation: a furniture seller on a
permanent 10 percent over cost, dependent on wholesalers who also supplied the
full-margin stores she undercut. Did: the Chicago wholesalers shut her out when
she was poor. When competitors sued her over carpet at $3.95 against their
$7.95, she told the judge she sold at 10 percent over cost and asked what was
wrong with that. Happened: the case was thrown out and the judge bought carpet
from her the next day. Then vs now: the library records that the wholesalers
shut her out but not their stated reason; the structural reading is inferred. A
modern founder gets a quiet non-answer, not a lawsuit. Move: expect the parties
your price undercuts to close the door first, and treat a competitor's
complaint about your price as validation.
Source: *The Women of Berkshire Hathaway*, Karen Linder.
`founders/2024-03-20-rose-blumkin-warren-buffetts-favorite-founder.md`

**A5. Ingvar Kamprad and the trade boycott.** Situation: IKEA's own trade
association organised a boycott to cut off its suppliers and banned it from
showing prices at furniture fairs, because his prices threatened every dealer's
margin. Did: found new suppliers in Poland, made small design alterations so
blocked products became technically different items, paid suppliers within ten
days when competitors took three or four months. Happened: loyal suppliers ran
night deliveries and stripped logos off their vans to keep serving him; the
forced alterations produced a style competitors could not match. Then vs now:
the blocker sat on the supply side, not in the buyer's chair, so the fit is by
analogy. He never spent a day persuading the trade. Move: when an entire trade
says no for a structural reason, do not negotiate with the trade. Qualify a
supply and a channel outside its reach.
Source: *Leading By Design: The IKEA Story*, Ingvar Kamprad and Bertil Torekull.
`founders/2024-11-25-the-founder-of-ikea-ingvar-kamprad.md`

**A6. Sol Price and the price floors.** Situation: wanting to sell national
brands below the fair-trade floors those brands enforced to protect every other
retailer's margin. Did: asked the incumbent membership discounter for a joint
venture and was refused. When suppliers would not let him discount their
brands, built the FM private label. Happened: fedmart, then Price Club on a
roughly 12 percent markup against competitors' 36 to 50. He later called the
refusal luck. Then vs now: a solo founder cannot manufacture a private label;
the modern version of a price floor is a vendor's channel policy. Move: when a
supplier's no protects a third party's margin, stop negotiating for the
exception and route around the channel.
Source: *Sol Price: Retail Revolutionary and Social Innovator*, Robert Price.
`founders/2024-03-22-sol-price-the-founder-who-taught-jim-sinegal-sam-walton-jeff-bezos-and-bernie-ma.md`

**A7. Adam Foroughi and the large studios.** PODCAST. Situation: AppLovin
bought 14 to 15 game studios to train its model, which made it a direct
competitor to its own developer customers. Did: accepted as logical that the
larger studios who now saw a competitor would walk. His named mistake was not
pre-briefing the developer community in his own words; they found out on their
own and assumed malice. Happened: smaller and mid-sized developers signed anyway
because AppLovin's own games visibly growing on the model was proof; the large
ones came back once they understood it. Then vs now: he had a mid-market to
prove the product on. A founder whose whole market is the buyers who see a
competitor has no fallback. Move: decide in advance which buyers you will lose
because your product competes with a line of theirs, then tell them yourself
before they discover it.
Source: Adam Foroughi, on the David Senra podcast, 2026-05-03.
`david-senra/2026-05-03-bootstrapping-a-business-to-5-billion-in-free-cash-flow-applovins-adam-foroughi.md`

# B. The Gould test: the incumbent who can buy

The question that splits the impossible column. Does your product cut what the
buyer's customers spend, or raise what the buyer can produce from what it
already owns? The same company can refuse the first and buy the second.

**B1. Jay Gould and the quadruplex.** Situation: wanting the telegraph business,
facing Western Union, too big to attack and certain to refuse any rival line.
Did: instead of pitching a rival network, acquired Edison's quadruplex, which
multiplied the messages a single existing line could carry, built a company on
the patents, and sold that company into the monopoly. Happened: Western Union
bought it. The same Western Union that refused the phonograph. The library
gives no price for the sale. Then vs now: this is the only clean case in 194
episodes of an incumbent buying because the product raised the output of its
installed base. Move: find the version of your product that makes the
incumbent's own infrastructure more valuable to them, and pitch that one.
Source: *American Rascal: How Jay Gould Built Wall Street's Biggest Fortune*,
Greg Steinmetz.
`founders/2024-02-01-how-jay-gould-built-wall-streets-biggest-fortune.md`

**B2. John Malone and the empty pipe.** Situation: the operator controlling
access to millions of cable homes, facing startup channels that needed his
subscribers more than he needed any of them. Did: bought 20 percent of a startup
channel for $180,000 plus a loan, closing in about 45 minutes, because he could
put it into millions of homes instantly. Happened: trading distribution for
equity became the pattern by which the incumbent took ownership of everything
flowing through its pipe. Then vs now: his channels cost him nothing to carry,
which is exactly the quadruplex condition. A founder whose product sits in a
reseller's catalogue is in the same seat, and the yes will be priced in equity
or margin, not cash. Move: an incumbent whose pipe your product fills is a
buyer. An incumbent whose product your product replaces is not.
Source: *Cable Cowboy*, Mark Robichaux.
`founders/2023-10-17-the-biography-of-john-malone-cable-cowboy.md`

**B3. Tom Murphy buying the threat.** Situation: a broadcaster in an industry
where every peer saw cable as a threat to its business. Did: with Ted Turner,
treated the technology that threatened him as the thing to buy. Happened: two
broadcasters in an entire industry bought the threat. Then vs now: he was
acquiring, not buying a founder's tool, so this is a screen rather than a sales
story. The base rate is the useful part. Move: inside a category that cannot say
yes, screen for the one operator who has already bought a threat before. Past
behaviour toward threatening technology predicts the answer better than
enthusiasm in the meeting.
Source: *The Outsiders*, William Thorndike.
`founders/2024-04-15-tom-murphy-warren-buffetts-favorite-manager.md`

**B4. Andrew Carnegie and the furnaces.** Situation: an iron and steel maker
among older Pittsburgh rivals who balanced their books once a year and could
not tell a profitable year from a losing one until the books closed. Did: spent
on improved furnaces the older makers mocked as extravagant, and put chemists on
his blast furnaces years before rivals, who said they could not afford one.
Happened: in thin-margin years nearly all his profit came from the fuel and
material the furnaces saved, and it was years before anyone copied him. Then vs
now: Carnegie knew his unit cost to the cent, which is why he could see a
payback his peers could not. Most incumbents in a founder's pipeline are the
peers. Move: an incumbent moves toward column one when your product removes a
cost line rather than a revenue line. Lead with the payback arithmetic, or build
the cost model for them.
Source: *The Autobiography of Andrew Carnegie*, Andrew Carnegie.
`founders/2024-01-23-the-autobiography-of-andrew-carnegie.md`

**B5. Walt Disney and the third network.** Situation: banks refused to fund the
park, every television network wanted his programming, and his own industry saw
television as an infestation. Did: refused to sell the show alone and made
funding the park the price of getting it. The two dominant networks wanted the
show and would not touch a park, so he took it to the distant third. Happened:
the third network said yes because it needed him more, and paid roughly $50,000
to $70,000 an episode, which meant he was paid to advertise a park for eight
months before it existed. Then vs now: he held content every network was
desperate for; a founder rarely has an asset that scarce. The ranking rule
transfers. Move: when several incumbents sit in the pipeline, rank them by how
badly they need a win in your category rather than by brand, and expect the yes
from the weakest.
Source: *Disney's Land*, Richard Snow.
`founders/2024-05-14-how-walt-disney-built-his-greatest-creation-disneyland.md`

**B6. Leonardo Del Vecchio and Armani.** Situation: a frame maker moving into
fashion sunglasses, approaching houses that owned a name but could not
manufacture what he made. Did: licensed the Armani name onto his frames and gave
Armani 5 percent of the company plus a board seat, because the name was doing
the work. Happened: the Armani deal alone reached 10 percent of company revenue
and opened every other fashion house. Then vs now: Armani was not threatened by
eyewear, so this is the clean additive case, and it only works once the product
objectively meets spec. Move: an incumbent whose customers trust it but who
cannot make what you make belongs in column one. Pay it in equity when its name
is the asset.
Source: *Leonardo Del Vecchio*, Thomas Ebhardt, with *The Spectacle of Big
Lens*, Sam Knight.
`founders/2025-07-13-this-orphan-built-a-116-billion-empire-without-you-noticing.md`

**B7. Ettore Bugatti and the supercharger.** Situation: a dominant racing
constructor whose whole channel was winning on the track. Did: dismissed
superchargers as vulgar and kept losing races for years to cars that had them,
then adopted them. Happened: he dominated racing again. The biography's frame is
that a contrarian position hardened into dogma before he noticed. Then vs now: a
constructor gets an objective loss record every weekend. Most incumbents do
not, so the founder has to find the equivalent scoreboard. Move: an incumbent
moves toward column one when it is visibly losing to a peer that already
adopted what you sell, and stays in column three while it is still winning. The
trigger is the loss record, not your demo.
Source: *The Bugatti Story*, L'Ebe Bugatti.
`founders/2024-04-03-the-biography-of-bugatti.md`

# C. Already excited: who actually bought first

**C1. Jim Clark and Hollywood.** Situation: the geometry engine, pitched to
large industrial buyers. Did: pitched them. Happened: they dismissed it as a
useless toy. Hollywood understood it immediately, and George Lucas and Steven
Spielberg came asking to be the first customers. Then vs now: the logical buyer
and the excited buyer were different people, and the excited one arrived asking.
This is the only line in the corpus that states the principle outright. Move:
stop pitching the logical buyer who keeps saying no and go find the adjacent
one who is already excited.
Source: *The New New Thing*, Michael Lewis.
`founders/2023-11-16-the-biography-of-jim-clark-founder-of-silicon-graphics-netscape.md`

**C2. Tamara Mellon's first order.** Situation: modelling the business around
selling 20 pairs of shoes a week. Did: took the product to a major department
store buyer. Happened: the first order was 3,000 pairs. Then vs now: she had a
finished product at spec; the size of the surprise is the point, not the
number. Move: define the minimum weekly volume that makes the business viable
and build to that. An excited buyer reveals itself by ordering past your plan.
Source: *In My Shoes: A Memoir*, Tamara Mellon.
`founders/2025-06-08-how-tamara-mellon-built-jimmy-choo.md`

**C3. Brunello Cucinelli's first 53 sweaters.** Situation: no money, no
collateral. Did: ran the business entirely on timely payments. Happened: a first
order of 53 sweaters, a yarn supplier who let him pay after he got paid, and a
customer who paid upfront on the strength of belief in him. Then vs now: the
upfront payer is the excited-column signal in its purest form. Move: a buyer who
pays before delivery has told you which column they are in. Move one customer
to prepayment and treat that as the growth budget.
Source: *The Dream of Solomeo*, Brunello Cucinelli.
`founders/2024-02-13-the-biography-of-brunello-cucinelli.md`

**C4. Bob Noyce and NASA.** Situation: a new integrated circuit with no
reference customer. Did: NASA chose the circuits for the first spacecraft
computers. Happened: orders poured in afterward, and Fairchild went from a few
thousand dollars to $130 million in ten years. Then vs now: an agency with no
commercial stake chose on merit; most modern reference logos are bought. Move:
identify the one customer whose logo would end the argument for everyone else,
and go win them.
Source: *The Tinkerings of Robert Noyce*, Tom Wolfe, Esquire, 1983.
`founders/2024-07-18-how-the-sun-rose-on-silicon-valley-bob-noyce-founder-of-intel.md`

**C5. Del Vecchio and the buyers who argued precision.** Situation: a parts
maker whose biggest customer had become a partner and then a squeezer. Did:
aimed his quality obsession at the buyers he noticed argued about precision
rather than price. Happened: those were the accounts that held. Then vs now: the
sorting signal is what the buyer argues about, and it transfers at any size.
Move: sort buyers by what they argue about. The ones arguing precision are
already sold; the ones arguing price are not.
Source: *Leonardo Del Vecchio*, Thomas Ebhardt.
`founders/2025-07-13-this-orphan-built-a-116-billion-empire-without-you-noticing.md`

**C6. Daniel Ludwig and the incumbent that needed an outsider.** Situation:
effectively insolvent at 37, with no capital to buy the ships his business
needed. Did: went to an oil company for a long-term hauling charter first, then
took the signed charter to a bank as collateral and had the oil company pay the
bank directly. Standard Oil chartered from an outsider because antitrust
exposure meant a tightly controlled operation could not look like one. Happened:
when each charter expired he owned a paid-off ship without pledging his own
credit. He named this structure the chief reason for his wealth. Then vs now: a
small business's annual contract is rarely bankable paper; the modern equivalent
is a deposit that funds the work. Move: look for the incumbent that structurally
must buy from an outsider, the inverse of the buyer that structurally cannot,
and sign it before you build capacity.
Source: *The Invisible Billionaire: Daniel Ludwig*, Jerry Shields.
`founders/2024-02-23-the-invisible-billionaire-daniel-ludwig.md`

**C7. Foroughi and the ten-person studios.** PODCAST. Situation: building an
ad platform when the biggest budgets sat with brand advertisers and the
incumbent was built to serve brands. Did: gave the brand business about a week
of consideration and refused it, because selling to brands means arguing for a
slice of budget with hand-wavy proof, and the largest cut goes to whoever
entertains the client best. Built instead for ten-person studios that got no
support anywhere else, on a model where the customer can see they made more
than they put in. Happened: eight months from launch to a million dollars a
month of run rate, with no sales force. Large enterprises arrived on their own
once the platform was big enough. Then vs now: he had two prior exits when he
made the refusal. The mechanism is scale-independent. Move: a buyer whose
decision runs on relationship and entertainment cannot say yes to a product
whose whole pitch is measurable return. Sort them out.
Source: Adam Foroughi, on the David Senra podcast, 2026-05-03.
`david-senra/2026-05-03-bootstrapping-a-business-to-5-billion-in-free-cash-flow-applovins-adam-foroughi.md`

# D. Still explaining: the logical buyer who has not moved

**D1. Michael Ovitz's standing Thursday.** Situation: one gatekeeping literary
agent who would not deal with him. Did: called every Thursday at 10am for a full
year. Happened: received a single manuscript, which became a hit miniseries and
opened the floodgates. Then vs now: a year of weekly calls is a real cost and
the library gives no second case with a timeline. Move: for the one column-two
buyer worth the most, set a standing weekly contact time and hold it for a year.
For the rest, do not.
Source: *Who Is Michael Ovitz?*, Michael Ovitz.
`founders/2025-03-21-the-rise-and-fall-and-rise-of-the-most-powerful-man-in-hollywood-michael-ovitz.md`

**D2. Del Vecchio and the distributor's second answer.** Situation: the best
distributor in the eyewear trade refused him. Did: came back a year later as a
bigger company and asked again. Happened: the distributor said yes the second
time. Then vs now: the refusal was about size, not about his revenue, which is
the distinction this whole column rests on. Move: separate the no that is about
your size from the no that is about their revenue. The first gets re-approached
in a year with evidence of what changed, not a better explanation.
Source: *Leonardo Del Vecchio*, Thomas Ebhardt.
`founders/2025-07-13-this-orphan-built-a-116-billion-empire-without-you-noticing.md`

**D3. Duveen and the three-year courtship.** Situation: one prospect,
Alexander Smith Cochran, worth a business built around eight to ten men. Did:
courted him for three years without a pitch, never mentioning his own galleries,
showing him palaces and other collectors' walls until Cochran asked to buy.
Then said the paintings were reserved and sold him $5 million of other objects
instead. When Rockefeller Jr. called a million and a half too high, refused to move the
price and left the pieces in Rockefeller's house on a one-year option. Happened:
Rockefeller paid the full price the day before the option expired. Ninety-five
of the 115 paintings in Andrew Mellon's collection came through Duveen. Then vs
now: this only works when the buyer cannot go elsewhere and has effectively
unlimited money. Move: for a buyer still being explained to, stop pitching and
let them ask. For a hesitant one, let them lose the thing once instead of
chasing.
Source: *The Days of Duveen*, S.N. Behrman, New Yorker, 1951, with material from
*The 48 Laws of Power*, Robert Greene.
`founders/2024-04-26-the-robber-barons-art-dealer-joseph-duveen.md`

**D4. Dyson and the partner who never intended to produce.** Situation:
negotiating licences with several firms. Did: watched one set of lawyers argue
single clauses for months while a Japanese firm that understood the product
closed in three weeks. Another partner stalled him eight months with endless
handle redesigns and never intended to produce. Happened: the three-week firm
was the real buyer. Then vs now: the only place in the corpus that attaches a
timing number to which buyers can say yes. Move: slowness is the signal. Set a
decision deadline for any column-two buyer and move them to column three when
they miss it.
Source: *Against the Odds: An Autobiography*, James Dyson.
`founders/2024-03-17-james-dyson-against-the-odds.md`

# E. Around the no: reaching the customer another way

**E1. Dyson and the Ballbarrow.** Situation: a wheelbarrow with a ball instead
of a wheel, rejected outright by builders' merchants and garden retailers. Did:
ran a tiny newspaper drawing with a mail-order offer to the public. Happened:
members of the public mailed cheques to a company they had never heard of. The
postscript: a hired sales manager later talked the company out of direct selling
into wholesalers, margins halved, and the business went cash-negative. Then vs
now: the trade's refusal removed nobody's revenue, which makes this the control
case for what a non-structural no looks like. A newspaper drawing is now a
landing page. Move: before filing a refusal in column three, run the identical
offer past end users. If they buy and the gatekeepers will not, the no is habit
and belongs in column two.
Source: *Against the Odds: An Autobiography*, James Dyson.
`founders/2024-03-17-james-dyson-against-the-odds.md`

**E2. Walton's own store.** Situation: rejected by the Ben Franklin executives
at 43. Did: opened a 16,000 square foot store of his own. Happened: $700,000 in
year one, growing 30 percent a year for fifteen years. Then vs now: he had
fifteen years of retail behind him. The move transfers; the growth rate is not a
forecast. Move: set a date to launch without the partner who said no.
Source: *Sam Walton: The Inside Story of America's Richest Man*, Vance Trimble.
`founders/2024-07-11-the-inside-story-of-americas-richest-man-sam-walton.md`

**E3. Estée Lauder and Harrods.** Situation: rejected by Harrods. Did: got a
magazine to write that her cosmetics were coming soon to London, before she had
any London distribution, and made three trips with repeated press rounds.
Happened: customers were asking for her by name and the buyer moved her to a
prestigious counter. Then vs now: three trips and press rounds are affordable at
any size. Move: generate end-customer demand first and let the gatekeeper
discover it, rather than pitching the gatekeeper cold.
Source: *Estée: A Success Story*, Estée Lauder.
`founders/2024-09-04-estee-lauder.md`

**E4. Lauder and the humiliating order.** Situation: Harrods first offered a
tiny slot buried with general toiletries, not next to the good cosmetics. Did:
took it. Happened: used the sales to negotiate up. Then vs now: universal. Move:
accept the worst version of the distribution deal to get in the door, then earn
the better terms.
Source: *Estée: A Success Story*, Estée Lauder.
`founders/2024-09-04-estee-lauder.md`

**E5. Hans Wilsdorf over the retailers' heads.** Situation: a reseller between
manufacturer and shop, in an industry where retailers stamped their own names on
the watches and nobody advertised to the public. Did: advertised directly to the
public at more than £12,000 a year, sustained for years, leading every ad with
the Class A chronometer certificate won in July 1914. Put an Oyster on a
Channel swimmer in November 1927 and bought the entire front page of the Daily
Mail when it ran ten hours through her crossing. Fought for his name on the dial
one watch in six, then two, then three, then five. Happened: customers walked
into shops asking for a Rolex by name, which inverted the power relationship and
gave him control of wholesale pricing. Then vs now: a national front page has
no equivalent for a small business. The ratchet does. Move: spend on end-customer
demand specifically to change your negotiating position with the gatekeeper,
and take the win one in six rather than all at once.
Source: *Rolex Jubilee Vade Mecum*, Vol. 1, Hans Wilsdorf.
`founders/2024-07-08-the-founder-of-rolex-hans-wilsdorf.md`

**E6. Jimmy Iovine buying around radio.** PODCAST. Situation: radio would not
play Dr. Dre and Snoop Dogg, and radio was the channel that made a record sell.
Did: bought 60-second ad slots in the top 50 markets and ran the song itself,
with nothing said before or after. Happened: listeners called the stations
asking for the ad, and the record got the airplay programmers had refused. Then
vs now: this needs a gatekeeper that sells paid access to the audience it gates.
Most B2B buyers do not. Move: when the blocker sells access to the very
audience it gates, buy the slot and put the product in front of them, so the
demand shows up on the blocker's own phone lines.
Source: Jimmy Iovine, on the David Senra podcast, 2026-02-01.
`david-senra/2026-02-01-jimmy-iovine-building-interscope-records-beats-by-dre.md`

**E7. Master P and the trunk.** PODCAST. Situation: southern hip-hop had no
access to MTV or national radio. Did: built from New Orleans on a local
community, selling records out of the trunk and growing city by city. Happened:
density grew until MTV had no choice but to cover them, and the relationship
inverted. Stoute's sizing rule from the same conversation: two hundred thousand
people who love you is a business. Then vs now: records from a trunk had near
zero delivery cost. A service business builds density slower. Move: build
regional or niche density among end customers until the gatekeeper's own
audience demands you, and size the plan against the true-fan count rather than
the national deal.
Source: Steve Stoute, on the David Senra podcast, 2026-06-21.
`david-senra/2026-06-21-the-marketing-genius-of-steve-stoute.md`

**E8. Dana White's Trojan horse.** PODCAST. Situation: the product was barred
from pay-per-view entirely. Did: stopped pitching live broadcasts and built a
reality-format show with taped contests, which gatekeepers found acceptable
because it could be cut if needed. Targeted a network that had just rebranded as
a men's channel and had recently wasted money on content that failed. When it
passed, offered to fund the entire production and let them simply air it.
Happened: the network aired it, reduced to pure distribution with no downside,
and the company kept everything. His verdict: paying that bill was the greatest
thing that ever happened to them. Then vs now: funding a whole production is
beyond most founders; targeting the distributor that just repositioned is not.
Move: repackage into a format the distributor already buys, target the one that
just changed strategy and needs a win, and carry the cost in exchange for the
rights.
Source: Dana White, on the David Senra podcast, 2026-05-10.
`david-senra/2026-05-10-dana-white-the-man-behind-the-ufc.md`

**E9. Jimmy Buffett's own channel.** Situation: every label turned him down,
radio would not play him, and mafia-controlled jukeboxes never paid him for
50,000 plays. Did: sold live shows instead of records, returned to the same
cities every year, launched a physical newsletter in 1985 so he held his best
customers' names, sold his own merchandise, and opened Margaritaville as a
500-square-foot t-shirt shop. Happened: roughly 37 years passed between his two
real hits and the business grew more valuable nearly every year in between.
Then vs now: cheap rent and small profitable numbers bought him decades to
outlast every no. A founder on retainers can do that; a funded one cannot.
Move: when gatekeeper opinion and customer behaviour disagree, act on the
behaviour, and replace every dependency where a middleman can decline to pay
you with a channel you own, starting with the customer list.
Source: *Jimmy Buffett: A Good Life All the Way*, Ryan White.
`founders/2024-04-10-the-biography-of-jimmy-buffett.md`

# F. Who not to serve, and one deal too large

**F1. Sleep, Zakaria and Ogilvy refusing accounts.** Situation: rich clients
who cost more in energy than they paid. Did: Nick Sleep and Qais Zakaria turned
away irritating investors. Ogilvy resigned accounts over personal dislike, on
the reasoning that we pass this way only once. Happened: the library records the
refusals, not their cost. Then vs now: universal. Move: fire or decline one
customer this month who costs more in energy than they pay in revenue.
Source: *Richer, Wiser, Happier*, William Green, chapter 6.
`founders/2024-09-26-nick-zaks-excellent-adventure-how-nick-sleep-qais-zakaria-built-their-partnershi.md`

**F2. The Nobels and the Tsar.** Situation: the father's St Petersburg factory
ran on contracts from a single Tsar, and he had expanded payroll to serve them.
Did: when the Tsar died the promises evaporated and the payroll stayed.
Happened: the father went bankrupt, twice in his life, at 36 and 58. Ludwig
watched, then deliberately capped his own exposure to state contracts even while
a third of the national budget went to the military, and grew over 80 years
into 50,000 workers and 300 ships. The file's action line puts the ceiling at
one-third of revenue. Then vs now: a Tsar is the extreme case of a buyer whose
yes rests on one person staying in the chair. The modern analogue is the
internal champion who leaves. Move: cap the share of revenue any buyer whose yes
depends on one person's tenure can represent, and do not hire against that
account.
Source: *The Russian Rockefellers*, Robert Tolf.
`founders/2024-08-19-the-nobel-family-dynasty.md`

**F3. Steve Jobs at NeXT, the cautionary case.** Situation: over $180 million
raised before real customers, burning $39 million against a $3 million plan, a
factory producing eight machines a day against a 16,000-unit target. Did: walked
away from a government distribution deal because he did not want to sell to the
government. Separately refused to fly to Dallas over a slide problem, which read
as lack of commitment and killed a $30 million IBM deal. Happened: the hardware
business failed. The pivot to software came only when bankruptcy left no
alternative. Then vs now: the money made the refusal survivable and it still
nearly killed the company. The 39-to-3 ratio is the transferable part. Move:
list every deal refused on principle and confirm the runway funds the
principle. Show up personally for any relationship worth more than a quarter of
your revenue.
Source: *Steve Jobs in Exile*, Geoffrey Cain.
`founders/2026-06-03-steve-jobs-in-exile.md`

**F4. Duveen's ten accounts, the dissent.** Situation: a business deliberately
built around eight to ten men. Happened: a forty-year monopoly on a ten-account
book. Then vs now: this is the dissent on concentration, and it worked only
because Duveen controlled scarce supply and each buyer had effectively unlimited
money. A small business has neither. Move: deliberate concentration holds only
when the buyer cannot go elsewhere. Otherwise F2 applies.
Source: *The Days of Duveen*, S.N. Behrman, New Yorker, 1951.
`founders/2024-04-26-the-robber-barons-art-dealer-joseph-duveen.md`

**F5. Ovitz and the Thursday paycheque.** Situation: CAA starting with no
money, choosing between film, the prestige side, and television. Did: started
in television, the less glamorous side, because talent and therefore agents got
paid every Thursday. Happened: cash arrived weekly while the agency built.
Then vs now: universal. Move: reorder the pipeline by speed of cash collection,
not by size of logo.
Source: *Who Is Michael Ovitz?*, Michael Ovitz.
`founders/2025-03-21-the-rise-and-fall-and-rise-of-the-most-powerful-man-in-hollywood-michael-ovitz.md`

**F6. Ogilvy's five names and the progress reports.** Situation: the day after
opening his agency, with no record, no reputation and no plausible claim on
any account. Did: listed the five accounts he most wanted. Targeted advertisers
with no agency at all rather than trying to dislodge incumbents. Mailed
frequent, substantive progress reports to 600 people across every walk of
life. Happened: all five eventually became clients. He called the list an act
of mad presumption himself. A Seagram founder quoted back the closing
paragraphs of a 16-page speech Ogilvy had sent, and hired him. Then vs now:
universal, and the library's timing detail, the day after opening, is the
transferable discipline. Move: write five names before you have a claim on
any, filter for no incumbent, and send each something worth reading if they
never buy.
Source: *Confessions of an Advertising Man*, David Ogilvy.
`founders/2024-03-24-david-ogilvy-confessions-of-an-advertising-man.md`

# G. Instrumenting the sort

**G1. Ted Turner's postmark sort.** Situation: a weak Atlanta UHF station,
broadcasters treating cable operators as enemies, Nielsen refusing to measure a
cable audience, national advertisers refusing to buy one. Did: to prove the
audience Nielsen would not count, sorted incoming direct-response cheques by
postmark into Atlanta and non-Atlanta piles. When traditional advertisers would
not touch the station, sold airtime to small direct-response companies.
Happened: the outside-Atlanta pile dwarfed the local one, and direct-response
revenue carried the business through the gap. The three networks, which had
bureaus and anchors but no belief in cable, sat the medium out. Then vs now:
Turner is a name any model reaches for; the postmark sort and the small
advertisers are the obscure, cheap, copyable parts. Move: when the industry's
own metric will not count your buyers, build your own instrumentation from what
buyers actually send you, and split the threatened column between the parties
your product threatens and the parties it expands.
Source: *Call Me Ted*, Ted Turner.
`founders/2024-04-14-the-autobiography-of-ted-turner.md`

**G2. Brad Jacobs and the vendor under the sector.** Situation: consolidating
an industry where nearly every large player ran on software from a single
vendor, and where competitors set pricing by feel after conditions had changed.
Did: bought the vendor, getting a platform plus anonymised data on gluts and
shortages forming across the market. Happened: adjusted pricing ahead of
conditions while rivals reacted after the fact. United Rentals matched in 13
months the roughly $1 billion of annual rental revenue Hertz had taken 37 years
to build. Then vs now: he bought the vendor with acquisition capital. The
reachable version is building on the tool every prospect already uses. Move:
identify the vendor, dataset or platform every buyer in your pipeline already
runs on. One relationship there tells you which deals are worth hours before you
spend them.
Source: *How To Make A Few Billion Dollars*, Brad Jacobs.
`founders/2024-04-22-how-to-make-a-few-billion-dollars-by-brad-jacobs.md`

**G3. Gaston Glock and the institutional buyer.** Situation: a new pistol
entering a market where consumer taste followed professional choice. Did: sold
to the institutional buyer whose choices consumers imitate. Happened:
professional and agency buyers set the taste of the far larger, higher-priced
civilian market, and bought in volume, hundreds or thousands per order, with
contracts worth tens of millions. Then vs now: the file gives no timeline for
the lag between the institutional win and consumer demand. Move: identify the
professional segment your consumers copy and win it first, even at lower margin.
Source: *Glock: The Rise of America's Gun*, Paul Barrett.
`founders/2024-03-26-the-biography-of-the-founder-of-glock.md`

**G4. James J. Hill and the aligned rate.** Situation: a regional railroad
monopoly across largely empty land, where a line serving nobody earns nothing.
Did: used monopoly power to push freight rates down rather than up, on the
argument that if the farmer is not prosperous the railroad is poor. Mailed more
than 100,000 pamphlets advertising cheap settlement land and recruited farmers
from Europe by persuading whole villages and church congregations to relocate
along the line. Happened: the only American railroad founder who never went
bankrupt. Then vs now: his buyers were captive and a small business cannot
import villages. The alignment test survives. Move: change one price or
contract term so the buyer wins more when you win, and sell to the group your
next hundred customers already belong to instead of one at a time.
Source: *James J. Hill: Empire Builder of the Northwest*, Michael P. Malone.
`founders/2024-11-26-james-j-hill-the-empire-builder.md`

# H. What is not on record

Every one of these was searched for and not found. State the relevant one in
every run. It is a required output line, because a corpus that has been
searched can say what it does not contain, and nothing reasoning from memory
can.

**How to state it.** As an absence, never as a verdict. Holding no case of
a founder reselling through the refuser does not mean reselling fails.
It means nothing is on record either way, so the member should price the
attempt as untested.

**The words the member reads.** Open with "I have no documented case of",
name their own situation in plain words, then name the closest case and how
it differs. The member never reads the words library, bank, card, corpus or
record of this file, and never a flourish about being the first: an absence
is information, not a warning against the ruling. Never "the library says
no."

1. **No buyer whose revenue the product removed later said yes to the same
   founder on the same offer.** The nearest are Del Vecchio's distributor, whose
   no was about size, and Gould, who sold a different product. The corpus
   offers exactly two ways out of column three: Gould's, and time plus scale.
2. **One clean case, in 194 episodes, of an incumbent buying because the
   product raised the output of its installed base.** Gould. Malone is the
   nearest analogue. The Gould test rests on one precedent, and says so.
3. **No incumbent bought something that cut its own revenue and came out
   ahead.** Not one.
4. **Every incumbent-buys case underpaid the seller unless the seller held
   equity or an audited upfront fee.** Dyson's licensee reached about $12
   million a year in sales while he collected the $60,000 guaranteed minimum,
   because reported sales were unverifiable from abroad. Armani took 5 percent
   and a board seat. Malone took 20 percent.
5. **No case in a services, agency or software context** of a buyer refusing
   because the product removes billable hours, commissions or a service line.
   Every structural refusal here is physical goods, a media channel or a retail
   format. The one software case, Foroughi, is a competitor, not a removed line.
6. **No founder compensated the refuser** with a revenue share, reseller margin
   or channel programme to overcome the arithmetic, succeeding or failing.
7. **No conversion rate, win rate or pipeline benchmark** anywhere. No founder
   reports one.
8. **Almost no timing data on how long a threatened category takes to come
   round.** Dyson, about a decade. Turner, roughly two years. Del Vecchio and
   Ovitz, one year each. Nothing else.
9. **Nobody in the corpus made the sort fast.** Every founder here spent years
   before walking. There is no precedent for identifying the structural no early
   and leaving, which is this skill's actual job.
10. **No go-around that cost the founder something from the blocker
    afterward.** Only the ones that worked are recorded.
11. **No pipeline vocabulary at all.** Qualify, disqualify, prospect and
    pipeline return nothing on topic.
12. **One explicit statement of sell-to-who-already-grasps-it.** Clark. The
    principle is assembled from cases filed under other headings.
13. **No case of a buyer's own internal department blocking an outside
    provider to protect its headcount or budget.** Every gatekeeper refusal in
    the corpus is a trade, a channel, a franchisor or a platform, never a
    function inside the buyer. A services founder blocked by the client's own
    team is at the edge of what this bank can support, and the nearest cards
    are the gatekeeper ones in section E.
